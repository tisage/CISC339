At noon the hill of São Gens shone white enough to hurt the teeth.

Dr. Beatriz Montalvão stood with one boot on a limestone block and one in a trench, her hat tied under her chin with a ribbon she had bought in Évora twenty years ago and never replaced. Below her the Alentejo rolled away in a dusty quilt of cork oaks and stubble fields, the Guadiana a dark thread at the horizon. The village of Vale de Cevada lay at the base of the hill, its roofs flattened by heat, its church bell tower the only thing tall enough to cast a proper shadow.

The castle cast almost none. That was part of its mystery.

“Professor?” Tomás called from the square cut they had opened beside the inner wall. He held up a shard of green-glazed pottery as if it were a wounded bird. “Another one.”

Beatriz climbed out of her trench, knees clicking. She had turned forty-six in March and had begun, unwillingly, to notice stairs. “Context?”

“Unit three. Same as the horseshoe nail.”

“Then it is not another one. It is the one that belongs exactly where you found it.” She took the shard, turned it in the light. A curved line of manganese under the glaze, twelfth or thirteenth century, likely from Mértola. “Bag it.”

Tomás grinned. He was twenty-two, from Lisbon, and had not yet learned to fear certainty. He had come to São Gens because Beatriz had written the article everyone cited: The Northern Spur: Military Adaptation in the Late Islamic Fortifications of the Lower Guadiana. In that article she had described São Gens as a modest but strategic hisn, an Islamic hillfort converted after the Christian conquest into a frontier watch post. Its broken curtain wall, its dry cistern, its single tower and inner courtyard had all seemed to agree with her.

Her father would have agreed too, if he had lived to see the excavation.

António Montalvão had first surveyed the site in the 1970s, when the road from Beja still turned to gravel halfway and the locals grazed goats in the ruins. He had drawn the tower in blue ink, argued with the parish priest about access to the chapel register, and come home with his pockets full of pottery his daughter was not allowed to touch. He had called São Gens “a little clenched fist on the border.”

Beatriz had inherited the phrase and, without admitting it, the fist.

Now the municipality wanted to build an interpretive trail and a small visitor center. There was European funding, a mayor with ambitions, and an old story: how Martim Peres, knight of no particular historical importance, had climbed the hill one dawn in 1167 and taken the castle from the Moors by surprise. The village embroidered him on tea towels. A restaurant by the roundabout served “Martim’s Lamb” with too much rosemary.

Beatriz had accepted the commission because the site deserved careful work before it became a place for school buses and laminated panels. Also because her father’s field notebooks still smelled faintly of dust and cigarettes, and because his last map of São Gens had ended with a question mark beside the cistern.

At two o’clock, when the heat made the cicadas sound mechanical, Mayor Orlando Serra arrived in a white shirt too clean for the hill. He climbed with the resentment of a man used to driving to his destinations. Behind him came a photographer from the district paper and Dona Alzira, the president of the parish council, who wore black despite the weather and carried a basket covered with a cloth.

“Doutora Beatriz!” the mayor called, spreading his arms. “How goes the battle?”

Beatriz wiped her hands on her trousers. “We do not call it that.”

He laughed as if she had made a joke.

The photographer pointed his lens at Tomás in the trench. Tomás straightened and tried to look like archaeology rather than a boy in a dirty T-shirt.

“We brought pastéis,” Dona Alzira said, lifting the cloth.

This softened Beatriz. “Then you may call it whatever you like.”

They ate in the sliver of shade thrown by the surviving tower. The tower had always bothered her. Its lower stones were too fine, too carefully fitted, while the upper section was rougher, later, hurried. The arrow slits were narrow and oddly placed, some facing not outward toward the slope but inward toward the courtyard. António had suggested a rebuilding after siege damage. Beatriz had repeated the suggestion in print.

“Any proof yet of the assault?” Mayor Serra asked through a mouthful of pastry. “The foundation wants something people can imagine. Swords, fire, ladders. Children like ladders.”

“We have found evidence of occupation across several periods,” Beatriz said.

“But the conquest layer?”

“We have ash in the west trench.”

“Excellent.”

“A hearth ash,” she said. “Not a burning castle.”

The mayor frowned, briefly, then recovered. “Still early.”

It was. They had opened only four trenches. The site was stingy with spectacle. Pottery, animal bone, roof tile, iron fragments corroded past romance. No swords. No ladders. No Martim Peres.

After the visitors left, carrying staged photographs and half the pastéis, João Faria came up from the slope with the survey tablet tucked under his arm. João was short, bald, and patient. He had worked with Beatriz for fifteen years and treated landscapes as if they spoke a language everyone else shouted over.

“You should see this,” he said.

He led her to the north side, where the ground dropped in terraces toward a line of almond trees. A week earlier the geophysical survey had shown a dark anomaly there, a long rectangular void beneath the rubble outside the supposed curtain wall. Beatriz had assumed a collapsed storage room.

João handed her the tablet. “Ground-penetrating radar. The anomaly continues under the wall.”

“How far?”

“Far enough to be annoying.”

The image showed an underground corridor running not parallel to the defensive line, as a casemate might, but beneath it, descending toward the interior of the hill.

“Drain?” she asked.

“Too tall.”

“Later tunnel?”

“Possibly.” João’s tone made possibly mean no.

Beatriz looked at the wall. Its stones were massive at the base, drafted margins around some of them, a style she would have placed in the tenth century if they had been anywhere else. Above them later rubble rose like bad repair work. She knelt and pressed her palm to the lowest course. The stone was cool.

“Open a sondage tomorrow,” she said.

That evening, at the guesthouse in Vale de Cevada, she phoned her mother in Évora. The call crackled. Dona Leonor disliked mobile phones and held hers as if it might accuse her of something.

“Have you found your father’s battle?” she asked.

“No.”

“Good. He already had too many.”

Beatriz smiled despite herself. Her mother had never forgiven archaeology for taking António away every summer and bringing him home thin, sunburnt, and possessed by dead people.

“I found a tunnel, perhaps.”

“A tunnel is useful. You can escape.”

From the guesthouse window Beatriz could see the hill, black now against a lavender sky. The castle’s broken tower stood less like a fist than a finger pressed to lips.

“Do you remember going there with Pai?” she asked.

“Once. There were bees. He told me the cistern was dry because the Moors poisoned it.”

“He told me they hid treasure in it.”

“He improved his stories depending on the audience.”

After the call, Beatriz opened António’s oldest notebook. The pages had warped from damp decades ago. His handwriting was elegant when he described walls, impatient when he described people. Beside a sketch of the cistern he had written: Local shepherd says there is another water below. Superstition? Investigate later.

He had underlined later twice. Then, as often happened in archaeology and in life, later had become never.

The next morning they began at the north wall before the sun cleared the ridge. Manuel Candeias, who owned the almond trees below the site and knew every stone on the hill by grievance or affection, watched from a distance with his dog. He was eighty if he was a day and wore a straw hat repaired with wire.

“You’ll wake what’s sleeping,” he said.

Beatriz leaned on her shovel. “Is that a warning?”

“It’s a fact.” Manuel spat neatly into the dust. “My aunt said the hill has a throat.”

“What did it swallow?”

“Water. Names.” He shrugged. “Goats, if they were foolish.”

By late morning Tomás’s pick struck empty air.

Everyone stopped.

The sound was unmistakable, a hollow note beneath packed earth. They cleared with trowels and brushes until the outline emerged: a stone slab, not collapsed but set deliberately, sealed around the edges with lime mortar. A finger hole had been plugged with a pebble.

Beatriz felt the small electric thrill she still felt at every threshold. Not treasure. Never treasure. A sealed context was better than treasure. It was a sentence with punctuation intact.

“Photograph,” she said. “Draw before lifting. João, get the supports.”

Tomás bounced on his heels. “Do you think it’s a postern?”

“No,” Beatriz said, though she had not yet allowed herself another answer.

They lifted the slab at four, when the light had begun to lean westward. A breath rose from below, cold and mineral, carrying the smell of wet stone from a hill everyone had called dry. Beneath the slab, steps descended into darkness.

Marta Bellini, the conservator from Bologna, crossed herself in an economical northern Italian way. “Finally,” she said. “A place with air-conditioning.”

Beatriz smiled, but her hands were damp inside her gloves.

They did not enter immediately. They tested the air, secured the entrance, ran a camera down on a pole. The first images appeared on João’s tablet in jerks: steps; plastered walls; a channel cut along one side; mineral deposits like melted candle wax; then, on the wall, marks.

Not random scratches. Lines of writing.

“Arabic?” Tomás whispered.

“Some,” Beatriz said.

Marta leaned closer. “And that?”

The camera light slid over another cluster of letters, angular, crowded.

“Hebrew,” Beatriz said.

No one spoke for a moment.

By regulation they should have waited for the epigrapher, but the corridor was stable, the air clean, and Beatriz knew that night rain was forecast, sudden storms coming up from the south. She allowed herself, João, and Marta to descend after documentation, leaving Tomás above to suffer nobly.

The steps were narrow, worn in the center by feet. Not soldiers’ boots, Beatriz thought; feet, sandals, children perhaps. She pushed the thought away. The corridor opened after seven meters into a chamber cut into the limestone and lined with hydraulic plaster. A spring seeped from the back wall into a stone basin, overflow feeding a channel that disappeared under the floor. The water was clear. In its surface their headlamps trembled.

On the walls were inscriptions. Dozens of them. Some scratched lightly, some painted in red ocher, some carved with care. Arabic blessings. Hebrew names. Latin letters in a Romance hand. Crosses, yes, but also a seven-branched lamp, a small open palm, a boat with a square sail, a bird. Dates, perhaps, though some used systems Beatriz could not read at sight.

Marta’s whisper sounded loud. “This is no military tunnel.”

Beatriz moved closer to a larger inscription above the basin. The Arabic was formal, Kufic softened by local habit. She read slowly, not trusting herself.

“Water is God’s loan. Let no blade be drawn here.”

João exhaled. “That is specific.”

On the opposite wall, in Latin letters, was a phrase in early Portuguese or Galician-Portuguese, scratched by someone less trained:

A agoa non ten senhor.

The water has no lord.

Beatriz stood so still her knees began to ache.

For years she had walked over this chamber. She had described the wall above it as defensive, the tower as command, the cistern as storage against siege. Her father had seen the question mark and left it waiting. The hill had not been clenched around power. At its heart, hidden beneath the supposed fortification, water had been kept under words forbidding violence.

At the back of the chamber Marta found a ceramic jar sealed with wax-blackened resin. It sat in a niche above flood level, wrapped in the remains of linen that collapsed at a touch of air. Marta hissed like an angry cat until everyone stepped back.

“No one breathes,” she ordered, which was impossible but effective.

They carried the jar up in a padded crate at sunset. The storm arrived as they secured the entrance again, fat drops exploding in the dust. By the time they reached the vehicles, the hill was slick and shining. Water ran down the trenches, filled footprints, darkened the limestone blocks to the color of bone under skin.

That night the team crowded into the guesthouse dining room while Marta examined the jar under a portable lamp. Dona Alzira hovered with soup no one remembered to eat.

The jar was not treasure. Inside, once Marta softened the seal, were thin lead sheets rolled around a bronze pin, a wooden tally stick, and three scraps of parchment that had survived by the mercy of dryness and resin. Beatriz called Dr. Nadira El-Amin in Granada, who answered on the third ring and swore affectionately when Beatriz described the inscriptions.

“I’ll drive tomorrow,” Nadira said. “Don’t you dare translate anything important without me.”

“I already translated water.”

“Water always thinks it is important.”

They worked until nearly two in the morning. The lead sheets bore lists: names, measures of barley, oil, linen, medicines. The names moved between worlds without asking permission. Yusuf ibn Faraj. Dona Maior. Ishaq the dyer. Sancha, midwife. Mariam of the mill. Frater Pelagius. Ibrahim who keeps the goats. Some entries were in Arabic, some in Latin script, two in Hebrew. The dates, once converted, spanned at least forty years—before and after the supposed conquest.

Before and after Martim Peres.

The wooden tally had notches grouped by months. The parchment, too fragile to unroll fully, showed one line in a practiced hand:

Those who come with fever shall leave their arms at the lower gate.

“A hospital?” João asked.

“Or hospice. Refuge. Way station.” Beatriz rubbed her eyes. “A protected spring.”

“In a castle,” Tomás said.

Beatriz looked at him. He flushed.

“Under what became a castle,” she said. “Or under what we called one.”

Rain beat against the shutters. In the corner, Manuel Candeias sat with a glass of aguardente he had not been offered and had somehow acquired.

“My aunt said names,” he said.

Beatriz turned. “What else did she say?”

“That São Gens was not a saint first. It was something older. People came when the low wells spoiled.” He shrugged. “Old women say many things.”

“What word did she use?”

Manuel frowned into his glass. “Casa da Água. House of Water. And Casa das Mãos, maybe. Hands because you came empty.” He held up both palms. “No knife.”

Beatriz wrote it down.

By morning the mayor knew something had happened. Archaeological news traveled through villages faster than scandal because it usually was scandal, only older. He arrived with mud on his shoes and alarm in his smile.

“I hear you found inscriptions,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Excellent. Moorish inscriptions in the conquered tower?”

Beatriz had slept two hours. Her patience was thin. “Not only Arabic. Hebrew and Portuguese too. The chamber appears to have been used across the political transition.”

Mayor Serra blinked. “Political transition?”

“The conquest.”

“Ah.” He looked toward the tower, where rainwater still dripped from the stones. “But there was a conquest.”

“Certainly. In the region.”

“At São Gens?”

“We have no evidence of assault here.”

“We have the chronicle.”

“The chronicle names a castle near the river. There are three candidates. My father favored this one.”

“And you?”

She looked at the north wall, at its careful lower stones sheltering the stair. “I am less obedient than I was.”

The mayor’s smile vanished. “Doutora, the funding proposal is approved for Castelo de São Gens. The trail, the panels, the annual reenactment—”

“Perhaps reenact people leaving their swords at the gate.”

He did not laugh.

For the next week the site changed under their hands.

Once they stopped forcing it to be a fort, features rearranged themselves with embarrassing speed. The “arrow slits” in the tower aligned not with fields of fire but with air channels ventilating the chamber below. The dry cistern in the courtyard was a later collection tank connected to the spring by ceramic pipes. The “barracks” yielded loom weights, spindle whorls, a copper needle, mortars stained with plant residues, and hundreds of tiny fish bones from species found only downstream in the river—food carried up, not provisions stored for siege. The ash layer in the west trench contained burned medicinal seeds: fennel, coriander, rue.

Nadira arrived in a red car dusted pale by the road and entered the chamber with tears in her eyes, though she denied them afterward. She was Sevillian by birth, with a Moroccan grandfather and a laugh that filled rooms before she did. She spent three days lying on foam mats, reading walls by headlamp.

“This one,” she said, pointing to a line of Arabic near the basin, “records repairs in the year 548. That is 1153 or 1154.”

“Before the Portuguese took the area,” Beatriz said.

“Yes. And this one, in Latin letters, says Lourenço, son of Mendo, gave two goats for the roof. No date, but the hand is later. After, perhaps.”

She shuffled sideways. “Here. Hebrew. ‘May Raquel daughter of Natan be remembered; she washed the dead in the drought year.’”

The chamber seemed to listen.

One afternoon, in the lowest room beside the spring, Tomás found human remains.

He called Beatriz with a voice so changed she ran.

It was not a mass grave. In a narrow side niche, behind a collapsed partition, lay three skeletons carefully arranged: an adult, a child of perhaps six, and an infant. No weapons, no obvious wounds. The adult’s arm curved around the child. At their feet was a small glazed bowl, a bronze ring, and a flat river stone incised with an open hand.

Tomás stood pale against the wall. “Are they plague victims?”

“Maybe.” Beatriz crouched but did not touch. “Maybe family. Maybe travelers who died here and could not be taken home.”

He swallowed. “I wanted a sword.”

“I know.”

“I don’t now.”

She put a hand briefly on his shoulder. “Good. But document as if you still do.”

The mayor grew more anxious as the finds became more definite. The district paper published a photograph of Beatriz emerging from the stair and the headline: CASTLE HIDES MOORISH SANCTUARY. By evening someone had sprayed over the sign at the base of the hill: ALWAYS OURS. By morning someone else had added beneath it in smaller letters: WHO IS OURS?

Beatriz found herself angrier at the grammar than the sentiment.

At the municipal meeting called to “clarify the narrative,” the hall filled past capacity. Farmers, teachers, reenactment enthusiasts, teenagers with phones, the owner of Martim’s Lamb, three men from a heritage association wearing polo shirts embroidered with little shields. Beatriz sat at a table beside Mayor Serra, Nadira, and a projector that refused to recognize João’s laptop until threatened with replacement.

The mayor introduced her with the tone of a man presenting a weather report he hoped might change.

Beatriz stood.

She had given hundreds of talks: conferences in dim hotel rooms, university lectures, donor briefings where people nodded most vigorously before lunch. This was different. Her mother had taken the bus from Évora and sat in the second row, handbag on knees. Manuel leaned against the back wall. Dona Alzira guarded the door as if history might try to escape.

Beatriz showed the hill from above. The old plan. Her father’s plan. Her own first interpretation. Then the radar image, the sealed stair, the chamber.

She did not say, We were wrong, not at first. She let the stones do it.

She showed the spring basin and the inscription above it. Nadira read the Arabic aloud, the sounds rounded and steady. Beatriz gave the Portuguese. Water is God’s loan. Let no blade be drawn here.

Murmurs moved through the hall.

She showed the lead sheets, the names. Yusuf. Sancha. Ishaq. Pelagius. Mariam. She showed the air vents, the pipes, the medicinal seeds. She showed the phrase scratched by a hand eight centuries dead: The water has no lord.

Mayor Serra shifted beside her.

A man in the third row stood. He had one of the embroidered shields. “Are you saying the conquest did not happen?”

“No,” Beatriz said. “I am saying this site does not show what we thought it showed.”

“My grandfather said Martim Peres took the castle.”

“So did my father.”

“Then your father was wrong?”

There it was, placed on the table like a blade.

Beatriz looked at her mother. Dona Leonor’s face was unreadable, but one eyebrow lifted slightly, as if to say, Well?

“My father wrote down a question,” Beatriz said. “He did not live long enough to follow it. I did.”

The hall quieted.

The owner of Martim’s Lamb raised a hand. “Should I change the menu?”

Laughter broke the tension, grateful and uneven.

Dona Alzira spoke from the door. “Call it House of Water Lamb and add more garlic.”

Even Mayor Serra laughed then, though softly.

After the meeting, people crowded around the projected images. Manuel tapped the photograph of the open hand stone.

“My aunt had one like that,” he said. “Kept it in the flour jar.”

“Where is it now?” Beatriz asked.

“Buried with her, if she had any sense.”

Her mother waited until the hall emptied. Outside, the evening smelled of dust and cooling stone. Children chased each other around the statue of an infantryman from a much later war.

“You did not shame him,” Dona Leonor said.

Beatriz leaned against the wall. She was suddenly exhausted. “I thought I might.”

“Your father loved questions when they obeyed him. He needed practice with the others.” She touched Beatriz’s cheek. “You gave him some.”

The official change took months, then years, because paperwork moved more slowly than groundwater. The visitor center was delayed, redesigned, argued over. The reenactment committee split, reformed, and eventually staged a procession in which children carried empty bowls up the hill and left wooden swords at the lower gate. The restaurant kept Martim’s Lamb but added Casa da Água stew, which was better.

The site report, when published, was cautious in the way of proper archaeology. It did not claim harmony, paradise, or miracle. It described phases of construction, water management, epigraphic evidence, mixed-use occupation, refuge practices, later militarization, and the absence so far of a destruction layer corresponding to the traditional assault. It used words like suggests and indicates and complicates. Beatriz insisted on complicates.

In late autumn, after the first rains had greened the slopes, she returned alone to São Gens. The excavation was covered for the season. Sandbags lined the trenches; geotextile lay over the floors; the entrance to the chamber had a locked gate installed below the stone slab so the hill still looked, from a distance, as it always had.

She carried António’s notebook in her backpack.

The village below smoked blue from chimneys. Somewhere a dog barked with bureaucratic persistence. Beatriz unlocked the gate and descended into the cool.

The spring whispered in its basin.

The inscriptions had been stabilized but not cleaned beyond recognition. She liked them with earth still in their cuts. Nadira had translated more since summer. One small line near the floor, probably written by a child or someone unused to letters, read only: I came thirsty.

Beatriz sat on the lowest step and opened her father’s notebook to the sketch of the cistern. Local shepherd says there is another water below. Superstition? Investigate later.

She took a pencil from her pocket. For a long time she did nothing. Then, beneath his question, in the margin he had left, she wrote:

Yes.

The graphite looked too new, almost rude. She closed the notebook.

At the basin she dipped her fingers. The water was cold enough to make her inhale. She thought of the adult skeleton’s arm around the child, of Sancha the midwife, of Raquel who washed the dead, of Lourenço’s two goats for the roof. She thought of Martim Peres, who might have climbed some other hill or none at all, and of all the names that had outlasted him not because they conquered, but because someone had scratched them near water.

Above ground, the ruined tower caught the last sun. Its inward-facing slits glowed briefly, channels of light instead of weapons, and then the hill entered shadow whole.
