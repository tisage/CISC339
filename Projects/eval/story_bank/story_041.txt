At three in the morning the padlock rattled, and Faustin Jn Baptiste was awake before he knew he was awake. He lay on the foam mat across the threshold of the front room and listened to his mother's hands working at the chain.

"Mama," he said. "Mama, it is night."

"Mwen ka chèché Augustin." I am looking for Augustin.

"Papa not out there."

"The boat come in. I hear the engine."

What she heard was the Atlantic. On the windward side of St. Lucia the sea never really stopped. It came into Dennery Bay the way it had all his life, booming against the rocks below the church and running loud up the black sand. When he was a boy it had been the sound of sleep. Now it was the sound of his mother getting up.

He sat up. His back made the small cracking noise it had been making since February, since the night she went down in the bathroom and he had to lift her off the wet tiles with his arms locked under hers, her whole weight hanging off his spine. She was standing over him now in the yellow nightgown his sister had mailed from Croydon. The streetlight came through the louvres and laid stripes across her. She had pulled the chain as far as it would go, so the door stood open a crack, the width of a hand, and the salt air was coming in through it.

"Come, Mama. Come back to bed."

"You is who?"

"Faustin."

She looked at him with the same wary politeness she gave the men from the electricity company. "Faustin is a little boy," she said.

"I grow up."

"Hm." She did not believe him. She turned back to the door and gave the chain another shake, as if it might change its mind.

He got up and took her elbows from behind, gently, the way the nurse had shown him, and walked her backward. She went three steps and then planted her feet. She was eighty-four, she weighed maybe ninety pounds, and when she planted her feet it was like trying to move a fence post.

"Lagé mwen," she said. Let me go.

"Mama."

"Lagé mwen, vòlè." Let me go, thief.

He let her go. She stood there swaying. After a while the urgency drained out of her face, the way it did, all at once. She let him lead her down the passage to the back room, where the bed smelled of the Dettol he used on the plastic sheet. He covered her up to the chin. She took hold of his wrist and held it, and he sat on the edge of the mattress with his arm bent at an angle that hurt. He waited until her breathing went long. Then he waited a little more, because sometimes it was a trick, not a deliberate one, just her body coming up out of sleep for no reason.

Outside, the sea went on. He went back to the mat by the door and did not sleep.

---

The padlock had cost forty-two dollars at the hardware in Castries. He had stood in the aisle holding it in its plastic packet for a long time, the way he imagined a man might stand holding a gun. Then he paid for it. The shop girl wished him a good day and he wished her one back.

That was in March, after the night Mr. Ferdinand found his mother at the bus stand in her slip, asking the drivers whether any of them were going to Micoud. Mr. Ferdinand brought her home in his van and did not say anything, but he patted Faustin twice on the shoulder. Faustin felt each pat land like a stone.

Before that there had been the Gramoxone. She found the bottle under the sink, behind the bucket, where it had been since his father's time, when they still had the little garden plot up the hill. Faustin came in from the yard and saw her standing at the kitchen counter, unscrewing the cap. Whether she meant to drink it, cook with it, or only smell it, he never knew. He had shouted so loud that she dropped the bottle and the stuff ran across the floor. They both stood there watching it spread, and she started to cry like a child who had been beaten.

Before that, the stove. Before that, she gave his phone to the boys who came selling plantain chips. Before that, the forgetting of small things, which at first had been almost funny. His mother had been a fierce woman. She kept the accounts for the fishermen's cooperative for twenty-six years and never lost a cent. Now she put the sugar in the fridge and the butter in the cupboard, and called the cat by the name of a dog dead since 1981.

Before all that, Faustin had a boat.

It was still there, pulled up on the sand above the tide line at the north end of the bay. A twenty-four-foot fibreglass pirogue, white and blue, with *BON MÈT* painted on the bow in letters his father had first painted on a different boat. The engine, a forty-horse Yamaha, was in the shed behind the house under a tarpaulin. He started it once a month so the carburettor wouldn't gum, and he let it run in a drum of water, and he stood watching it. For two years he had been telling Lenus Joseph that he would be back out soon.

---

In the morning he made her porridge, cornmeal with nutmeg and condensed milk, the way she used to make it for him. She ate four spoons and then pushed the bowl away and said it was too hot, though it had gone cold. He gave her the white tablet for her pressure and the half of a yellow one for her nerves. The doctor at Dennery Hospital had been very clear about the yellow ones. Half in the morning. One at night. Never more than that, Mr. Jn Baptiste, at her age, with her heart. She will fall. She will stop breathing. You understand?

He understood.

After breakfast came the bath, and the bath was the worst of it, worse than the nights. She did not want to be undressed. She had never in her life let anyone see her undressed. She had borne three children in the hospital at Victoria with a sheet tented over her knees and a nun standing guard. Now her son peeled the nightgown off her over her head while she held her arms down stiff at her sides and called him a nasty man, a *malpwòp*, and said she would tell his father. She would tell Father Lambert. She would tell the police.

He sat her on the plastic chair in the shower stall and ran the water warm. He soaped the washcloth and did her back first, because her back she minded least. Her spine stood out like a row of knuckles. There was a mole below her left shoulder blade that he remembered from when he was small and she used to bathe in the river. He could not have said why he remembered it. Then her arms. Then, quickly, with his eyes on the wall tile, everything else.

Halfway through she caught his forearm in her teeth.

She did not have many teeth left, but she had enough. He yanked his arm back, and there was a white crescent on the skin with a dot of blood welling in it. Before he knew what he was doing he had her by the wrist, hard, harder than he had ever held anything that could break. Her face went small and old and frightened, and that was what stopped him. He let go. He stepped back until his shoulders touched the wet wall.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry, Mama. Sorry."

She looked at her wrist. Then she looked at him with her chin trembling and said, "Gertrude never do me so."

---

Nurse Augier came on Tuesdays. She was a broad, unhurried woman from Babonneau who had been the district nurse for this part of the coast for eleven years. She called his mother Ma Ida and his mother called her *chè*, and the two of them got on beautifully. With the nurse his mother turned into the woman she must have been at the cooperative: brisk, gracious, full of questions. *And your mother, she keeping well? And the little one, she pass her exams?* Faustin sat in the kitchen doorway and listened. He felt like a man watching his wife flirt.

The nurse took the blood pressure and checked his mother's heels for sores. Then she turned the thin arm over and saw the bruise. It had come up overnight, purple and green, in four distinct marks the shape of fingers.

"Ma Ida," she said lightly, "what happen to your hand?"

"I don't know, chè. I am an old lady. I bump everything."

The nurse looked across the room at Faustin. He made himself look back at her.

"She bit me," he said, and held up his forearm, and then wished he hadn't, because it sounded like an excuse. It was an excuse.

Nurse Augier finished what she was doing. She packed her bag. At the door, while his mother sat on the gallery humming a hymn, the nurse said quietly, "Mr. Jn Baptiste. When last you sleep?"

"I sleep."

"A full night."

He thought about it, and found he could not answer.

"There is a woman in La Ressource," the nurse said. "Miss Pelagie. She does sit with old people. Not cheap, but not bad. There is also the Marian Home in Castries. They have a list. I can put her name on the list."

"My sister say no home."

"Your sister is in England."

"Yes."

The nurse put her hand flat on his chest for a moment, right over the sternum. It was a strange thing to do, and he never forgot it. Then she went down the steps to her car.

---

On Saturday Lenus Joseph came up the hill with a bag of oranges and sat on the gallery wall with his feet hanging.

Lenus was fifty-four, two years older than Faustin. They had been in Standard Three together. They had gone out on their fathers' boats together at eleven, twelve, throwing up over the gunwales together. The sea had made them both into men with the same forearms, the same squint, the same habit of looking at the sky before they said good morning. Lenus had a big new boat now, thirty-two feet, with a cabin and a GPS. His sons ran it most days.

"Dowad running," Lenus said. Dorado. "Big. Out by the FAD, twelve, fifteen mile. Kenroy pull in four hundred pounds Wednesday."

"I hear."

"I going Thursday night. Leave two o'clock, back by ten, eleven. Kenroy have to go Vieux Fort for the baby christening." Lenus peeled an orange with his thumb in one long curl. "I need a man."

"You have plenty man."

"I need a man who know what he doing."

Faustin looked out at the bay. Saturday afternoon, and the fish fry was setting up in the road by the jetty, women hauling coal pots, a boy stringing lights between the almond trees. In a few hours there would be music so loud the louvres would hum. His mother would wake up and think it was Carnival in 1965 and try to go and find the Dennery band.

"I can't leave her."

"One night."

"I can't leave her one night."

"Your sister not coming?"

"Christmas."

"Christmas," Lenus said, and ate a piece of orange, and said nothing more. He was too decent. He had said Christmas the same way last year.

When Lenus left, Faustin went down to the north end of the bay and stood by the boat. He put his palm on the hull. The fibreglass was warm from the day. There was sand drifted against the transom and a crab hole under the bow, and someone, probably a child, had drawn a heart in the dust on the gunwale with a finger. He thought about his father. Augustin Jn Baptiste, who in June of 1979 went out past the reef in a sea that every other man in Dennery had looked at and left alone. He had not come back. His boat came back four days later, upside down, on the beach at Fond d'Or. Faustin was nine. He remembered his mother walking down to the water every night for a year. She would stand at the edge with her arms folded and look east, as if something might still be on its way.

---

Gertrude called on Sundays at four, which was eight in Croydon. She had married a man from Barbados named Clyde and worked in a care home in Purley, which Faustin understood to be the joke God was telling. She was the eldest. She had left in 1988 on a nursing scholarship and never come back except for Christmases, and not every one of those. The middle one, Rosemund, was in Toronto. Rosemund sent money and did not call because, she said, it was too hard to hear Mama like that. Faustin held this sentence somewhere under his ribs and turned it over at night like a stone in his pocket.

He put the phone on speaker and held it near his mother's face.

"Mama? Mama, is Gertrude. You hear me?"

And his mother lit up. There was no other word for it. She straightened in the chair and her mouth opened and her eyes came clear and wet. "Gertrude! Oh, my darling. My good girl. When you coming home?"

"Soon, Mama. Christmas."

"You eating? You looking after yourself in that cold?"

"I fine, Mama. And you? Faustin treating you good?"

His mother's eyes slid over to him, and slid away.

"He try," she said. "He is not like you."

When it was done and his mother had drifted back down into whatever country she lived in now, Faustin took the phone off speaker and went out onto the gallery.

"Fos," Gertrude said, "you have to be patient with her."

He watched a frigate bird hang over the bay without moving its wings.

"She say you hurting her. She tell me you squeeze her hand."

"She bite me."

"She don't know what she doing, Fos. She's not well. You have to be patient."

"Gertie," he said. "The nurse say the home in Castries have a list."

There was a silence on the line with the hiss of the ocean in it, and he could not have said which ocean.

"You want to put Mama in a home," Gertrude said.

"I want to sleep."

"She would die in there, Faustin. You know how they does treat them in those places. I work in one. I *see*. She would die of shame. Mama, who was so proud, who keep the books for the whole—" Her voice cracked and recovered. "I will come for Christmas. I will take two weeks. You will get a rest."

"Two weeks."

"Clyde's mother sick too, you know. It's not easy here either."

"I know."

"I will send something extra this month. Get somebody to help you."

"Yes."

"Kiss her for me."

"Yes."

He hung up and stood with the phone in his hand and the urge to throw it went right down his arm to the elbow. Then it went away. He went inside and kissed his mother on the top of her head, for Gertrude. His mother said, "Who that?" and he said, "Nobody, Mama," and she said, "Hm."

---

Wednesday evening he left her sitting on the gallery in her chair with the cushion, the radio playing the Kwéyòl gospel hour, and went in to bathe himself. It took six minutes. He knew because afterward he went over and over it, the way his father's friends must have gone over the sky of June 1979. Six minutes. Cold water, a bar of blue soap. He hadn't latched the gallery gate because he never latched it in the daytime, because she never got up in the daytime, because the yellow tablet made her heavy until dark.

When he came out toweling his head, the chair was empty.

He did not shout. He went down the steps barefoot and through the gate and down the hill. He passed the Ferdinands' yard and the shop and the parked minibuses and came out onto the sand. He was running before he knew it, his heart going in his ears so loud it drowned the sea. And there she was.

She was walking into the water in her housedress. She was already past her knees. A wave came in and broke against her hips and she swayed but stayed up. Her arms were out a little from her sides for balance, and she was looking east, to where the sky had gone violet over the dark line of the sea.

"Mama!"

She did not turn. He went in after her. The water was colder than it looked and the bottom fell away faster than he remembered, and the next wave hit him in the chest. He got hold of her from behind, an arm around her waist. She fought him, God, she fought, twisting and kicking, her elbow cracking against his cheekbone. She was screaming his father's name, not calling it, *screaming* it, *Augustin, Augustin*. He dragged her backward through the surf with his heels digging trenches in the sand. When they were clear of the water she was still fighting. He threw her down. He did not lower her. He threw her down on the hard wet sand, and he heard the breath go out of her.

He stood over her with water running off him and screamed into her face, "He's DEAD! You hear me? He's dead! He's been dead forty years! He's never coming back, you stupid, *stupid*—"

Then he saw the people.

They were at the fish fry tables up by the road, maybe a dozen of them. Mr. Ferdinand, the two Charles girls who sold accra, a man he didn't know holding a beer with the bottle stopped halfway to his mouth. Nobody moved. The sea came up behind him and washed around his ankles and went back down.

His mother lay on the sand with her housedress plastered to her and her mouth open. She was not crying. She was looking up at him with no recognition at all. She looked the way you might look at weather.

He picked her up. She weighed nothing. He carried her up the beach, past the tables, past the people, and nobody said anything. He carried her up the hill and into the house and got her out of the wet dress. She let him do it now. She was shivering and docile and silent, and that was worse than the biting. He dried her and put her in a clean nightgown and put her in bed, and he sat in the kitchen with the light off for a long time.

Then he got up and went to the shed and pulled the tarpaulin off the Yamaha.

---

He called Lenus at nine. "Thursday," he said. "What time?"

Lenus was quiet for half a second, which meant he had heard about the beach already. Everybody would have heard about the beach. "Two," he said. "I go pull your boat down with mine. We go take both. Bring your fuel."

"Yes."

"Fos."

"What."

"Nothing. Two o'clock."

---

Thursday he moved through the day as though everything had been decided by someone else. He gave her the porridge, the white tablet, the half of the yellow. He bathed her and she did not bite. He changed the sheets. He cooked her rice and red beans and a little stewed chicken and fed her with a spoon. He put the radio on for her and sat with her through the afternoon while she slept in the chair. Once she woke and said, quite clearly, "The roof need paint," and he said yes, it did.

At eight o'clock he gave her the yellow tablet. Then he stood at the kitchen counter with the bottle open in his hand and shook a second one out onto his palm.

He looked at it for a long time. It was very small. The doctor's voice was very clear in his head. *She will fall. She will stop breathing.* He thought about the size of the house, how far it was from the bed to the door. He thought about the chain, the padlock, the window louvres too narrow for even a child. He thought about four hundred pounds of dorado, and what four hundred pounds of dorado would mean against the electricity bill and the pharmacy and the man in Castries who wanted eight hundred dollars to fix the roof. He thought about the people at the fish fry tables. He thought about the frigate bird hanging over the bay without moving.

He broke the second tablet in half. He put one half back in the bottle. He crushed the other half with the back of a spoon and stirred it into her cocoa.

She drank all of it. She said it was very nice. She said he made it nearly as good as Gertrude.

He sat with her until her mouth fell open and her hand went slack on the coverlet. He checked her breathing with the back of his finger near her lips, the way you check a baby. He did that four more times between ten o'clock and one-thirty. Then he put on his old boots and his oilskin. He put a bottle of water beside the bed, which was stupid, she would never think to drink it, and he knew it, and he did it anyway. He turned the radio on low so the house would not be silent if she woke. He went out and pulled the front door to and put the chain through the hasp, and his fingers were shaking so badly he had to try the padlock three times before it closed.

---

Lenus and his nephew Junior had the *Bon Mèt* already down at the waterline. The Yamaha, which Faustin had carried down at midnight in a wheelbarrow, was clamped on the transom. The tank was full. There were no stars to the east, only a band of cloud low on the horizon, and a fingernail of moon going down behind the hills.

They pushed off. The engine caught on the second pull, and Faustin felt it come up through the tiller into his hand and into his arm. Something in his chest opened like a fist unclenching.

They went out through the gap in the reef, Lenus's boat ahead with its masthead light swinging, the *Bon Mèt* following in its wake. Beyond the reef the Atlantic took them. It lifted the bow and set it down, lifted it and set it down, in the long swell that came all the way from Africa without hitting anything. The spray came back cold over the gunwale and Faustin tasted it on his lips. He had not tasted the open sea in two years and two months. He found he had been counting.

They ran for an hour and twenty minutes east by southeast, until the island behind them was only a darker darkness with a few lights strung along the coast. Somewhere among those lights was a house on a hill with a padlock on the door. He did not look back. Then he did. Then he made himself stop.

The fish aggregating device was a buoy and a tangle of palm fronds and old rope anchored in two thousand feet of water. At first light the dorado were there under it the way Kenroy had said, green and gold and electric in the grey water, more than he had seen in years. He and Lenus fished side by side, their boats rising and falling a hundred feet apart. His hands remembered everything. The bait, the drag, the moment of the strike when the line went hard and the rod bent double and a fish broke the surface thirty yards off, shaking its blunt head, flashing every colour there was. He brought it in, and the next, and the next. He stopped thinking. For three hours there was nothing in his head but line and water and the heave of the hull under his boots.

Around seven, with the sun up and the fish slowing, he sat down on the thwart with a bottle of water. Lenus cut his engine and drifted near.

"How much?"

Faustin looked at the hold. "Two-fifty. Maybe three."

"Is good."

"Is good."

They sat with their boats knocking together gently. Far off, a container ship was going south toward Trinidad, so slow it seemed nailed to the horizon. Faustin looked at it. He thought: it would be so easy. He did not mean anything dramatic. He didn't mean the water. He meant only to keep going, east, past the edge of what he could see. Another island. Another name. A job on a boat in Martinique, where nobody knew him. Gertrude would have to come home then. Rosemund would have to pick up the phone. Then they would see. He sat with the thought for a long time, the way he had stood in the hardware with the padlock, and let himself hold it in his hand.

Lenus was watching him.

"Your father," Lenus said, "was a fool to go out that day."

"I know."

"Everybody tell him."

"I know."

"My father say he was the best fisherman in Dennery, and the biggest fool." Lenus spat over the side. "Come. Is nearly eight. Tide turning."

---

It was nearly half past nine when they came through the reef, later than he'd meant, the sun already hot. The fish fry tables were bare, the almond trees dropping leaves on the sand. He left the fish with Lenus and Junior to sell at the cooperative and went up the hill fast, faster, until he was running again with his boots slapping the hot road. The neighbours' yards went by and he thought he could feel faces at the windows.

The padlock was there. The chain was there. The radio was still playing; he could hear it through the door, a woman's voice reading the Thursday death announcements, the names going by slow and solemn. *Mrs. Marie-Thérèse Florent, of Grande Rivière. Mr. Ambrose Simeon, formerly of La Caye.*

He could not get the key into the lock.

When he got the door open it would not go more than a few inches. Something soft was against it on the inside. He put his shoulder to the frame and his face to the gap and said, "Mama," and his voice was not a voice he recognised.

She was on the floor just inside the door, sitting up against it with her legs folded under her and her back to the wood. He worked his arm through and touched her shoulder and she moved, she was moving, she was alive. He got her shifted enough to squeeze his body through.

The smell hit him. She had soiled herself, and the nightgown, and the floor, sometime in the night, and she had sat there in it. Her feet were grey with dust from the passage. She had come out of the bedroom in the dark, and down the passage in the dark, and found the door, and stayed. The water bottle was still beside the bed, unopened.

She looked up at him. Her face was creased from leaning on the door frame. Her eyes moved over his face, his oilskin, the salt dried white on his boots, and something in her came up from very far down.

"Augustin," she said. "Ou vini." You came.

He knelt down in it. He didn't care. He knelt down on the floor beside her and she put her hand flat on his cheek, on the bruise her elbow had made, and she did not take it away.

"I wait up," she said. "I tell them you would come. They say, Ida, go to bed, the sea too rough. I say no. He will come."

"Mwen la," he said. *I'm here.*

"You catch anything?"

"Dowad," he said. "Plenty."

"Good," she said. "Good. The children hungry."

---

He bathed her. She did not fight him. She sat on the plastic chair in the shower stall with the warm water running down her and her eyes closed and her face turned up into it like a girl in rain. When he did the place below her shoulder blade, the mole, she said, "You remember that," and he said yes. He did not know who she thought she was talking to, and he did not ask.

He put her into a clean nightgown, the blue one, and sat her in the chair on the gallery. He went in and got down on his knees with a bucket and a brush and the Dettol and scrubbed the floor by the door. Then he scrubbed it again. Then he sat back on his heels in the hall with his hands red and stinging and looked at the chain hanging from the hasp. He stayed like that until the radio finished the death announcements and went on to the fishing report.

When Lenus came up at noon with the envelope, his mother was asleep in the chair. Faustin was on the top step cleaning a dorado on a board across his knees. The guts went into a bowl for the cat whose name she thought was Rex.

Lenus sat down two steps below him and held out the envelope. Faustin wiped his hand on his trousers and took it and did not open it.

"Six hundred and forty," Lenus said. "Price good this week."

"Thank you."

Lenus looked at the old woman in the chair with her mouth open and her hands folded on her stomach. He looked at the bruise on Faustin's cheek and at the scrub marks still drying on the floor inside the door, and he did not say anything about any of it.

"Kenroy going Vieux Fort again in two weeks," he said instead. "The wife mother."

Faustin slid the knife under the skin of the fish and drew it back in one long motion. The fillet came away clean and he laid it in the pan of salted water. He looked at his mother. Her hand twitched on her stomach the way a dreaming dog's paw twitches, and she said something in her sleep, a name maybe, too soft to hear.

"Miss Pelagie in La Ressource," he said. "You know her?"

"The one who does sit with old people."

"You think she would come one night? Two o'clock to ten?"

Lenus thought about it. "She would come," he said. "If you pay her."

Faustin turned the fish over and started on the other side.

"Two weeks," he said.

"Two weeks."

Lenus went down the hill. The sea came in and went out. On the gallery his mother slept with her face turned toward the water. Faustin sat on the step and cleaned the fish, all of it, and when he was done he went in and washed the knife and the board. He put the envelope in the biscuit tin on top of the fridge, where she used to keep the cooperative money. He put the tin back up where she could not reach it.

Then he went back out and sat down next to her chair. When she woke and asked him who he was, he told her.
