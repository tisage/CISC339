Nguyễn Thị Bích Vân had been keeper of the Hòn Vàm light for eleven years when the fog began to sing.

Before that, her father had kept it for twenty-three. Before him, there had been a man from Phan Thiết whose name survived only in the logbooks, written in a slanting French hand beside reports of dead lamps, typhoons, and drifting mines. The tower had stood through wars, through changes of flag and paint, through years when its lantern burned kerosene and years when it ran from a generator that coughed black smoke into the rafters.

Now it shone with a clean electric pulse every six seconds: white, white, white, over the black water beyond the mangrove coast.

Hòn Vàm itself was no more than a hump of stone and scrub seventy kilometers from the mainland, with the lighthouse on its highest ridge. There was a concrete house at the foot of the tower, a rainwater tank, a diesel shed, a vegetable patch fenced against monitor lizards, and a little ancestor shelf in Vân’s room where her father’s photograph leaned between an incense bowl and a chipped blue cup.

On clear mornings, fishing boats appeared as dark strokes against the horizon. On good afternoons, the supply boat came from Rạch Giá with rice, oil, batteries, cigarettes for Vân’s assistant, and letters that always smelled faintly of fish sauce no matter who had written them.

But in the last week of the eighth lunar month, fog came down over the sea.

Fog was rare there. Rain, yes. Squalls that tore laundry from lines. Monsoon clouds, yellow and swollen. But fog belonged to the northern mountains, to the road over Hải Vân Pass, to stories told by people who had never spent a night on Hòn Vàm.

The first morning it came, Vân woke before dawn because the light in her room had turned gray.

She lay still, listening.

The sea was hidden. The cliff below the keeper’s house might have been gone. Even the tower, only twenty paces away, had vanished except for the faint revolving blink that swept through her window.

Beside her, on the floor mat, Trí snored once and turned over.

“Chị Vân,” he murmured without waking. “The weather report?”

“There wasn’t one.”

She dressed, tied her hair into a knot, and climbed the spiral stairs to the lantern room. The glass panels sweated from the cold. Beyond them was nothing.

She checked the light. She checked it again.

Then, at 4:17, while writing the visibility report in the logbook, she heard a woman singing.

The sound came from the west, below the cliff.

It was so clear that Vân first thought someone had landed a boat. She opened the narrow service hatch and leaned out.

“Who is there?”

The fog pressed against her face, wet and cold.

The woman sang on.

It was an old vọng cổ melody, one Vân knew from her mother’s kitchen radio when she was small. The singer had a low, worn voice. She drew out the last word of each line until it trembled, then broke into a breathless little laugh.

Vân listened.

She did not recognize the song at first. Then she did.

It was “Dạ Cổ Hoài Lang.”

Her mother had sung it sometimes while washing rice, never more than a few bars. She had died when Vân was twelve, and after that her father had forbidden the radio in the evenings because he said it made the house feel crowded.

The voice in the fog reached the familiar line.

From a husband far away, a wife waits through the night.

Vân’s hand tightened around the metal hatch frame.

“Má?”

The song stopped.

Only then did she realize that Trí was standing at the bottom of the stairs.

He was twenty-seven, thin as a bamboo pole, with a scar beside one eyebrow from falling out of a tamarind tree as a child. He had been sent to Hòn Vàm six months earlier after his cousin, the previous assistant, left to marry a woman in Cần Thơ. He had a boy’s appetite and a man’s habit of pretending not to be frightened.

“Did you call someone?” he asked.

“No.”

“I heard singing.”

Vân shut the hatch.

“Probably a radio on a boat.”

“In that?”

“There are boats with radios.”

Trí looked toward the fogged windows. “Not boats that sing like that.”

Vân said nothing. She returned to the desk and wrote in the logbook:

04:17. Heavy fog. Visibility less than 30 meters. Unidentified vessel possibly near western approach. Heard music. No visual confirmation.

She did not write that the voice had sounded like her mother.

By noon, the fog had lifted. It did not burn away under the sun so much as loosen and drift apart, leaving the sea bright and innocent.

There was no boat below the cliff. No anchor marks in the small cove. No trail across the rocks. Trí took the binoculars and searched the horizon until his neck reddened.

“Nothing,” he said.

Vân stood beside him.

Far out to sea, a trawler moved steadily north, its nets trailing like torn wings.

“Maybe it passed before sunrise,” she said.

Trí did not answer.

That evening, he put a cup of tea beside her at the control desk and said, “My grandmother used to say fog carries people who have not found their way home.”

“Your grandmother said many things.”

“She also said never answer if someone calls you from the sea after midnight.”

Vân looked at him.

Trí busied himself with the radio set. “I’m only saying.”

“My father heard things too.”

The young man stopped.

Vân had not meant to say it.

Her father, Nguyễn Văn Khải, had died four years before in weather so clear the officials had used the word “unfortunate” three times at his funeral. He had taken the motorboat out at night, alone, after a distress signal came over the radio: a fisherman calling from somewhere west of Hòn Vàm, saying his engine had failed.

The radio recording had been damaged by salt water, but the investigation office in Rạch Giá had managed to identify the call sign. It belonged to a vessel that had sunk two years earlier in a storm near the Cambodian border. All six men aboard had been lost.

Her father’s boat was found at dawn, circling slowly with its engine still running. One oar was gone. The fuel tank was half full. His sandals were neatly placed under the bench as if he had stepped out of them before entering someone’s house.

His body was never found.

Afterward, Vân had come to Hòn Vàm to settle his things and stay for one month. The replacement keeper had not arrived. One month became three. Three became a year. Eventually, people stopped asking when she planned to leave.

That night, Trí slept with the radio turned low beside his mat.

Vân did not sleep at all.

The fog returned the following evening.

It came in from the west after sunset, swallowing the last smear of orange sky. By eight, the lighthouse beam had become a pale pillar that went nowhere. The generator thudded in its shed. Water ticked from the eaves.

At 9:32, the foghorn gave its automatic blast.

A long low moan rolled over the island.

Then another horn answered from the sea.

Not an echo.

Vân knew the difference. An echo came back blurred and weakened, a ghost of the first sound. This was deeper. Closer. It began a moment after Hòn Vàm’s horn ended and held its note for nearly ten seconds.

Trí rose from his chair.

“Chị.”

Vân was already at the radio.

“Any station, any station, this is Hòn Vàm Light. Identify vessel west of Hòn Vàm. Over.”

Static crackled.

She repeated the message in English, then in Vietnamese again.

Nothing.

The foghorn sounded at 9:35.

The other horn answered.

At 9:38, a voice came through the static.

Not over the radio.

From outside.

“Vân ơi.”

Her father’s voice had always been rough in the mornings and gentle after he had drunk rice wine. It was gentle now.

“Vân ơi. Bring the lamp.”

Trí’s chair scraped backward.

Vân stood very still.

“Chị Vân,” he whispered.

Her father spoke again, this time from below the window, where the cliff dropped straight into twenty meters of water.

“Bring the lamp. It’s dark.”

Vân crossed the lantern room in three strides. Trí caught her arm.

“Don’t open it.”

“Let go.”

“That isn’t bác Khải.”

She stared at him. In the sweep of the light, his face seemed green and hollow.

“He’s dead,” Trí said.

Vân slapped him.

The sound shocked them both.

Trí’s hand went to his cheek. Vân’s breath came fast. Outside, her father called her name once more.

Then the second foghorn sounded.

This time it was very near.

The glass panels shuddered in their frames.

Vân ran down the stairs, past the equipment room, through the ground-floor door, and into the fog.

Behind her, Trí shouted, “Chị Vân!”

The air smelled wrong. Not of seaweed, mud, and salt, but of incense ash after rain.

She could see only the next few stones of the path. The lighthouse beam passed overhead in pulses, whitening the fog, then leaving it blacker than before. Somewhere to her left, at the edge of the cliff, water struck rock in slow, heavy blows.

“Ba!” she called.

No answer.

She walked farther than she should have. She knew that later. She knew it whenever she woke sweating in the months afterward: the path had a broken railing thirty meters from the tower, and beyond it the hillside sloped down through loose limestone. One wrong step in that fog and she would have gone over.

But then she heard something else.

A bell.

A small brass bell, ringing rapidly.

The bell came from the old shrine near the western cove.

It had been built before Vân’s father’s time: a little concrete shelter holding a painted wooden figure of Bà Thủy, Lady of the Waters, with a red sash around her waist and plastic flowers at her feet. Fishermen who stopped at Hòn Vàm left cigarettes, oranges, tiny cups of liquor. Vân did not consider herself particularly devout, but she swept the shrine on the first and fifteenth of every lunar month. Her father had always done so.

The bell rang again.

Vân stumbled toward it.

The fog thinned just enough for her to see the shrine’s roof. A lantern burned inside.

That was impossible. There was no lantern there. There had not been oil in it for months.

She approached slowly.

At the foot of the shrine steps lay a bundle of wet netting.

The bell rang from inside the net.

Vân bent and pulled it aside.

A little girl stared up at her.

For an instant, Vân saw her clearly: perhaps seven years old, hair cut bluntly at the chin, wearing a yellow dress darkened with water. Her lips were blue.

“Cô,” the girl said. “My grandmother is cold.”

Then Trí crashed through the fog behind Vân, gripping a flashlight.

The beam struck the bundle.

There was no girl.

Only netting, a chipped bell, and an old life jacket swollen with seawater.

Trí seized Vân by both shoulders.

“What are you doing?”

She looked past him, toward the cove.

The fog had opened over the water.

A boat floated there.

It was a narrow wooden fishing boat, painted blue once, though almost all the paint had peeled away. No engine showed at the stern. No light burned aboard. It rocked without sound in the dark water twenty meters from shore.

On its bow was a name, written in faded white letters:

MINH HẢI 7.

Vân knew the name.

Everyone along that coast knew it.

The Minh Hải 7 had gone down during a typhoon seventeen years ago. It had carried twenty-three people from a village near Hà Tiên, including a grandmother named Lệ and her granddaughter, who had been traveling to the mainland for treatment. Only four bodies had been recovered.

Vân had been sixteen then. She remembered the photographs in the newspaper: men standing on a ruined pier, women gripping each other’s shoulders, a child’s pink sandal tangled in reeds.

The boat in the cove gave a small creak.

Then it drifted backward into the fog and disappeared.

Trí made a sound like a prayer caught in his throat.

Vân did not speak until they had returned to the keeper’s house and bolted the door.

At dawn, the fog was gone.

In the cove, caught between two rocks, was the old life jacket.

Nothing else.

The next supply boat was due in five days. Vân tried to call the mainland station, but the radio signal faded in and out. She reported the foghorn, the possible vessel, the poor reception. She did not mention the singing, her father, or the Minh Hải 7.

The station manager, a man named Đức with a soft voice and an impatience for anything that complicated schedules, told her to keep records and conserve fuel.

“Fog at this time of year is strange,” he said. “But weather is strange now.”

“The horn wasn’t weather.”

“Perhaps a cargo ship.”

“There was no response.”

“Then keep calling.”

Vân almost said, The boat sank seventeen years ago.

Instead, she said, “Yes, anh Đức.”

After the call, Trí stood in the doorway with his arms crossed.

“You should tell him.”

“And say what?”

“What happened.”

“What happened, Trí?”

He looked away.

They spent the day preparing the motorboat. It was a squat aluminum craft with a patched canopy, named BÌNH MINH in flaking red paint. Its engine was old but reliable. Vân checked the fuel line, battery, emergency flares, life jackets, compass, hand radio, and the small sack of rice they always kept aboard in case they were stranded overnight.

Trí watched her from the dock.

“You’re going out.”

“I’m going to look at the western channel.”

“In this weather?”

“The weather is clear.”

“That boat wasn’t there in daylight.”

“Then I’ll find out where it came from.”

“You said it sank.”

“I said nothing.”

Trí’s eyes hardened. “Your father went looking too.”

The wrench slipped in Vân’s hand and struck the hull.

For a while, only the small slap of water under the dock could be heard.

Then she said, “You can stay.”

Trí climbed into the boat and took the bow line from its cleat.

The western channel was a bad place in low visibility. Beneath the surface, black limestone rose in ridges like the backs of sleeping animals. There were reefs not marked on recent charts and sandbars that appeared after storms, then vanished again. Vân’s father had taught her to read the water: green meant shallow; a smooth dark patch might mean depth, or might mean oil; birds clustered where fish came up.

They circled the island once. Nothing.

They went farther west, toward the old coordinates of the Minh Hải 7’s wreck.

At noon, the sea changed color.

“Stop,” Vân said.

Trí cut the engine.

The water around them was unnaturally still. Not calm; held. The breeze continued to push at Vân’s shirt, but the sea lay flat as poured glass.

She looked over the side.

At first she saw only her own reflection, broken by the boat’s shadow. Then, beneath it, came a pale square.

A roof.

No, not a roof.

The top of a cabin, far below.

The wreck lay under them, its hull split open and furred with coral. Fish moved through its windows. A length of net trailed from the stern like hair.

Vân crouched at the gunwale.

Something was caught in the net.

A brass bell.

It turned slowly in the current though there seemed to be no current at all.

Trí made the sign of the cross, though he was not Catholic. His mother had taught him the gesture after his brother drowned in a flooded quarry.

“Let’s go,” he said.

Vân did not move.

Through the clear water, she saw a second shape near the wreck: an anchor chain descending into the blue.

It was fresh iron. Too clean. Too straight.

“Trí,” she said. “Look.”

They followed the chain with their eyes until it disappeared beneath the boat. At the surface, thirty meters away, a small black buoy rose and fell.

On it was a strip of red cloth.

Vân started the engine.

They went toward the buoy.

The chain led down to a cage.

It took them several minutes to understand what they were seeing. The cage was made from welded steel bars, perhaps three meters long and two wide, sunk just beneath the surface. A blue tarpaulin had been tied over the top, but the knots had loosened. Through a gap, Vân saw a hand gripping one of the bars.

“People!” she shouted.

The hand disappeared.

Trí swung the boat alongside. There were voices inside the cage—hoarse, frightened voices. Vân ripped at the tarp while Trí held the boat steady against the chop.

Underneath were six people.

Three men. Two women. One teenage boy.

They were packed together in knee-deep seawater. Their faces were burned raw by salt and sun. One woman held a plastic bottle to her chest as if it were a baby.

For a moment, no one spoke.

Then the boy said, “Please.”

Vân’s stomach turned cold.

The cage had no door visible from above. A chain had been padlocked through its lower frame.

“They left us last night,” one of the men said. His accent was from the far north, but his Vietnamese was clear. “They said another boat would come.”

“What boat?”

The man shook his head. “They took our phones. They took everything.”

Vân grabbed the bolt cutters from the emergency kit.

“They may come back,” Trí said.

“Then they can find us gone.”

The lock was thick. The cutters slipped once, twice. Her palms blistered. Beneath her, people began to cry quietly. The woman with the bottle tried to smile at Vân and failed.

On the fourth attempt, the shackle snapped.

The cage door opened outward with a scream of rusted metal.

The people climbed onto the BÌNH MINH one by one. The boat sank lower with every body. Vân gave them water in capfuls, afraid they would drink too fast. Trí wrapped the youngest woman in the emergency blanket. The teenage boy sat at Vân’s feet and stared back at the cage.

“What is this place?” he asked.

No one answered.

As they turned toward Hòn Vàm, the fog began to form again.

It came fast.

The sky was blue overhead. The sun was bright enough to hurt Vân’s eyes. Yet a wall of white advanced across the sea from the west, swallowing the buoy, then the wreck site, then the open water behind them.

“Go faster,” Trí said.

The overloaded motorboat groaned. The engine sputtered but held.

The fog reached them before they had gone a kilometer.

Within seconds, the island vanished.

Vân gripped the tiller. Her compass needle swung wildly, then spun.

“No,” she said.

The hand radio crackled on the floor.

At first it was only static.

Then a woman began to sing.

The passengers froze.

The old vọng cổ melody rose around them, not from the radio but from every direction. It sounded farther away than before, and sadder. Vân could hear the singer’s breath between the phrases.

The teenage boy began to sob.

Trí leaned close. “Chị. The lighthouse is east.”

“The compass is broken.”

“You know the bearing.”

“I know.”

But she did not. The fog had made the world without edges. Engine noise, frightened breathing, water against the hull—these came from nowhere and everywhere. She turned the boat one way, then another.

The foghorn sounded.

A long low call.

Vân wheeled toward it.

“No!” Trí grabbed her sleeve. “That isn’t ours.”

The horn sounded again, deeper than Hòn Vàm’s.

Then, under the song, Vân heard her father.

“Bring the lamp.”

Her hands shook.

The boat’s bow swung west.

“Chị Vân!”

Trí slapped the tiller from her grip. The BÌNH MINH lurched. One of the rescued men cried out as he slid against the side.

Vân caught herself against the engine housing. For an instant, anger flared through her so fiercely she nearly struck Trí again.

Then he pointed.

Ahead of them, barely visible through the fog, rose the black ribs of the Minh Hải 7.

Not beneath the water now.

Before them.

The wreck stood half out of the sea as if lifted by a giant hand. Water poured from its broken hull. On its tilted deck were figures: a woman in a conical hat, a child in yellow, men with their hands raised against the light.

Her father stood among them.

He was wearing the pale blue work shirt in which he had disappeared. He looked exactly as he had on the last morning Vân saw him alive, when he had eaten two bowls of porridge and complained that she put too much pepper in it.

“Vân ơi,” he called.

The rescued people were screaming. Trí had both hands on the tiller, fighting to turn them away.

The boat’s engine coughed.

The horn sounded once more.

Vân saw, behind the wreck, the sudden white teeth of a reef.

The BÌNH MINH was racing directly toward it.

She seized the throttle and shoved it down. The engine roared. Trí swung the bow hard to port. The boat tilted so violently that water poured over the side. The woman under the emergency blanket fell against Vân’s legs.

For one terrible second, the propeller chewed empty water.

Then it caught.

The boat shot sideways.

Something struck the hull with a grinding blow. Vân was thrown across the deck. She heard metal tear. But they had cleared the reef.

Behind them, the singing stopped.

The foghorn of Hòn Vàm sounded from somewhere ahead.

This one had the familiar unevenness in its last note, caused by a small crack in the horn’s diaphragm that Đức kept promising to replace.

Vân laughed once, sharply, because she could not help it.

“There,” she said. “That one is ours.”

They followed the broken note.

When the island emerged from the fog, Trí began to cry. He kept one hand on the tiller and wiped his face with the other as if irritated by the tears.

They brought the six people ashore.

The keeper’s house had only two rooms, but they made space. Vân cooked rice thin with water and added the last of the dried fish. The rescued people ate slowly. The teenage boy’s name was Hậu. One of the women was named Mến. She had a deep cut on her ankle and a wedding ring tied to a string around her neck.

“We were told we would work in kitchens,” Mến said after dark. “In another country. They said papers would be arranged.”

“Who told you?”

“A man called Lộc. Maybe that wasn’t his name.”

She looked toward the window, where fog beaded on the glass.

“They kept us on a boat for two days. Last night they put us in that cage. We heard a horn. Then they said a patrol was coming, and they left.”

Vân thought of the wreck, the buoy, the cage anchored over the dead boat.

“They’ll be back,” Trí said.

“Yes,” Vân said.

The radio could not raise the mainland station. The fog swallowed the signal before it traveled ten kilometers. Vân tried the emergency frequency. Nothing.

At midnight, she climbed alone to the lantern room.

The light swept its patient circle across the white dark.

Below, the foghorn breathed every three minutes.

On the desk lay the logbook. She opened to a blank page and wrote the time, the location of the cage, the number of people rescued, the condition of the motorboat. Her handwriting was steadier than she expected.

Then she heard the bell.

Not the shrine bell. Not the brass bell from the wreck.

The telephone bell.

The old black landline on the desk had not worked in years. Its wire ran down the tower wall and ended in a junction box that had corroded long before Vân became keeper.

It rang once.

Vân looked at it.

It rang again.

She picked up the receiver.

For several seconds there was only a wet rushing sound, as though someone held the phone under water.

Then her father said, “The channel is not safe.”

Vân closed her eyes.

“Ba,” she said.

“You must turn on the old lamp.”

“The old lamp is gone.”

“Downstairs.”

The line clicked dead.

Vân stood with the receiver in her hand.

Then she went down.

The original lamp assembly had been stored in the maintenance room beneath tarps and spare cables. It was a brass-and-glass apparatus from the days before automation, its lens cracked, its kerosene reservoir empty. Her father had kept it though everyone told him it was useless. Once, when she was a girl, he had polished its brass for an entire afternoon while telling her that old lights had their own memory.

“Ships don’t only follow brightness,” he had said. “They follow what they know.”

Vân uncovered it.

Behind the lamp, hidden in a wooden crate, was a small cabinet she had never seen opened. Its lock had rusted away. She lifted the lid.

Inside were paper charts.

Not official charts. Her father’s.

He had drawn them by hand over many years: the reefs, the shifting sandbanks, the places where illegal boats liked to shelter. On the newest chart, dated four months before his death, a red circle marked the wreck of the Minh Hải 7.

Beside it, in her father’s writing, were three words:

CAGE ANCHOR POINT.

Vân stared at the ink.

Underneath lay a page torn from a logbook.

11:40 p.m. Unidentified vessel. Voices in fog. Possible holding platform near old wreck. Reporting failure. Going to verify.

The date was the night he died.

For a long time, Vân could hear nothing but the lighthouse mechanism turning above her.

Then a boat engine sounded from the western side of the island.

Low. Approaching without lights.

She ran upstairs.

Through the lantern glass, beyond the reach of the beam, she saw a dark shape moving in the fog.

Another boat followed it.

The smugglers had returned.

Vân went down two steps at a time. In the house, Trí was already awake.

“I heard them,” he said.

“They’re coming for the cage.”

“And for us?”

“They don’t know we’re here.”

A fist struck the front door.

Everyone inside flinched.

Another fist.

A man’s voice called, “Keeper! We need fuel. Engine trouble.”

Trí looked at Vân.

The six rescued people had gone silent. Hậu clutched a kitchen knife in both hands.

Vân shook her head.

Outside, the man called again. “Open up!”

Then, from farther down the path, came another sound: a foghorn.

Not one blast.

Three short blasts, repeated.

Vân’s eyes went to the old lamp.

Her father’s hand-drawn chart lay on the table between them. The western channel. The marked reef. The shoal that reached almost to the surface on the approach to the cove.

She understood what he had meant.

Not turn on the lamp.

Use it.

“Trí,” she said. “Can you climb?”

“What?”

“The old lamp. The shutter wheel still works if we connect it to the emergency battery.”

He stared at her, then looked at the chart.

Outside, something struck the door hard enough to crack the frame.

“Yes,” he said.

They moved quickly.

Vân sent the rescued people into the equipment room and barred it from outside. She gave Hậu the flare gun and told him not to use it unless someone entered the house. Trí carried the old lamp up to the lantern room while Vân dragged the emergency battery from the generator shed.

The men outside had stopped pretending their engine was in trouble.

“Open the door!” someone shouted.

A window shattered.

Vân connected the leads with hands that shook from effort and fear. The ancient lamp flickered once, twice, then sprang to life in a weak yellow blaze.

“It won’t reach far,” Trí said.

“It doesn’t need to.”

She opened the old signal shutter, a heavy metal screen that could be swung across the lamp to make flashes.

Below them, the unlit boats were moving closer to the western rocks.

Vân studied her father’s chart.

Then she began to signal.

Long flash. Short. Short. Long.

Not Morse. Not any official code.

The pattern her father had used when he taught her to find him on shore as a child: two quick flashes, one long, two quick again. This way, Ba. This way.

The smugglers’ first boat turned.

Its engine note changed.

A man shouted something across the water.

Vân gave the signal again, lower this time, angling the shutter so the yellow flashes pointed toward the false gap between two rocks.

The fog made the light seem farther away than it was.

The boat followed.

Trí saw what she was doing and went pale.

“That’s the shoal.”

“Yes.”

“They could die.”

“So could everyone downstairs.”

The first boat accelerated toward the yellow flashes.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then came a noise like a house splitting open.

The boat struck the reef broadside.

Men shouted. An engine screamed. The second boat swung away too late and collided with the first. One of its men fired something—perhaps a pistol, perhaps only a flare—but the red light shot upward, arced, and fell hissing into the sea.

The foghorn sounded.

Hòn Vàm’s horn.

Its long call rolled out over the water, enormous and certain.

Vân seized the radio handset.

“Mayday relay, mayday relay,” she said. “This is Hòn Vàm Light. Human trafficking victims rescued. Two vessels wrecked on west reef. Immediate assistance required.”

Static answered.

She spoke again.

Then, through the hiss, a voice said, “Hòn Vàm, this is Coast Guard Station Kiên Lương. Repeat your message.”

Vân sank into the chair.

By sunrise, the fog had begun to break.

A coast guard cutter came from the east, blue and white against the gray water. Its crew took the rescued people aboard, wrapped them in clean blankets, and carried the injured smugglers from the reef. One man had broken his leg. Another had cut his head badly. The rest were hauled from the boats wet, furious, and alive.

The cutter captain, a square-faced woman named Lieutenant Hạnh, listened without interruption as Vân described the cage, the wreck, the charts, and the radio failures.

When Vân showed her father’s hidden log entry, Lieutenant Hạnh read it twice.

“He found them,” she said.

“Yes.”

“He may have been trying to report it.”

“The radio recording said he answered a distress call from a boat that had sunk years before.”

The lieutenant looked out toward the western water.

“People use old call signs,” she said. “Old boats. Old names. Makes it difficult to trace them.”

Vân said nothing.

Lieutenant Hạnh folded the paper carefully and put it into a waterproof folder.

“We’ll search,” she said.

That afternoon, the fog was gone completely.

The sea lay blue and hard under the sun. Around the wrecked smugglers’ boats, coast guard divers moved in and out of the water. Farther west, above the old wreck of the Minh Hải 7, a buoy now marked the place in bright official orange.

Before the cutter left, Hậu came back up the lighthouse path.

He had changed into a coast guard uniform shirt too large for him. He stood awkwardly beside the shrine, where someone had righted the small brass bell.

“Cô Vân,” he said.

“Yes?”

He held out a folded square of paper. “I drew this for you.”

It was a picture in blue ballpoint ink: the lighthouse on a cliff, a boat beneath it, and a huge sun above the sea. The lighthouse beam extended all the way across the page.

Vân took it.

“Thank you.”

Hậu nodded and ran back toward the dock before she could say anything else.

That evening, Trí repaired the broken window with a sheet of plywood. He worked in silence for a long time.

Finally, he said, “I’m sorry I stopped you that day.”

“What day?”

“When you heard your father.”

Vân looked toward the west, where the sunset had turned the water copper.

“You were right to stop me.”

“He did help us, though.”

She did not answer.

Trí hammered another nail.

After dark, Vân climbed to the lantern room alone.

The electric light turned, faithful as ever. White, white, white.

On the desk, she opened the logbook.

For a while she held her pen over the page.

Then she wrote:

19:12. Visibility good. Sea moderate. All systems functioning.

She closed the book and stood by the glass.

Below, the shrine bell moved once in the evening wind.

Vân waited for singing, for a horn from a drowned boat, for her father’s voice asking her to bring the lamp.

Nothing came.

Far out beyond the island, a fishing vessel answered the lighthouse with one ordinary flash of its deck light, then continued north through the dark.
