Marva Sookdeo’s theatre fit into a fish crate, a length of white cotton, and the hollow beneath the old bandstand on the Brian Lara Promenade.

By four o’clock, when the heat had softened enough for people to leave the shade, she had the two bamboo poles tied upright with clothesline and the cloth stretched between them. She weighed the corners with bottles of Mauby and laid out her tin cup for coins. Behind the screen she arranged her puppets: Anansi with his eight wire legs, La Diablesse in her long red skirt, a fisherman with a net made from an onion bag, a blue-devil with bottle-cap eyes.

Then she placed one small square of black cloth on the ground.

It looked like nothing. A handkerchief, maybe, folded twice.

“Miss Marva,” said a voice from the front. “You doing the spider story today?”

A little girl stood there in a yellow school uniform, her plaits tied with green beads. She had a book bag larger than her back.

“If you bring your whole classroom, I might,” Marva said.

The child considered this. “I have only me.”

“Then I doing it especially for you.”

Marva ducked behind the cloth. From there she could see the crowd gathering by their feet: school shoes, office shoes, slippers, one pair of white trainers with grass stains, a man’s polished brown loafers. She knew the crowd by feet before she knew it by faces.

She struck two spoons together.

“Ladies, gentlemen, children, and all who have come to borrow breeze on the Promenade,” she called, “welcome to the smallest theatre in Trinidad. Here you will see a spider who believes himself cleverer than hunger, a lady with one hoof, a fisherman who catches the moon, and”—she paused, as she always did—“a boy who has no name.”

The spoons clicked once more.

Anansi climbed up first, a sharp little black shape against the white cloth. The girl in yellow laughed when he tripped over his own legs. The fisherman came next, flinging his onion-bag net over a silver paper moon. La Diablesse crossed the screen with her parasol tilted low, and every child in the front row leaned sideways to see whether Marva would show the hoof.

She never did.

The crowd grew thicker. A woman from the doubles stand paused with a foil container in each hand. Two delivery riders stopped their bicycles. A man selling phone cases set down his cardboard tray. Somebody whistled when the blue-devil appeared, dancing on his hands.

Then Marva lifted the black square of cloth.

The crowd quieted.

The cloth rose by itself.

It did not float exactly. It climbed, slowly, as though invisible fingers held its corners. It unfolded in the air. Behind it came the shadow of a boy: thin arms, loose knees, head cocked slightly to one side.

He wore no costume. He was only a silhouette, blacker than the other shadows. Yet people always watched him more closely than they watched the puppets.

The boy picked up the silver moon in both hands and rolled it across his shoulders. He balanced it on one finger. He tossed it high. It became a small fish, then a bird, then a drum.

When he began to dance, the spoons in Marva’s hands kept time without touching each other.

At the end he bowed.

Coins chimed into the tin cup.

The girl in yellow stayed after the others had drifted away.

“Who is he?” she asked.

Marva began folding the white cloth.

“A boy with no name.”

“But he have to have one.”

“Everybody thinks so.”

The child peered beneath the screen. Marva’s heart tightened, though there was nothing to see but the fish crate, her sandals, the bamboo poles, and the dust-coated black square resting where it had begun.

“You does have somebody hiding?” the girl asked.

“Only cockroaches.”

The girl seemed satisfied by this. She put a ten-dollar note in the tin cup, which was far too much, and ran toward a woman waiting by the pavement.

Behind the folded cloth, where no one could see, a shadow put one hand on Marva’s wrist.

It was cool and weightless as the underside of a leaf.

“You give away too much money,” Marva murmured.

The shadow hand squeezed once.

“You always was a fool.”

It had no voice. It had not had one in twenty-three years. But Marva understood him.

She carried the crate home to St. James on the bus, sitting with it between her knees. At every stop she checked that the black square lay flat inside, beneath the puppets. She checked again when she got off by the parlour near Mucurapo Road. She checked once more at her gate.

Her mother, Kamla, was sitting on the gallery with her feet in a basin of warm water. A hand towel covered her hair. The radio beside her was playing an old chutney song at low volume.

“You make anything?” Kamla asked.

“Enough for bread and some tablets.”

“You buy the tablets?”

“I buy the tablets.”

Kamla gave a small grunt. She had been suspicious of good news since the first time Marva had brought home a man with a car and a gold chain.

Marva took the crate inside. In the kitchen, she unfolded the black cloth on the table. For a while, nothing happened.

Then the shadow of a boy appeared on the wall beside the refrigerator.

He was not cast by the kitchen light. The fluorescent tube overhead was behind him, and his shadow should have fallen the other way. But there he stood, long-limbed and fifteen forever, one shoulder higher than the other.

“You bend your wrist when you dance,” Marva told him.

The boy’s shadow looked at its own wrist.

“You always did that. People could recognize.”

He made a dismissive gesture, the same one Ravi had made when he was alive and seventeen and too handsome for his own good.

“Don’t start with me,” Marva said. “Today a child ask questions.”

The shadow leaned against the wall.

“She was small.”

“Small ones have mouths too.”

At the mention of mouths, the shadow’s head dipped.

Marva immediately regretted it.

Outside, a car horn blared. Somebody on the street shouted for somebody else to bring back a bucket. The ordinary sounds of evening came through the louvre windows: pots clanging, a baby crying, the sharp pop-pop-pop of a bicycle tyre rolling over broken gravel.

Kamla called from the gallery, “Marva! You cooking or you standing up there talking to yourself?”

“Cooking.”

Marva made pelau with less chicken than she would have liked and too much pumpkin, then took two plates to the gallery. Her mother ate slowly, watching the road.

“The woman from dialysis tell me she see you on the internet,” Kamla said.

Marva stopped with her fork halfway to her mouth.

“What internet?”

“She say some boy film you by the Promenade. He put you on the TikTak.”

“TikTok.”

“That foolishness. She say plenty people watching.”

Kamla’s eyes stayed on the road. Her voice had changed, becoming careful.

“Who film?”

“I don’t know.”

“You better find out.”

Marva did not answer.

Later, when Kamla had gone to bed, Marva borrowed the neighbour’s Wi-Fi from the back room and searched her own name. There were dozens of videos: children screaming at the blue-devil, tourists laughing when Anansi stole the moon, Marva herself in her patched purple head wrap, looking older than she felt.

Then she found the new one.

It had been posted by a page called TriniHiddenGems. In the video, the camera shook with laughter and traffic noise. The caption read:

YOU THINK YOU KNOW STREET MAGIC? WATCH THIS TO THE END.

The screen filled with the white cloth.

La Diablesse passed by. The fisherman caught the moon. Then the black square rose, and the boy appeared.

The person filming whispered, “Nah, nah, nah. Look at that.”

The boy danced.

The video had two hundred and thirteen thousand views.

Marva watched it through once. Then twice. On the third viewing she saw what she had missed.

Near the end, just before he bowed, Ravi had done the old move: the small twist of his right hand, palm turned outward, fingers fluttering as though he were shaking rain from them.

He had learned it from their father, who had danced in a tassa group when he was young. Ravi used to do it in the kitchen, on the road, in church after weddings when the music started and the older people said, “Look at that Sookdeo boy making a nuisance of himself.”

Marva’s stomach went cold.

On the screen, comments rolled beneath the video.

This is edited.

No way that boy real.

A man behind the curtain, obviously.

I remember something like this from years ago on Frederick Street.

Who is the dancer???

One comment had been posted only eleven minutes before.

That looks like Ravi Sookdeo. But Ravi dead since 2001.

Marva shut the phone so quickly that it slipped from her hands and hit the floor.

The shadow on the wall had gone still.

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Marva said, “We not performing tomorrow.”

Ravi’s shadow lifted one hand.

“No.”

“No,” she repeated. “No Promenade. No Queen’s Park. Nowhere.”

The shadow bent and folded in on itself until it was only a black shape on the wall. It slid down to the floor and disappeared beneath the door of the back room.

Marva slept badly. In the middle of the night she woke to the sound of music: the thin, tinny rhythm of tassa played from no instrument she could see.

In the morning, there was a message on her phone.

Hello Miss Sookdeo! My name is Cleon Fernandes. I manage cultural content and live events. Your shadow theatre is incredible. We would love to feature you at our Carnival Family Fair this Saturday. Paid booking, proper stage, lighting, promotion. Call me.

Below it was another message, from a number she did not know.

Marva? This is Renée from school. I saw the video. I know it sounds mad, but that dancer really does look like your brother.

Marva read the second message until the letters blurred.

She had not spoken to Renée Ramdial since Form Five. Renée had been at Ravi’s funeral. She had stood in the back of the chapel with two other girls and cried noisily into a pink handkerchief. Ravi had flirted with all three of them. He had been dead six days.

Marva deleted neither message.

At breakfast, Kamla drank tea without sugar and watched her daughter’s face.

“You see it,” she said.

“Yes.”

“What you going to do?”

“I don’t know.”

Kamla looked toward the closed door of the back room.

“Your brother was always fond of a crowd.”

“Mummy.”

“I saying what I saying.”

“You didn’t see that comment?”

“I see.”

“They calling his name.”

“They don’t know.”

“One person know.”

Kamla’s lips pressed together. The thin skin around her eyes had folded deeply in the last year. “A dead person can get tired too, Marva.”

Marva stood so sharply that her chair scraped.

“Don’t say that.”

Kamla flinched, and Marva hated herself at once.

Her mother lowered her voice. “He been coming when you call for twenty-three years. You make a little stage and you give him stories. You give him children clapping. Maybe that is good. Maybe it is not. But don’t behave like he is yours to keep.”

“He came because he wanted to.”

“Did he?”

Marva picked up the plates and carried them to the sink.

When Ravi died, he had been on his bicycle, coming down the hill in Curepe after rain. A maxi had skidded at the corner. The driver had said the road was slick. The police had said Ravi had not been wearing a helmet, as though that settled the matter.

At his wake, people had sung too loudly. The house had smelled of lilies and curry and wet umbrellas. Marva, who was sixteen and furious at everyone, had hidden behind a sheet hung in the backyard to keep off the rain.

There, in the dark behind the cloth, she had seen Ravi’s shadow walk past.

Not a memory. Not grief. Not the flutter of headlights.

Ravi.

She had called his name once.

The shadow stopped.

Then their grandmother, old Nani Bissessar, came up behind Marva and slapped one hand over her mouth.

“Never call a night-walker by name if you want him to find his road,” Nani whispered.

But Ravi had not found his road.

Over the years he came more easily to cloth, to screens, to the dark shine of a switched-off television. He could not touch anything solid. He could not speak. He could dance.

When Marva began performing on the Promenade after the garment factory closed, he had stepped behind the white cloth during a rainstorm and lifted Anansi’s puppet without strings.

The first crowd had screamed.

The second crowd had paid.

Marva told herself it was not a cage. She told herself he could leave whenever he wanted.

She did not tell herself why she always kept the black cloth folded under her pillow.

On Friday evening Cleon Fernandes came to the house wearing white sneakers and a shirt printed with tiny gold palm trees. He was younger than Marva had expected, perhaps twenty-eight, with a bright smile and a phone mounted on a little stick.

“Miss Sookdeo,” he said, extending both hands. “A privilege. Honestly. I love authentic Trini art.”

Marva looked at the phone. “Put that down.”

He laughed. “Of course, of course.”

He did not put it down.

They sat on the gallery while Kamla listened from her chair. Cleon spoke about branding, sponsors, an audience of families, a proper stage with proper lights. He offered Marva five thousand dollars for twenty minutes on Saturday afternoon.

Five thousand dollars was two months of dialysis transport, groceries, and the electricity bill. It was a new roof sheet above the kitchen where rainwater had been collecting in a blue barrel. It was enough money to make her palms sweat.

“You see the video?” Marva asked.

“Everybody see the video. That is why I’m here.”

“Take it down.”

Cleon blinked. “Take down what?”

“The video.”

“But it driving interest.”

“Take it down.”

He tilted his head, considering whether she was serious. “Miss Sookdeo, I understand artists have their mystery. I’m not asking how you do it.”

“You don’t understand anything.”

Kamla made a small sound in her throat. Marva looked at her. Her mother’s gaze had shifted beyond Cleon, toward the dark hallway.

The shadow of a boy stood there, barely visible against the wall.

Cleon followed Marva’s eyes but saw nothing.

He smiled again. “Saturday is going to be lovely. Children, local food, mas camp demonstrations. We put you on at five-thirty, before the fire dancers. You can use the money.”

“I said no.”

“Marva,” Kamla said.

It was the first time she had called her by her full name in years.

Marva stared at the worn floorboards of the gallery. Ravi’s shadow had moved closer to the doorway. His head was bent.

Five thousand dollars.

The comment beneath the video.

That looks like Ravi Sookdeo. But Ravi dead since 2001.

“All right,” Marva said.

Cleon clapped his hands together. “Wonderful.”

“But no filming.”

He hesitated.

“No livestream. No phones near the stage. No recording.”

“We can ask people, but—”

“No recording.”

Cleon’s smile thinned. “That is difficult in 2024.”

“Then find somebody else.”

He looked from Marva to Kamla and back again. At last he nodded.

“Fine. We make it special. You have my word.”

After he left, Marva went inside and shut the back-room door.

Ravi’s shadow stood on the far wall.

“You hear?” she asked. “One show. After that, we rest.”

He did not move.

“You want to go?”

The shadow’s shoulders changed. It was the nearest thing he had to a shrug.

“You could have gone all this time.”

He lifted his right hand and made the old rain-shaking gesture.

Marva laughed once, but it came out like a sob.

“You wicked, Ravi.”

The shadow moved toward her. When it touched her face, she felt no hand, only a sudden coolness around her eyes.

Saturday arrived bright and breathless. The Carnival Family Fair occupied the eastern end of the Savannah, all tents and bunting and competing music. Children in sequined costumes chased each other between food stalls. A moko jumbie in blue trousers stepped above the crowd on long stilts. The smell of corn soup, hot oil, sweat, and damp grass lay over everything.

Cleon had given Marva a small stage under a canopy. It was too large. Her white cloth looked fragile there, stretched between poles that rose above her head. Two security guards stood at the front, telling people not to block the aisle.

No recording, Marva reminded them.

One guard nodded without looking at her phone.

Behind the screen, Ravi’s shadow lay flat and dark against the black cloth at Marva’s feet.

“You don’t have to come,” she whispered.

Nothing moved.

The audience gathered. Far more people than Marva had ever played for on the Promenade. Some had come because they knew her work. Some had come because they had seen the video. Many held phones at chest height despite the signs.

At the edge of the crowd, Marva saw Renée Ramdial.

She was heavier now, with silver in her hair and sunglasses pushed up on her head. Beside her stood a boy of about nine, chewing the string of a balloon.

Renée saw Marva looking and raised one uncertain hand.

Marva’s mouth went dry.

Cleon stepped onto the stage with his little microphone. “Family, family, family! We have something special for you now. A true hidden jewel of Trinidad and Tobago. Please welcome Miss Marva Sookdeo and the Moon-Shadow Theatre!”

Applause.

Marva stepped behind the cloth.

She began with Anansi.

Her hands shook so badly that the spider caught one wire leg in the fisherman’s net. A few children giggled. Marva let Anansi struggle theatrically, and they laughed harder. The familiar rhythm took her: spoon click, puppet rise, pause for laughter, turn the paper moon so it flashed.

La Diablesse came out. The blue-devil danced. The fisherman threw his net.

Beyond the cloth, the crowd murmured.

“Is this the one from online?”

“Wait till the boy comes.”

“My daughter show me that video.”

Marva heard every word.

She finished the fisherman’s story. She should have bowed then. Thanked them. Taken her crate and gone home.

Instead, a small hand appeared beneath the bottom edge of the screen.

A child had crawled under the stage.

Marva froze.

“Hey,” she whispered.

The child’s hand felt along the floor, searching. He had dropped something—perhaps a toy, perhaps the balloon string. His fingers closed around the black cloth.

The shadow at Marva’s feet shuddered.

“Let go,” Marva said, sharper now.

The little hand jerked back. There was a frightened squeak from the other side of the screen. Then a boy’s voice said, “Mummy?”

The crowd shifted. Someone called, “A child under there!”

Before Marva could reach him, the black cloth rose.

It rose between her hands, higher than it had ever risen. It opened wide, swallowing the light from the foot of the stage. On the white screen appeared Ravi’s shadow, not dancing now but crouched low.

The audience went silent.

A little shadow crawled toward him.

Marva could see the boy properly only by the shape he made on the cloth. He was tiny, kneeling beneath the stage, one hand pressed against his face. Ravi reached toward him.

The child’s mother screamed from the crowd.

Ravi took the boy’s hand.

Then, with no body beneath the screen and no place for either of them to stand, he led the child’s shadow sideways, through the painted shadow of the fisherman’s boat.

The boy emerged from behind the opposite side of the stage, clutching his yellow balloon.

The crowd erupted.

People shouted and clapped. A woman began crying. The mother rushed forward and scooped up her child, kissing his cheeks, his hair, his hands.

Phones lifted everywhere.

Marva stood behind the cloth, unable to breathe.

Ravi’s shadow remained on the screen. It had turned toward the audience.

Renée had taken off her sunglasses.

“Ravi?” she said.

Not loudly.

But Marva heard.

The shadow’s head lifted.

Renée stepped closer, one hand over her mouth. Her face had gone pale beneath her brown skin.

“Ravi Sookdeo,” she whispered. “Oh God.”

The cloth trembled.

Marva dropped Anansi. The puppet struck the stage boards with a dry little clack.

Outside, the crowd had begun chanting for the dancer.

“Again! Again! Again!”

Cleon’s voice came through the microphone, delighted and breathless. “You all seeing this? The famous shadow boy himself!”

He swung toward the screen.

Marva saw his phone in his hand.

“No,” she said.

He could not hear her over the crowd.

Ravi’s shadow began to fade at the edges. First his fingers thinned. Then the outline of his face softened, as though rain were falling over it.

Marva reached for the black cloth.

Ravi turned toward her.

For twenty-three years, she had seen him as he had been at seventeen: impatient, sly, beautiful, always half turned toward some music nobody else had heard. But now his shadow seemed older. Not old exactly. Only tired.

Beyond the screen, Renée was crying.

Marva understood then what Kamla had meant.

A dead person can get tired too.

She lifted the moon puppet in one hand.

With the other, she raised the black cloth as high as she could.

The audience thought it was part of the show.

Ravi took the moon from her. He rolled it over his shoulders once, twice. It became a fish, then a bird, then a drum, just as it always had.

Then he changed it into a road.

Not a real road. Only a shadow road, narrowing toward a place where the white cloth met darkness. He stood at its beginning, looking back.

Marva’s throat closed.

She could have called him.

She knew every name he had ever been given. Ravi. Ravesh. Little Rave. Mummy’s handsome boy. The nuisance. The one who came home late. The one who never learned to wear a helmet properly. The one whose shoes were still in the bottom of Kamla’s cupboard.

She said none of them.

Instead she clicked the spoons together.

Once.

Ravi smiled—not with a mouth, not exactly, but with the tilt of his head—and made the old gesture with his wrist, shaking invisible rain from his fingers.

Then he walked down the shadow road.

The white cloth brightened.

The black square fell to the floor.

For a moment, the audience kept clapping, believing there was another trick coming. Marva stood very still. Then she brought Anansi up from the boards and made him bow.

The children laughed.

That broke the spell. People cheered. Cleon hurried onto the stage, talking into his microphone about magic, tradition, wonder. Someone put a flower garland around Marva’s neck. Her tin cup filled with notes and coins.

Renée did not come to speak to her.

She stood at the edge of the crowd for a long time, holding her grandson’s hand. Then she touched two fingers to her lips and lifted them toward the stage before walking away.

At home that night, Kamla sat on the gallery waiting.

Marva placed the fish crate beside the door.

Her mother looked at it, then at Marva’s empty hands.

“He gone?” she asked.

Marva nodded.

Kamla closed her eyes.

For a while they listened to the frogs beginning in the drain and the far-off thud of music from somebody’s party. A breeze moved through the gallery. It smelled faintly of rain, though the sky was clear.

Finally Kamla said, “Come inside, girl. Your pelau getting cold.”

The next Wednesday, Marva set up on the Promenade again.

She stretched the white cloth between the bamboo poles. She arranged Anansi, La Diablesse, the fisherman, the blue-devil. She put the black square of cloth in the crate but did not take it out.

The girl in the yellow uniform came running from the pavement, though now she had two friends with her.

“Miss Marva!” she called. “You doing the boy story?”

Marva looked at the empty space behind the screen.

The afternoon light made a plain rectangle on the pavement. Nothing moved in it except the flutter of the cloth.

“No,” she said, picking up her spoons. “Today Anansi have plenty trouble.”
