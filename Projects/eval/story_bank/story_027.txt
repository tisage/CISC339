Detective Inspector Afi Mensah arrived at Lantern House while the sky over Jamestown was still the color of unpolished pewter. The sea was making its old argument with the rocks below the lighthouse. Gulls hung over the fishing canoes and the gutters smelled of last night’s rain, red earth, smoked fish, and diesel.

Lantern House had once been a cocoa warehouse, then a storage place for imported tiles, then nothing at all for twenty-two years except a home for cats and boys with nowhere to sleep. Now its face had been lime-washed white, its shutters painted a proud blue, its courtyard strung with small yellow bulbs. A banner sagged over the arched entrance:

THE DJAN FOUNDATION INAUGURAL SCHOLARSHIP EVENING  
“LIGHT FOR THE CHILD WHO READS”

Under the banner stood two constables, a sleepy photographer from the Daily Graphic, and a young woman in a gold headwrap weeping into a folded program.

Afi showed her warrant card. One constable straightened. “Morning, madam.”

“Who called it in?”

“His wife. Around eleven-forty last night.”

“Where is she?”

“In the library room. We put everybody there, like you said on phone.”

“Everybody who was here?”

“Those who did not run home before we locked the gates.”

Afi looked at him.

He swallowed. “Most of them, madam.”

Inside, Lantern House was all polished cement floors and fresh paint, the kind of renovation that left one old wall rough on purpose so donors could admire history without being troubled by it. The main hall still held the remains of the scholarship launch: plastic chairs in rows, a lectern with a microphone, wilted palm fronds, half-empty bottles of water, cups stained red from sobolo, the hibiscus drink everybody in Accra claimed to make better than everybody else.

A narrow corridor led to the back office. The air there was close and carried a sharp medicinal smell beneath the hibiscus and perfume. The body lay on the floor beside a carved desk. Nana Kwame Djan had been a big man in life—broad-chested, handsome in a way that endured into his sixties, white beard trimmed like a retired chief’s. In death he seemed smaller, one hand curled under his chin, the other stretched toward a leather satchel knocked on its side.

Afi crouched. His lips were swollen, his eyes half-open. A faint rash had risen along his neck.

Dr. Sarpong, the police pathologist, was already there, tying his shoe with irritation as if death had interrupted him on his way to something better.

“Anaphylaxis, most likely,” he said. “Severe allergic reaction. The throat closed. He also struck his head on the desk when he fell, but that did not kill him.”

“Allergic to what?”

“The wife says groundnuts. Peanut, as the Americans say. Very severe. He carried adrenaline pens.”

Afi glanced at the satchel. “Where are they?”

Dr. Sarpong shrugged. “Not in the bag. Not on him. Wife says he never went anywhere without two.”

“Could he have forgotten them?”

“Could I forget my own spectacles and still do my work?” Dr. Sarpong tapped the bridge of his nose. “Possible. Unlikely.”

Afi put on gloves and opened the satchel. Inside: reading glasses, an envelope of speech notes, a phone, a packet of antacids, a small Bible with a cracked spine, keys, breath mints, a fountain pen. There was an elastic loop sewn into the side, empty.

On the desk sat a stainless-steel tumbler with a black lid. Afi unscrewed it and sniffed. Sobolo, ginger, cloves, sugar. Something earthy too, faint but present, like the powder left at the bottom of a bag of roasted groundnuts.

Behind her, someone made a sound.

Afi turned. A woman stood in the doorway with a constable’s hand hovering near her elbow. She was in her late fifties, tall and very straight, wrapped in black-and-white cloth too formal for morning. Her face had the drained, stunned look of a person still obeying manners because grief had not yet shown her what else to do.

“Mrs. Djan?” Afi asked.

“Abena Djan.”

“I’m Detective Inspector Mensah.”

The woman nodded. “He did not forget his EpiPens.”

“You are sure?”

“My husband forgot birthdays, receipts, promises. Never those. His first attack nearly killed him in London. After that, he kept one in his bag, one in his car, one in his office drawer.”

Afi looked at the drawer. It had been pulled open. Inside were stationery, a spare phone charger, old envelopes, a tin of peppermints. No EpiPen.

“Who knew where he kept them?”

“Everyone close to him.”

“That is a long list?”

Mrs. Djan’s mouth tightened. “Kwame liked people to feel close.”

In the library room, eight witnesses sat among shelves of new books that still smelled of glue. They looked like students awaiting exam results. Afi stood in the doorway and watched them before speaking.

There was Abena Djan, the widow, hands folded over her cloth. Beside her sat Kweku Djan, the dead man’s younger brother, sweating through a linen shirt. On a low stool near the window, Hajia Rukaya Suleman, the caterer, murmured prayers under her breath, her hennaed fingers worrying a string of amber beads. The security guard, Nii Aryee, leaned against the wall, trying to look invisible in a uniform one size too large. Sika Amarteifio, a poet with copper braids and silver rings on every finger, stared defiantly at the ceiling. A young foundation volunteer named Eyram Agbeko clutched her phone as if it might testify for her. Lastly, near the children’s shelf, sat Adjoa Korankye, the foundation manager, a compact woman in a navy dress, eyes red-rimmed but dry.

Afi introduced herself. Her voice was calm, neither gentle nor harsh. Her father, a fisherman from Anloga, had always said a net tears when you throw it in anger. Her mother, an Akuapem trader, had said anger was useful only if it knew how to count.

“I will speak to each of you separately,” Afi said. “Until then, do not discuss your statements. If you need water, ask the constable. If you need to use the toilet, ask the constable. If you think of leaving, think again.”

Kweku Djan lifted a hand. “Inspector, are you saying my brother was murdered?”

“I am saying your brother is dead and the things that could have saved him are missing.”

Silence settled.

Afi began with the widow.

Abena Djan sat opposite her in the small reading alcove. Morning light came through blue shutters and striped her face.

“Tell me last night from the beginning,” Afi said.

“The program began at seven. Speeches. A choir from Korle Gonno. Kwame spoke at eight-fifteen. After that they served refreshments. Around nine, the lights went out for some minutes. The generator came on, then off, then on properly. People laughed. Kwame disliked disorder. He went to his office. I thought he was taking a call.”

“When did you last see him alive?”

“Just before the blackout. He was near the sobolo table with Adjoa and that girl, Eyram.”

“Where were you during the blackout?”

“In the prayer room.” Her gaze did not move. “I needed quiet.”

“How long?”

“Ten minutes. Maybe fifteen.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

Afi made a note. “And when did you find him?”

“After the awards. He was supposed to give closing remarks at ten-thirty. Adjoa said perhaps he was resting. I went to call him. The door was not locked.”

“Who entered before you?”

“No one. I screamed.”

Afi looked up. “Mrs. Djan, did your husband have enemies?”

Abena laughed once, without humor. “He had admirers, debtors, dependents, women who thought he understood them, men who thought he owed them, and children he had promised to help. Choose your word.”

“What about you?”

“Me?”

“Did he owe you anything?”

The widow’s eyes sharpened. “Twenty-nine years.”

Next came Kweku Djan. He smelled of expensive cologne fighting a losing battle against fear.

“My brother was a great man,” he said before Afi asked anything. “Too generous, even. That is why people took advantage.”

“Including you?”

His face folded. “Inspector?”

“Where were you when the lights went out?”

“At the front gate. I went to take a call from my driver.”

“Your driver’s name?”

“Yaw.”

“Number?”

“My phone battery died afterward.”

“Then we will charge it.”

He looked toward the door. “Fine. Fine, yes.”

“You and your brother argued last week.”

“Families argue. Our father had one temper and shared it between us.”

“About what?”

“Land in Prampram. Nothing serious.”

“Witnesses heard you tell him, ‘You will regret disgracing me.’”

Kweku wiped his face. “He wanted to remove me from a company account. I had made some mistakes. Business mistakes. Not killing mistakes.”

Afi let silence do its work.

He sagged. “I was not calling my driver. I was speaking to someone I owe money. Betting. Small, small it became large. If my wife hears, Inspector, I beg you. But I did not go near Kwame’s office.”

“How much do you owe?”

He whispered the sum.

Afi wrote it down. It was not small.

Hajia Rukaya entered with dignity, though her hands shook.

“I told them,” she said, settling heavily into the chair. “No groundnuts. I have catered for Nana Djan three times. I know. I even used separate spoons.”

“Who prepared the sobolo?”

“My girls and I. Hibiscus, ginger, pineapple, cloves. Sugar. No groundnut. Who puts groundnut in sobolo?”

“Did Nana Djan drink from the general table?”

“He had his own tumbler. Madam Adjoa brought it to us to fill before the program.”

“Adjoa Korankye?”

“Yes. She said, ‘Hajia, fill this one first. No mistake.’ I washed my hands. I filled it myself.”

“Did it leave your sight?”

“Only when Eyram came to collect it.”

“During the blackout, where were you?”

“In the kitchen area. Ask anybody.”

“Did you leave the kitchen?”

“No.”

Afi looked at her.

The caterer looked back.

“Hajia,” Afi said, “if I ask your girls, they will tell me the same?”

The beads moved faster through her fingers. “Inspector, business is hard.”

“Did you use groundnut oil in any dish?”

“Not for him! For the kelewele sauce only. It was cheaper. I bought pure vegetable oil, but the boy delivered groundnut oil and time was going. Nana did not eat. He never eats at events.”

“You lied because you feared blame.”

“I lied because people hear ‘groundnut’ and stop thinking.” Her eyes filled. “I did not kill him.”

Afi believed her, which meant nothing yet.

Nii Aryee, the security guard, gave his answers like a schoolboy reciting a lesson he had not learned well.

“The lights went off because ECG took it,” he said.

“In this area, the whole street had power.”

“Then the generator—maybe it coughed.”

“Generators do not cough for six minutes unless someone teaches them.”

His ears reddened.

“Where were you during the blackout?”

“At the gate.”

“The gate camera shows no one there.”

He swallowed. “Camera was off.”

“Before it went off.”

His eyes slid away.

“Nii.”

He rubbed his palms on his trousers. “Madam, I only tapped small current. For the cold store woman next door. She pays me something. My mother is sick. The generator overloaded when the program lights and her freezer were both on. I ran to disconnect. That is all. If Mr. Djan knew, he would sack me.”

“Did you see anyone near the office corridor?”

“It was dark.”

“Did you hear anything?”

“People shouting. The choir girls laughing. Someone crying maybe.”

“Crying?”

“Or singing. These poets, you know.”

Sika Amarteifio objected to being questioned before she even sat down.

“I hope you understand how traumatic this is,” she said. “I came to perform, not to be trapped like a thief.”

Afi opened her notebook. “Where were you during the blackout?”

“In the green room, rehearsing.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

“What did you rehearse?”

“My piece.”

“Recite it.”

Sika blinked. “What?”

“The piece you rehearsed.”

“It is not a circus trick.”

“No. It is a murder investigation.”

The poet’s mouth tightened. Then she began, voice low and beautiful despite herself:

“We have built lamps from the bones of our mothers,  
called it progress, called it—”

She stopped. “I was not in the green room the whole time.”

“Where were you?”

“In the archive upstairs.”

“Doing what?”

“Looking.”

“At?”

Sika stared at her rings. “There is a first edition of Ayi Kwei Armah’s essays in a glass case. The latch is loose. I intended to borrow it.”

“Borrow.”

“Return later.”

“Did you?”

“No. The lights went off and I knocked over something. I panicked and came down.”

“Did you pass the office?”

“No.”

“See anyone?”

“A woman in navy. Or black. Near the stairs. But half the women in Accra wear navy when they want to look responsible.”

Adjoa Korankye wore navy.

Eyram Agbeko came next. She was twenty-three, perhaps, with large glasses and the intense exhaustion of the underpaid. She kept unlocking her phone, locking it again.

“You collected Mr. Djan’s tumbler from Hajia,” Afi said.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Before his speech. Around eight.”

“And gave it to him?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“At the side of the stage.”

“Did anyone touch it between the kitchen and the stage?”

“No.”

“Did you leave it unattended?”

“No.”

“Eyram.”

The girl’s chin trembled. “I put it down one time.”

“Where?”

“On the donation table. Only for a minute. Madam Adjoa called me to bring programs. But there were people everywhere.”

“Who was near it?”

“I don’t know. Donors, volunteers.”

“Why did you not say that before?”

“Because Madam Adjoa said I must be precise. She said police like blaming small people.” Eyram’s tears came quickly and silently. “Also, I left early after the awards. Before they found him. I climbed the wall at the back.”

“Why?”

“My mother thinks I am doing national service at a hospital. She does not like this foundation work. She says rich men use girls to polish their names. I was supposed to be home by ten.”

Afi leaned back. “Did Nana Djan ever trouble you?”

“No. He barely saw me.”

“But Adjoa?”

Eyram hesitated. “She sees everything.”

When Adjoa Korankye entered, she closed the door herself.

“Detective, I know how this looks,” she said.

“How does it look?”

“As if everyone is hiding foolishness while Kwame lies dead.”

Afi almost smiled. “Are you hiding foolishness?”

Adjoa’s expression did not change. “I am hiding grief. People confuse the two.”

She spoke clearly. She had joined the foundation twelve years earlier, after Nana Djan paid her university fees through a scholarship. She rose from receptionist to programs director to manager. She knew his allergies, his habits, his moods.

“Who had access to his office EpiPen?”

“Staff. But the office was busy all week. Painters, electricians, volunteers.”

“And his bag?”

“He carried it himself.”

“Except when he didn’t.”

Adjoa looked at the closed notebook on Afi’s lap. “Except when he didn’t.”

“Did you bring his tumbler to the caterer?”

“Yes. I did not trust open cups.”

“Who else knew that tumbler was his?”

“Everyone who saw him use it. It has his initials.”

“Where were you during the blackout?”

“With the scholarship children in the hall. Some of them were frightened.”

“Which children?”

Adjoa named three.

Afi had spoken to the constables guarding the witnesses; none of those children remained at Lantern House. Their parents had taken them home before the police sealed the place.

“After the blackout?”

“I looked for Kwame. He said he needed five minutes. Later, when he did not return, Mrs. Djan found him.”

“Did you enter his office last night?”

“No.”

“Not at all?”

“No.”

“Did you drink last night?”

For the first time, something flickered.

“Water.”

“Only water?”

“I do not drink alcohol.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Adjoa’s jaw tightened. “Inspector, if you want to ask whether I killed the man who changed my life, ask it.”

“Did you kill Nana Kwame Djan?”

“No.”

Afi watched her. Adjoa watched back.

By midday, the lies lay across the case like tangled fishing line.

Kweku had no driver call, but phone records showed him outside near the gate speaking for seven minutes to a loan shark in Tema. Hajia had lied about groundnut oil, but no trace of her sauce had touched the dead man’s mouth. Nii had caused the blackout through his illegal connection but had remained at the generator shed, where grease on his sleeves and a neighbor’s statement placed him. Sika had gone upstairs to steal a book and had seen someone in dark clothes. Eyram had left the tumbler unattended on a donation table for less than two minutes, then lied out of fear and obedience.

Abena Djan’s prayer-room story broke at two o’clock.

Afi found the prayer room empty except for two plastic chairs, a dusty crucifix, and a window whose sill bore the clean scrape of recent use. Outside, the window opened onto a narrow passage beside the building. A torn scrap of black-and-white cloth clung to a nail.

Abena looked at it when Afi placed it on the table between them, then closed her eyes.

“I met someone,” she said.

“Who?”

“My lawyer.”

“At a scholarship launch?”

“She would be traveling today. Kwame watched my movements. Public events were the only times I had privacy.” Abena’s voice had gone flat. “I was filing for divorce.”

“Your lawyer will confirm?”

“She will. And she will be furious.”

“Why divorce now?”

Abena looked toward the shelves of donated books. “Because at my age, if you do not leave a burning room, people begin to call the smoke your perfume.”

“Did your husband know?”

“He suspected. That was enough for him.”

“Did you take his EpiPens?”

“No.”

“Did you put groundnut in his drink?”

Abena’s face twisted. “If I wanted Kwame dead, Inspector, I had twenty-nine years to become clever.”

Afi believed her too, which irritated her. Belief was a lazy tool unless sharpened by proof.

The proof began with a child’s drawing.

One of the scholarship recipients, a nine-year-old from Chorkor named Kojo, had left behind a paper folder of colored-pencil sketches. Afi found it under a chair in the main hall while retracing the evening’s movement. Most drawings showed the lighthouse, the stage, Nana Djan with an oversized beard. One showed the sobolo table: red bowl, stacks of cups, a girl with glasses, a woman with blue beads, and a black tumbler marked with two yellow letters.

KD.

Beside the tumbler, in the child’s uneven perspective, was a hand. The person attached to the hand had been cut off by the edge of the page, but the wrist wore a bracelet of blue beads.

Afi stared at it.

Hajia had amber beads. Sika wore silver rings. Abena wore gold bangles. Eyram wore no jewelry. Adjoa, in the library that morning, wore no bracelet.

But in photographs from the event—pulled from the foundation’s own social media page—Adjoa Korankye stood beside Nana Djan wearing a bracelet of blue glass beads.

At three-fifteen, Afi asked a constable to bring Adjoa back in.

The foundation manager entered without the bracelet.

“Where are your beads?” Afi asked.

“My beads?”

“The blue bracelet you wore last night.”

“I don’t know. Maybe at home.”

Afi showed her the child’s drawing.

For a long moment, Adjoa said nothing. Then she laughed softly. “Inspector, a child drew a hand near a cup. That is your case?”

“No. It is a hand near the cup Eyram left unattended. It is the bracelet you removed before I interviewed you. It is also the empty elastic loop in Mr. Djan’s satchel and the missing spare from his office.”

Adjoa sat down. “Many people wore beads.”

Afi placed an evidence bag on the table. Inside was a small blue glass bead.

“I found this behind the office desk, near the drawer. It broke off something. Perhaps when someone pulled the drawer open in a hurry.”

Adjoa’s face remained controlled, but her eyes moved once to the bag and back.

“Your office is next to his,” Afi said. “Your fingerprints belong everywhere, so they are not enough. You knew he would drink only from his tumbler. You also knew that if his reaction began, he would reach first for his bag, then for the office drawer.”

“You have imagination.”

“I have time. And I have sent officers to search the archive upstairs.”

That landed. A small tightening around the mouth.

“What will they find there, Adjoa?”

“Old books. Rat droppings. A poet’s guilt.”

“And?”

Silence.

Afi leaned forward. “You told me you do not drink alcohol. That was a lie. I could smell it on you this morning, not fresh, but in your sweat. I did not care. People survive how they survive. But Sika saw a woman in navy near the stairs during the blackout. Not in the hall with children. Upstairs.”

Adjoa looked at the shuttered window.

“What were you doing in the archive?”

When she spoke, her voice was lower. “There is a cabinet behind the colonial ledgers. I keep a bottle there.”

“What else did you keep there last night?”

No answer.

A constable knocked and entered with another evidence bag. Inside lay two adrenaline auto-injectors and a small empty sachet with red lettering: ROASTED GROUNDNUT POWDER—FOR SOUP.

The constable said, “Madam, found behind old files in upstairs archive. Also one half-bottle of gin.”

Adjoa closed her eyes briefly.

Afi dismissed the constable.

Outside, the hall had grown noisy with reporters pressing against the gate, with relatives arriving, with the city continuing its business around one dead man.

“Tell me,” Afi said.

Adjoa opened her eyes. They had lost their redness and become very clear. “He was going to close the scholarship.”

Afi waited.

“Not publicly. Publicly, he was expanding it. Cameras, speeches, the children singing. Privately, he had signed papers to transfer most of the foundation funds into a new development company. Lantern House would remain as a symbol. A nice hall for events. The actual money would go into apartments at Labadi. He said scholarships make good stories but property makes legacy.”

“Is that why you killed him?”

Adjoa’s mouth trembled once, then stilled. “He found the other account.”

“What other account?”

“I moved money. Not to myself.” She said it quickly, fiercely. “To students he had rejected. Girls who were pregnant. Boys with bad grades because they were working nights. Children who did not photograph well. He wanted perfect suffering—clean uniforms, bright eyes, mothers who cried on cue.”

“How much?”

“Enough for prison.”

“So when he found out—”

“He said I had embarrassed him. He said he would hand me to the police after the launch. Smile tonight, Adjoa. Tomorrow you answer for yourself.”

She laughed once, and it was an ugly, broken sound. “I smiled. I arranged his microphone. I filled his tumbler.”

“With groundnut powder.”

“Not at first.”

Afi frowned.

Adjoa rubbed her forehead. “During the blackout, I went upstairs. I drank. I thought I would run. Then I came down and saw his tumbler on the donation table. Just sitting there, as if even God was tired of him. I had bought the powder weeks ago for a cooking demonstration in Nima. It was in my handbag. I don’t know why I still had it.”

“You put it in.”

“Yes.”

“And the EpiPens?”

“I took the one from his bag earlier, when he was greeting donors. I don’t know why. Anger. Fear. I took the office one after he went in. He was already coughing. He looked at me like…” She swallowed. “He said, ‘Adjoa, help me.’”

Afi’s pen stopped.

“I opened the drawer,” Adjoa said. “He thought I was helping. I took it and left.”

For a few seconds the room held only the sea’s distant roar and the muffled voices beyond the wall.

“Where were you when Mrs. Djan found him?”

“In the hall.” Adjoa looked down at her bare wrist. “Clapping for the children.”

Afi closed her notebook.

When she told Abena Djan, the widow sat very still.

“Adjoa?” she asked.

“Yes.”

Abena turned the name over in her mind as if it belonged to some language she did not speak. Then she said, “Kwame paid her school fees.”

Afi did not answer.

“He told that story everywhere.”

“Yes.”

Abena looked toward the office corridor. “He never told people she worked for the money afterward.”

Outside Lantern House, afternoon had burned the clouds away. The reporters surged when Adjoa was led out, cameras lifted, questions flying in English, Twi, Ga, all colliding into one hungry noise. Adjoa kept her head high until she reached the police vehicle. Then, just before ducking inside, she looked back at the building.

Not at the banner, not at the donors clustered in shocked groups, not at Abena Djan in her widow’s cloth.

At the children’s drawings taped crookedly along the hall window.

Afi watched the vehicle pull away.

Nii Aryee hovered near the gate, cap in hand. “Madam Inspector,” he said, “about the generator—”

“Someone from the district office will speak to you.”

His shoulders fell.

“And Nii,” she added, “tell the cold store woman to buy her own meter.”

He nodded miserably.

Hajia Rukaya was packing her trays with violent efficiency, muttering that she would never cater for rich people again, not even if the president himself was chewing air. Sika Amarteifio slipped the first edition book onto a table when she thought no one was looking. Kweku Djan stood under a palm decoration, whispering into a recharged phone, probably making a bad situation worse. Eyram Agbeko sat on the steps, typing a message and deleting it, typing again.

Everyone had told the truth eventually, or some portion of it. The truth had come out dented, reluctant, smelling of gin, fear, oil, debt, and old paper.

Afi stepped into the courtyard. The yellow bulbs looked foolish in daylight. Above them, the lighthouse stood white and patient, facing the sea as it had through storms, festivals, elections, funerals, and all the private lies people carried through public doors.

Her phone buzzed. Dr. Sarpong.

“Yes?” she answered.

“Preliminary test confirms peanut protein in the tumbler,” he said. “A lot of it. Whoever did it wanted no ambiguity.”

“There rarely is, after people finish making ambiguity.”

“What?”

“Nothing. Send me the report.”

She ended the call.

At the gate, a small boy in a school uniform peered through the bars. He was not one of the scholarship children, just a neighborhood child drawn by police vehicles and adult distress.

“Madam,” he called, “is the library closed?”

Afi looked back at Lantern House. Constables moved through the hall. A dead man’s face smiled from the banner. Somewhere inside, new books waited on new shelves.

“For today,” she said.

The boy considered this with the seriousness of a judge.

“Tomorrow?”

Afi put on her sunglasses. “Come and see.”
