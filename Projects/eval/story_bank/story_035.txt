The bus put him down at the Moga stand a little after five, in a gray light that smelled of diesel and wet tea leaves. Gurnam Singh stood with his trunk at his feet and his rucksack on his shoulder and waited for somebody to see him.

Nobody did. A rickshaw-wallah called out, and he shook his head. He was wearing civilian clothes, a blue shirt Pammi had sent him two winters ago and a pair of grey trousers that had come out of the trunk creased in squares. He had imagined, on the long train from Pathankot, that he would be recognised even so. He had imagined a lot of things on that train, and had been ashamed of most of them.

His left hand was in his pocket. He took it out and held the trunk handle with it, the three fingers and the thumb closing around the metal, then put it back in.

He had called from Ambala the night before. Pammi had said, "Come, come. I'll send Gaggu to the stand." Her voice had been far away and quick, with the clatter of steel behind it. Gaggu was his brother's boy, seventeen now, and had been a child with a runny nose when Gurnam left. At half past five a boy on a black Splendor came through the gate of the stand with white wires hanging from his ears. He looked at the faces and then at Gurnam, and his face did a small sum.

"Chacha ji?" He pulled the wires out. "Sat sri akal. I'm sorry. I thought the bus would be later."

"It was on time."

"It's never on time." Gaggu took the trunk from him with both hands, and Gurnam let him. The boy tied it on the back of the scooter with a rubber tube cut from a tyre, and Gurnam sat behind him with the rucksack in his lap. They rode out past the shuttered cloth shops and the new yellow flyover. Gurnam did not remember a flyover.

"When was that built?"

"Which? Oh. Two years. Three."

The wheat was standing yellow in the fields beyond the town, and the harvest had begun. A combine sat at the edge of one plot with its jaws open like a sleeping animal. Gurnam looked at the stubble burning in the distance, thin lines of smoke leaning in the same direction, and breathed through his nose. For two years the air had been so thin and cold that it had felt like something he had to earn with each breath. Now it was thick, warm, and full of dust and dung smoke. He was surprised to find it did not feel like his.

---

The gate had been painted green. It had been rust-coloured iron with his father's initials welded into the top bar, and now it was green with a steel nameplate that said *S. Joginder Singh Residence* in English. He read it twice. His father had been dead fourteen months.

Inside, the neem tree was still there, but the place beneath it where his father's manji had stood, where the old man had spent every afternoon of Gurnam's life, held a white Alto with a cloth over its windscreen. Beyond it a wall had gone up through the middle of the courtyard. It was only brick and cement, not yet plastered, but it was a wall, and it ran from the verandah to the back fence. On the far side of it a woman was shouting at a child.

"Tarsem Chacha's side," Gaggu said, not looking at him. "Last Diwali. It was just for the, you know. The water, and the meter. Two bills."

"Yes," Gurnam said.

The kitchen door on his side stood open, and the light inside was on. He could hear a pressure cooker venting and the *tak-tak-tak* of a knife on a board. He stepped up onto the verandah and stood in the doorway.

There were four women in the kitchen. Two of them sat on the floor cutting onions into a big aluminium basin. One stood at the stove. At the counter, in a printed salwar suit with her hair twisted up and a green apron over it, Pammi was lining up steel dabbas in rows on a long tray, snapping lids onto them, forty or fifty, one after another. Each had a strip of masking tape with a name or a number. She was moving fast and not looking at her hands.

She looked up, and her face opened.

"Oh," she said. "You're here already."

She put down the dabba. She came across the kitchen, wiping her hands on the apron, and put her arms around him, and he put his right arm around her and kept his left hand low against his trouser leg. She smelled of cumin and steam and the coconut oil she had always used. Her head came up to his collarbone, as it always had. She held on for about as long as it takes to count five.

"You've gotten thin," she said into his shirt. "Look at you. Did they not feed you?"

"They fed us."

"Sit, sit. Rano, give him tea. No, I'll do it." She did not. She turned back to the counter and said over her shoulder, "Sit down, I have to get these on the bike by eight, Lakhwinder's coming. It's the bank staff, forty-two boxes. Sit anywhere."

There was a stool by the window. He sat on it. The two women on the floor had put their heads down and gone on cutting onions with great concentration, the way people do when a husband is in the room. The one at the stove raised a spoon to him, and he nodded.

He looked around the kitchen where his mother had once cooked for a joint family of eleven. He knew the shape of it, the window and the floor, and nothing else. The old mud chulha had been bricked over and tiled. There was a steel rack with a hundred dabbas hung on hooks in graduated sizes. A calendar from a spice company hung beside a chalkboard on which someone had written in three kinds of handwriting: *Rajni 6 veg. Bank 42. Dr Sandhu 2 no onion.* When had she started? She had written about it once, he thought, in a letter. *A few ladies from the street asked me to make for them.* He had been in a bunker with ice in his beard. He had read it and thought, good, a little pocket money.

"Where's Simran?" he said.

"School." Pammi didn't turn around. "It's Tuesday. She had a test today, mathematics. I told her you were coming, but she said Miss will give zero if she misses it. She's very serious about her marks."

"It's all right."

"She'll be back at two. Gaggu will fetch her."

"I could go."

"Oh." The lid in her hand paused. "She doesn't know you. I mean, she knows you, but at the gate she'd see a man she doesn't know. Better Gaggu."

"Yes," he said. "Yes. Better."

---

The tea came in a cup he did not recognise, thin white china with a gold rim. It was set on the sill by his elbow by one of the women, who then withdrew almost to the door. He drank it. The sweetness was exactly right.

At nine the kitchen emptied. A young man came for the boxes and went off with them on a carrier bike, humming. The women washed their hands and left, calling out *Sat sri akal, Bhaji,* in voices that curved shyly around the doorframe. Pammi untied the apron and hung it on a nail, and for the first time since he had come she stood in front of him with nothing in her hands.

"Come," she said. "I'll show you the room."

The room was where it had always been, at the end of the verandah. The bed was the same bed, with the carved headboard his father had bought them on their wedding. But the left wall had a long table against it with a Usha sewing machine and rolls of fabric in plastic. There were cardboard cartons labelled in marker. The almirah where his uniforms had hung held her suits now, in clear plastic covers. His clothes were in the trunk Gaggu had carried in. That was where, she explained, with a gesture that seemed to apologise to the room and not to him, she had put his things. He had written on the trunk himself, in white paint, years ago: NK GURNAM SINGH 14 SIKH LI.

"I do some stitching on the side," she said. "Blouses, school uniforms. It's seasonal. You can put the trunk under the bed, I've cleared space. Or in the store, if you like. The store is empty now." She stopped. "That is, Tarsem's things are not in it any more."

"This is fine."

"I didn't know exactly what you'd want."

"It's fine, Pammi."

She looked at the floor between them. Then she reached out and took his left hand out of his pocket, and held it up between both of hers, and turned it over. She had seen the photograph, the one the medical officer had sent with the letter. Photographs don't have weight. She weighed it now, the missing ring finger and the missing little finger, the pink knot of skin where they used to be.

"Does it hurt?"

"No. It itches sometimes. The ones that are gone. I get an itch in a finger that's not there."

"How do you scratch it?"

"You can't." He nearly smiled. "You scratch the one next to it, and sometimes that helps."

She nodded slowly and held on a little longer. Then she let go, and something went across her face that he could not read. It might have been tenderness. It might have been the look of a woman checking a seam.

"You must be tired," she said. "Lie down. I'll wake you before Simran comes."

He lay down on the bed in his clothes. He did not expect to sleep and he did not. He lay with his eyes open and listened. The inverter in the corner clicked and chirped. A mixer ground something in the next house. Through the wall his brother's wife was calling for someone named Honey. A scooter went by. A bell rang in the gurdwara and a tape of kirtan began, then stopped, then began again. Each sound sat separately in the air, distinct and without direction, and his body kept trying to decide which of them was a threat. He lay very still, and each time the answer was no, no, no, and each time it cost him something.

---

His mother lived on Tarsem's side. This he learned at noon, when he went around the wall through a gap left for the buffalo, which was also gone. The old woman was sitting on a plastic chair on the other verandah with a shawl around her shoulders despite the heat, shelling peas into her lap. She looked up and her mouth worked.

"Puttar."

He bent and touched her feet, and she put her palm on his head, and for a long while she did not say anything. Then she said, "You're so dark."

"The sun up there."

"Your father asked for you at the end."

He took a breath. "I know, Bibi ji. They told me. They told me at the post. The weather was—"

"He said, *where's Gurnam,* and I said, *he's coming.* He said, *tell him the roof tank leaks.*" She laughed, a dry rattle. "That was all. Not a word about anything else. The roof tank."

"I'll look at it."

"Tarsem fixed it in the monsoon." She went back to the peas. "You mustn't mind. Everything is done. When your father went there was so much to be done, you know, the bhog, the people, the money for the hospital. Tarsem did it all. He ran about. Your money came, thanks to Waheguru, every month, and we paid. So that's finished. We owe nobody."

"Good."

"You must eat on this side tonight. Balbir is making rajma."

"Pammi will have made something."

"Pammi doesn't cook for the house, she cooks for her customers." She said it with no malice, as a fact about weather. "She's a clever girl. Doing so well. She eats with us when she remembers."

He looked at his mother's hands, which had grown small, the knuckles round as walnuts. "And you, Bibi ji? You're well?"

"I'm well. I'm old. Honey's school is making a play and I have to embroider a cloth for it." She held up a peacock-blue square with a half-finished bird. "Sit, sit, why do you stand like a visitor?"

He sat on the step. A girl of about four peered around a door at him, then ran. He realised she was Tarsem's youngest, whom he had never met, born in the second year.

Tarsem himself came home at one, from the cooperative bank where he had a clerk's job, and shook Gurnam's hand in both of his and then embraced him hard, thumping his back. He was heavier than he had been, with a moustache in the new fashion, waxed and turned. His eyes were wet. He talked fast: the harvest, the price at the mandi, the new combine he'd hired by the hour. "You don't need to worry about the land," he said. "I've arranged everything. Sit, I'll tell you."

The two acres Gurnam's father had left them were leased to a contractor named Brar. Tarsem had borrowed against the lease to dig the tubewell and had put in the pipes, "and so the share is, you understand, it's a little complicated, we'll sit with a pen and paper." He wasn't looking at Gurnam's eyes. He was looking at his shoulder. "But you've got your pension and the lump sum, haven't you, from the Army. You don't need to farm. You have a good name there. Retired as a Naik, no?"

"I haven't retired. I've taken a discharge on medical grounds. It's different."

"Same thing. You're free now, you can sit and eat." Tarsem slapped his knee. "We'll find you something. The Cooperative needs a night watchman, maybe."

"A watchman."

"A responsible man. Day job's no good for you, you need to rest. A fauji with that kind of discipline—" He saw Gurnam's face and stopped, then went on in a softer voice. "I only mean you've done enough, bhai. Enough for ten lives. Let others do the carrying now."

Gurnam thanked him, because there was no other thing to do. He ate the rajma at his brother's table, and the child Honey put her hands over her eyes every time he lifted his fork, to hide from him, and her mother said "Beta, don't," with a laugh.

---

Simran came at a quarter past two.

He was in the courtyard when the Splendor stopped at the gate. He had washed his face and put on a clean shirt and had the thought that he should have a gift. He had in fact bought one, in Pathankot, a little steel pencil box with a tiger, and it was in the rucksack, and the rucksack was inside, and he found that he could not make his legs move to go and get it.

A girl got off the pillion, small and straight-backed in a navy pinafore and a white shirt, with a braid down her back and a bag too big for her. She had Pammi's chin. She stopped three steps inside the gate.

He had seen her on his one leave, three years ago, when she was five and wept at his beard. Then there had been the video calls, on a phone borrowed from a Havildar whose wife knew how. A little face going in and out of the screen, and his own voice shouting *Can you hear me, Simran, can you hear me?* over the wind. In those calls she had called him Papa. She had said it easily, as a word she had been given to say.

"Sat sri akal," she said now. She joined her hands.

"Sat sri akal, beta."

"Mummy said you're here." She looked at his face with great seriousness, as though comparing it against a drawing. "You're taller."

"Am I?"

"On the phone you're small."

"Ah." He laughed. It came out odd. "That's the phone."

She looked at his left hand. He felt it, and did not put it away this time. She said, "Is that where the cold got you?"

"Yes."

"Mummy said it was frostbite. I looked it up. It's when the blood stops going to the fingers because it's too cold and the fingers die. It's like a plant." She said it in a level, informative voice, and he realised she was frightened and had decided to be a scientist about it. "Did you see it happen?"

"I didn't see. I felt it, and then I couldn't feel. A doctor took them off in a tent."

"Did it hurt?"

"After. Not then."

She nodded as if this confirmed something. Then she said, "I have to change. Then I do my homework, then Mummy gives me milk. After that I can go on the roof. You can come if you want."

"I'd like that."

"Gaggu Bhaiya says you were a sniper."

"No. I was a signaller, mostly. I carried the radio."

"Oh." She seemed disappointed, then politely hid it. "That's good too."

She went inside. He heard her call, *Mummy, I'm back,* and he heard Pammi answer from the kitchen, and a drawer open, and the ordinary noise of a household that had a shape and ran on it. He stood on the dust of the courtyard for a while with his hands at his sides.

---

The roof was Simran's, he learned. She had a corner of it with a plastic mat, a tin of chalk, a row of potted marigolds she had labelled with ice-cream sticks, and a small telescope made of cardboard tube. She showed him where she watched the planes go over toward Adampur, and how she tracked them with a chalk line on the parapet. There were forty or fifty lines, with dates beside them. Her father's old plain world had been a thin place high in the mountains where there were no marigolds, and he held the cardboard tube to his eye and looked through it at a sky with nothing in it, and said, "I see one."

"You don't. It's not time."

"I see the hole where it will be."

She giggled once and stopped herself, as if it had slipped out.

After a minute she said, "Bhua says you'll live in the store now."

"Which Bhua?"

"Balbir Chachi. She's not a Bhua. Honey calls her that." She frowned. "Mummy says we're going to Moga in June."

He lowered the tube. "To Moga?"

"I got into Sacred Heart. It's a big school. You have to take a test and I did, and I'm in. Mummy found a shop near it to cook in, and upstairs we have two rooms. I'll have my own bed." She traced a chalk line with her shoe. "She didn't tell you? She said she'd tell you."

"She's going to tell me."

"Okay." She looked relieved. "I thought she'd forgotten."

---

He did not sleep that night, either. At one o'clock he got up and went out and sat on the verandah step. The courtyard was the colour of tin in the moonlight, the wall a pale stripe across it, the Alto a low shape under its cloth. He sat with his bare feet on the cool cement. There was a sound he could not place, high and regular, and he listened to it for a long time before he understood it was a cricket. He had forgotten crickets.

After a while the door opened behind him.

"You'll catch cold," Pammi said.

"It's April."

"You say that. Your body is used to something else." She came and sat on the step above him, a shawl around her shoulders, and put her feet next to his. They were small and strong, with silver toe rings. He had bought those rings in Ludhiana the week before the wedding.

For a while neither spoke.

"You were going to tell me about Moga," he said.

"Simran told you."

"She said she thought you'd forgotten."

Pammi breathed out through her nose. "I didn't want to say it on the phone. And then you were here, and I thought, tonight. Then I thought, tomorrow."

"It's tomorrow."

"I paid the deposit in February. Forty thousand. The shop is on Dhilon Road, a double-shutter. The landlord's a decent man. There's a gas connection, a proper exhaust." She spoke carefully, laying each item down. "Rano and Surjit are coming with me. I have eleven regular contracts and three more waiting. I make more now than the pension would be. Do you understand? It's not that I want to say it like that."

"I understand."

"I can't stay on this side of the wall, Gurnam. Your mother is good to me but I'm her daughter-in-law who isn't Balbir. Every day I walk through a house where the stove is in the wrong place and someone else's child is shouting. I started the kitchen to pay for Simran's tuition, and it grew, and I thought, if I leave, I'll have something that is mine." She wiped her cheek with the back of her wrist, angrily. "You signed again. You signed for two more years without asking me."

"We had the hospital debt. The allowance was—"

"I know what the allowance was. You didn't ask. You wrote and told me after, as if it were a decision about the weather. And then the telegram came about your father, and you weren't here, and I sat beside him and washed his body with Tarsem. I did that. Then I cooked for a hundred and sixty people with a fever. Nobody asked me either."

"I didn't know he was going."

"Nobody knew. That's what I'm saying." She was quiet a moment. "I'm not angry now. I was angry for a year, and then it burnt out, and what was left was this. A life. It's working, Gurnam. I'm afraid of breaking it."

He looked at his hand lying open on his knee. The fingers that weren't there itched. He scratched the next one.

"Can I come to Moga?"

"Of course you can come."

"I mean to live."

She didn't answer immediately, and he felt the silence move through him and come out the other side.

"There are two rooms," she said. "One is the kitchen's store. The other is hers and mine. I don't know where I'd—" She stopped. "I don't know how to say it so it isn't cruel. You stand in doorways, Gurnam. Since this morning. You stand in the door of every room, like a man at a shop who isn't sure he's allowed to touch. And I look at you and I don't know how to tell you to come in. I don't know what comes in."

"I don't either," he said.

It was the truest thing he had said all day. She turned her head and looked at him then, properly, in the light from the moon, and her face was soft and exhausted and quite unfamiliar, and he saw that she was looking at his the same way.

"Come to Moga," she said finally. "Don't come to our house. Come to Moga. Find a room. Find work. Come on Sundays, and see her. Let her see you. Let me see." Her voice cracked. "I'm not asking for forever. I'm asking for Sunday."

He nodded.

"Is that terrible?"

"No," he said. "It's a thing I can do."

---

He found the room on the fourth day, above a tyre shop four lanes behind the bus stand, ten feet by twelve with one window and a water tap in the corner. The owner, a Jain with a dusty cap, wanted two months' advance. The cold storage plant on the Ferozepur road needed a night guard, someone with a service record and a licensed weapon, and the manager looked at the discharge papers and then at the hand and asked only, "Can you stay awake?"

"Yes," Gurnam said. "That's the one thing."

On the last night before he left the village he went up to the roof with a torch. Simran's marigolds were in bloom, and her chalk lines marched along the parapet. He found a free stretch of stone near the corner and, kneeling, drew a short crooked line with a stub of her chalk. He wrote the date beside it, in English, the way she did. Then he stayed and watched the sky until a plane came over, small and red-lighted, going toward Adampur, and he let it go all the way.

---

In June the village shrank behind the bus window, the green gate, the neem, the wall. His mother had wept at the door and given him a bag of mathi, and Tarsem had pressed an envelope into his pocket that he did not open until Ludhiana. It held five thousand rupees and a note that said *For the land, we will sit down soon,* and he read it and folded it in four and put it in the trunk with the rest.

The first Sunday he took the bus at nine. He had on a pressed shirt. He had bought a box of jalebis from the shop at the corner of Dhilon Road, hot, the syrup shining through the cardboard, and he carried the box in his right hand because he could grip it that way. In his left he carried the pencil box with the tiger, which he had finally taken out of the rucksack.

The shop was a double-shuttered thing painted yellow, with *Pammi's Home Kitchen* in red lettering and a phone number. The shutters were half down. Up a narrow stair at the side, a door.

He climbed. He stood in front of it. The smell of frying onion came down the stairwell, and a steel spoon scraped against a pot, and a radio was playing a song he knew from his childhood, a Rafi song, thin and sweet. He lifted his hand and then held it in the air, an inch from the wood.

The door opened before he could knock.

Simran stood there in a yellow frock with a hair clip in her mouth. She took it out when she saw him.

"Papa," she said. And then, because she was an honest child, "You're early. Mummy said ten."

"It's quarter to."

"We're not ready." She looked past him, down the stairs, as if someone might be following, then at the box in his hand. "Is that jalebi?"

"Hot."

"Mummy doesn't let me have it before lunch."

"Then I'll hold it until after lunch."

She weighed this. Behind her, in the room, he could see a bed with a quilted cover, a small table with a plastic cloth, a stove, a window with light falling across the floor. A calendar. The cartons. Pammi's back at the stove, turned halfway, her shoulders stiffening at the sound of his voice.

He stood on the top step with the box and the pencil case and his three fingers and his thumb, and did not step over the sill.

Simran looked at his feet and then up at his face. She stepped back and held the door wide with her whole arm, the way a child does when it matters.

"You can't stand there," she said. "The flies come in."

He went in.
