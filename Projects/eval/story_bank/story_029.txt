The rain had been falling for nine days when Sigrid Halvorsen first noticed the hole in her morning. She lay in bed in the Sandviken apartment, the window rattling, and did not sit up to look at the fjord. Outside, the water would be the color of old pewter. The light would be thin. She pressed the monitor on her left wrist until it gave two violet pulses, which meant that her contentment index was holding. Then she took the small white pill from the red box on the bedstand and swallowed it without water. The pill was called Innhold, but she only called it the morning pill.

In the bathroom she smelled wet wool. Aksel's old sweater hung inside the wardrobe door, and the damp air had woken it. She did not touch it. She counted the buttons on her uniform jacket: eight. The collar pin was a stylized fjord with a line through it. The line meant No Distance. The children, before the Uroen, had called it other names.

She walked to the tram in the rain. At the Fisketorget, a girl of maybe eight was humming a tune under the awning, a thin old melody. Sigrid's monitor prickled. The girl saw the uniform and stopped. The sound vanished into the hiss of rain on cobblestones. Sigrid looked away and did not report her. It was a small omission. The day's file would not be smaller for it.

At the Ministry of Emotional Hygiene in Marken, the windows were blue-tinted. The building had been a bank before the Uroen, and it still had the thick doors of a bank. Sigrid sat at her desk and opened the intake list. Her colleague Roald Kjos came over with a cup of coffee and placed a folder on the edge of her screen.

"New one for you," he said. "Old woman at quay four. Third report. She stands there every morning, looking at the Nordstjernen."

The Nordstjernen was an old coastal steamer, decommissioned and tied at the industrial quay, rust bleeding through the white paint. The Ministry wanted it scrapped, but there was a debate about the cost of towing it out to the shipbreaker.

"She hums," Roald said. "Vital signs show a drop. Maybe a seven-A."

Seven-A was the code for lengsel. The word was no longer used in official documents, only the code. Longing, homesickness, nostalgia, the unproductive hunger for something absent or lost. It had been classified as an Affective Offense after the Uroen, when the old loyalties had almost torn the country apart. That was the official explanation.

"Old people stare at ships," Sigrid said.

"Then the interview will be short." Roald smiled, but his eyes were flat. "But remember, if you clear too many, they audit your own index."

After he left, she opened the folder. The woman was Marit Vang, seventy-six, born in Sør-Trøndelag, former radio operator on the Hurtigruten. She lived alone in Nordnes and had no surviving relatives on record. The previous reports noted that she stood for two hours each morning in rain and wind, facing the ship, with her hands open at her sides. The reporting party was a neighbor, a Block Wellbeing Officer, who had attached a sound file. Sigrid listened. Through the hiss of the recording she could hear a few notes, broken by wind, in a minor key. She recognized them. It was the old call signal of the Hurtigruten, from before the Uroen. Her throat tightened. She swallowed and set the monitor to record.

The holding room at the Ministry had no windows. Marit Vang sat with her hands folded over a grey wool coat. Her hair was white and pinned up, and her face was lined like a fjord in winter: still, but shaped by weather. She did not look afraid.

Sigrid sat across from her and turned on the recorder. "Fru Vang, you have been observed standing at quay four every morning between six and eight for nine weeks. You look at a decommissioned ship. Can you explain?"

"I like the way the light falls on the water," Marit said.

"Light does not require nine weeks."

"Maybe I am slow to trust a sunrise."

Sigrid tapped the file. "You were recorded humming a pre-Uroen work melody associated with the coastal service. It is on the prohibited list. Humming it in public while looking at a decommissioned ship is considered evidence of Affective Offense seven-A."

Marit looked at the monitor on Sigrid's wrist. "Do you know what that ship carried, frue? Not herring. Not only post. It carried people going home. 'Hjem' is a word. Do they still allow it?"

"The destination is permitted. The feeling about it is not."

"And what do you feel when you see the ship?"

Sigrid kept her face steady. "I don't feel anything. I am here to assess you."

Marit nodded. "Then why did you hesitate?"

Sigrid wrote in the file: Subject shows high attachment to decommissioned object. No direct confession. Recommend Level 2 monitoring. She closed the folder. "Stay away from the quay, fru Vang. If the monitor records another drop, I will not be the one interviewing you."

Marit stood. She was smaller than Sigrid had expected, but her back was straight. "You cannot stop water," she said. "You can only build walls. The water always finds the way out."

That evening, Sigrid walked home through the rain. Her son Jonas sat at the kitchen table with his school tablet pushed aside, drawing on paper, which was no longer common. He had his father's dark hair and long fingers. The drawing showed a mountain shaped like a sleeping woman, with a door at the waterline and a path leading down to a small boat.

"What is that?" Sigrid asked.

"Just a mountain." He slid it beneath his workbook.

She saw that his wrist monitor lay on the table, its strap unbuckled. She said, "Put it on."

"It itches."

"It itches everyone. The alternative is worse."

"What alternative?" He looked up. "To feel something? To remember something?"

"Don't say that."

"Did you know him?" Jonas asked. He was thirteen, and this was his weapon.

She felt her jaw tighten. "We don't talk about your father."

"You don't. I didn't know him. Is it illegal to want to know someone who's dead?"

"It is not about dead people, Jonas. It is about staying alive. We are not a family that can afford questions like that."

She went into her bedroom and closed the door. In the bottom drawer, beneath folded stockings, was a small wooden boat her father had carved decades ago, when she was a child on Askøy. The mast was missing. The hull was smooth as skin. She took it out and held it. Her monitor gave a faint amber pulse. She put the boat away, took a second pill, and lay down in her uniform until the pulse faded to violet.

On Sunday, she visited her mother at Solgløtt, the residential center on the hillside. Inger Halvorsen sat in a chair by the window, knitting with no yarn, the needles clicking. She had not recognized Sigrid for two years. The reconditioning had left her peaceful, the nurses said, but there were holes in her memory like the holes in old lace. Sometimes she hummed. Sometimes she said the names of islands.

Sigrid sat beside her. Inger looked out at the rain.

"The boat from Os comes at six," Inger said.

"There is no boat, mor."

"Then we will wait."

Sigrid opened her mouth and closed it. Her monitor gave a soft amber light. She stood quickly, so the nurse would not see.

Inger reached into the pocket of her cardigan and pressed a small ball of red yarn into Sigrid's hand. "For the mittens," she said.

Sigrid put the yarn in her coat pocket and rode the tram home. In the wardrobe, beside Aksel's sweater, she placed the red yarn on the shelf. It sat there like a heart that would not stop beating.

On Monday, Roald appeared at her desk with another folder. The committee had reviewed her Level 2 recommendation and overridden it. The video analysis of Marit Vang's interview had found pupil dilation at the mention of the ship, a sigh, and a micro-expression in the left cheek. She was scheduled for omstilling at sixteen hundred hours. Sigrid would escort her from the holding center to the Institute of Affective Correction.

"She is seventy-six," Sigrid said.

"She is a clear case," Roald said. "You are too soft, Halvorsen. That is not a compliment."

Sigrid went to the washroom and stood gripping the sink. In the mirror, her face looked older than thirty-nine. She did not know what she felt. That was the worst part: the feeling was there but had no name, because giving it a name was already a crime. She thought of Marit's open hands at the quay, the small ball of red yarn, the mountain with a door.

At Nordnes, she used her inspector's code to enter Marit Vang's apartment. It was not a search. She told herself it was a final risk assessment. The apartment was small and spare but clean. On the windowsill stood a carved seabird, grey wood with one wing raised. A photograph hung in the hall: a man with soot-stained hands and a wide smile, standing on a ship's deck. There was a radio set with a dial polished by use. The bed was made. In the kitchen, Marit was not surprised to see her.

"You are here to take me," Marit said.

"I am here to ask you one question." Sigrid sat at the narrow table without being asked. "The night they took your husband, what would you have done if someone had told you there was a boat leaving the quay at midnight?"

Marit poured two cups of cardamom tea. "I would have gone."

"Even if it meant never coming back?"

"Coming back is not the point." Marit sat down. "The point is to have a place you want to return to. A person you want to find. That is what the Ministry cannot stamp out. They can erase the words, but the body counts the hours."

Sigrid drank the tea. It was hot and sweet and tasted like a winter she had almost forgotten. Marit leaned closer.

"The Nordstjernen is not dead. The engine still works. A man named Edvin, who was a grease monkey on the old route, works nights at the quay. He falsifies the maintenance logs. Tonight at two, he will start the generator for half an hour. The harbor patrol changes at one fifty. If someone were to walk up the gangway at two, before the security sweep, they could be gone by half past. The Ministry will not miss one old woman and a ship that is supposed to be scrap."

"How do you know the patrol changes?" Sigrid asked.

"Because I have been standing at that quay for nine weeks, frue. Not just staring at the light."

Sigrid felt a pulse in her wrist. It was not violet. She looked down. The monitor glowed amber, then red. She covered it with her sleeve.

"There are islands to the north," Marit said. "Rock and mist and not enough arable land for the Ministry to bother with. The old people call it Heimlandet. It is not a country, only a place where the monitors do not reach. You can bring your son."

"I did not say I would go."

Marit smiled. "You did not say you would not."

That afternoon, Sigrid returned to the Ministry and locked herself in a records room. She entered her own mother's name. The file opened with a soft chime. Inger Halvorsen had been admitted to Solgløtt three years after the Uroen. The primary offense was listed as seven-A, secondary: unresolved grief after the disappearance of spouse. Arvid Halvorsen, her father, had been the owner of a small fishing boat called Fiskebøl. The file did not say what had happened to him. It said only that he was missing. Sigrid knew the rest: he had gone out before dawn in the last autumn before the Samveldet closed the coastal waters. She remembered standing on the dock with her mother, and the way the sea had swallowed the running lights. She had not thought of the running lights in twenty years. Her monitor went red, and she did not turn away from the screen. She let it pulse against her skin, bright and dangerous, until the records room door opened and a junior clerk asked if she needed anything. She closed the file and left.

That evening, the school called. Jonas had been detained in the rector's office after his Art and Contentment teacher found the drawing of the mountain with a door. A pattern recognition unit had flagged it as "cartographic nostalgia," a minor offense, but the teacher had reported three amber pulses on his monitor during the lesson, and he had refused to take the afternoon pill. The rector spoke to Sigrid with the careful voice of a man who could ruin her.

"Your son is a bright boy, frue Halvorsen. But he asks questions. He draws unauthorized places. If this continues, the Youth Wellbeing Board will become involved."

Sigrid arrived at the school in her uniform. Jonas sat in the hallway, his chin up. The drawing was in his lap, folded but not destroyed. They released him into her care with a warning. Outside, the rain had stopped. The sky over the city was a strange, deep blue.

"I won't apologize," Jonas said.

"I know," Sigrid said.

They walked home without speaking. At the apartment, she told him to pack a small bag: one change of clothes, no tablet, no monitor. He looked at her, and for the first time in months, his eyes were not guarded.

"Are we going to the mountain?" he asked.

"We are going to a ship," she said.

She packed the wooden boat, the red yarn, and Aksel's sweater. She did not know why she took the sweater, except that it smelled of wool and salt and had survived longer than any love she had been allowed to name. She sent a message to the Ministry saying she had been exposed to a low-grade affective disturbance and would self-isolate overnight. It would not hold, but it might buy an hour.

At a quarter to two, they walked to the industrial quay. The streets were empty. The fjord lay black between the mountains, and the Nordstjernen rose against the sky like a ghost. The gangway was unguarded. Edvin met them at the top, an old man with grease under his nails and a seaman's cap. Marit Vang stood on the deck, wearing the grey coat, looking out at the city.

"Took you long enough," she said.

Edvin led them to the engine room. The ship hummed, a low sound that Sigrid felt in her teeth. Edvin said, "The harbor boom is the only problem. We can cast off, but the automatic gate at the harbor mouth needs an inspector's code to open."

Sigrid gave him her code card. He took it and disappeared toward the bridge. Marit showed Jonas the radio room, where the old dials gleamed in the dim light. Sigrid stood at the rail and watched the city of Bergen slide away: the white building with blue windows, the fish market's dark awnings, the lit window of their apartment in Sandviken. Her monitor glowed red. She did not cover it.

Searchlights swept the quay as the lines dropped. A Ministry vehicle skidded to a stop at the gate, and Roald's voice came through a loudspeaker, metallic and thin: "Halvorsen! Step off the vessel! This is your final chance!"

She did not move. The engine throbbed. Edvin shouted from the bridge that the boom was opening. The ship glided out of the harbor mouth, and the searchlights caught the side of the old white hull, then fell away.

Roald's last words were lost in the wind. Sigrid turned to find Marit standing beside her.

"The ship remembers, you said," Sigrid said.

"No," Marit said. "Now it will remember you."

The city shrank to a scatter of lights. Jonas came up from the radio room, his face bright. He was laughing, but it was not a stable laugh; it was the laugh of someone who had been holding his breath for years. He put his hand on Sigrid's arm.

"Mor," he said. "I can see the stars."

She looked up. The sky was full of them, impossible and old. She unfastened her wrist monitor. It had been on her arm since the first year of the Samveldet, and the skin beneath was pale and new. For a moment she thought of dropping it into the water, but she did not want to poison the fjord with even one more piece of the world she was leaving.

So she held it out to Jonas. "Throw it as far as you can."

He threw it over the rail. It fell red through the dark and vanished without a sound. Behind them, the Norwegian coast folded around its black water. Ahead, the North Sea opened. Marit began to hum the old call signal, and this time no one stopped.
