At 5,200 meters, the air had teeth. Park Ji-hoon pressed his thumb into the frozen condensation on the cook-tent window and wiped a small clear circle. Through it, the southwest face of Langdak Ri rose in one unbroken sweep of blue ice and wind-scoured snow—7,041 meters of mountain that had never been climbed. The face looked beautiful and impossible, like something cut from paper and held against the sky.

Inside the cook tent, the expedition leader, Kang Dae-hyun, was taping a satellite photograph to an aluminum box. The half-melted water in the pot beside him hissed. Dawa, the cook, moved quietly between propane burners, and the smell of garlic and dal bhat filled the warm space.

“The traverse here,” Dae-hyun said, drawing a gloved finger along the photograph, “under the hanging glacier to the col at sixty-seven hundred. From there, we go up the southwest couloir to the summit snowfield. It is the only line that makes sense.”

“Only line?” Kazuki Tanabe looked up from his notebook. The Japanese climber had been drawing the same curve for nearly thirty minutes, pressing the pencil so lightly that the line was almost invisible. He was thirty-eight, lean, with a face that seemed to have been cured by wind rather than aged. He had climbed more eight-thousanders without supplementary oxygen than anyone else in the tent had climbed with it. “That is not the only line.”

Dae-hyun stiffened. He was a brilliant organizer, a former army officer who had led two successful expeditions to Gasherbrum IV, but he did not like being questioned by quiet men with notebooks. “Then present your route, Tanabe-san.”

Kazuki turned the notebook around. The drawing showed Langdak Ri’s southwest face, but a dark line ran up the left-hand rib, a jagged spine of rock and ice that dropped two thousand vertical meters to the glacier below.

“That is a suicide line,” Min-seo said. Yoon Min-seo was a cardiologist from Busan, thirty-one years old, with short black hair and a laugh that could thaw a frozen tent. She was the strongest woman Ji-hoon had ever climbed with, and she was not easily frightened.

“No one has died there,” Kazuki said.

“Because no one has been stupid enough to attempt it,” Dae-hyun said.

Kazuki closed the notebook. “The glacier is breathing. I have watched it for six days. The tongue moves three centimeters every morning. The traverse under it will be gone before the full moon.”

Ji-hoon translated the sentence to himself, though it had already been spoken in English. *The glacier is breathing.* He did not know whether to be worried or fascinated. The phrase hung in the tent like the steam from the dal.

Dae-hyun tapped the satellite photo. “The traverse is the only route approved by the permit. We climb the mountain, not our fears.”

“We climb the mountain, not our egos,” Kazuki said quietly.

That was the first decision no one understood. It was hardly a decision yet, but the line had been drawn.

---

Two days later, at Camp 1, Ji-hoon woke with ice in his nostrils. The camp had been pitched at 6,100 meters on a broad snow platform at the top of the fixed lines. It was the only flat place, the only sane place, but Kazuki had refused to pitch his tent there. He had spent the afternoon digging a hole into a slight rise forty meters to the east, below a small crevasse lip, and he had set his single yellow tent inside it, half-buried, out of sight from the main platform.

When Dae-hyun asked him why, Kazuki said, “The snow has memory. It remembers the wind. This platform collects.”

No one understood that either. The sky was clear, the wind moderate. Ji-hoon thought it might be the first symptoms of altitude sickness, the kind of private logic that arrives quietly at high altitude, but Kazuki’s eyes were calm and his speech was clear. He had no headache, no nausea. He just dug that hole.

That night, the wind shifted. It came down the south face in long, screaming gusts and filled the tents with spindrift. Ji-hoon lay awake listening to the fabric crack like a sail. In the morning, he found the main platform buried under a dune of wind-loaded snow. His own tent door had to be kicked open from inside, and he emerged into a world erased to white. Kazuki’s tent, in its dug-in hole, had been protected. The yellow fabric moved slightly in the breeze, a small bright flag.

At breakfast, Dae-hyun did not mention it. Kazuki did not mention it. Ji-hoon stood outside and looked down the mountain to the glacier, where the shadows were long and blue, and he felt the first small turning in his chest.

---

The mountain let them go no higher that week. A storm rolled in and pinned them at base camp, then at Camp 1, then back at base camp. The permits allowed three weeks. The weather window, when it finally came, was forecast to be short: five days of high pressure, then another front.

During the storm days, Kazuki became more distant. He spent hours alone with his telephoto lens aimed at the southwest face. He photographed the hanging glacier at dawn, at noon, at dusk. He filled his notebook with columns of numbers. He walked out to the edge of the moraine and placed stones in a line—a line that, Ji-hoon noticed, matched the angle of the left-hand rib.

One afternoon, Ji-hoon found him behind the kitchen tent, packing a small red dry bag: a stove, a fuel canister, a first-aid kit, one half-liter of water. He added a pair of gloves and a headlamp.

“Are you leaving?” Ji-hoon asked in Japanese. He had learned it in Seoul, from a former girlfriend who loved Japanese literature, and it was enough to hold a conversation.

Kazuki looked up. “I am leaving a door.”

“A door?”

“A way back.” He pulled the drawstring tight and stood. “If the door is not there when we need it, it is not a door.”

Ji-hoon watched him walk toward the glacier, carrying the red dry bag. He did not know whether to follow. By evening, Kazuki had returned without it.

That night, Ji-hoon sat with Min-seo in the mess tent. She was stitching a torn gaiter with a curved needle, the headlamp casting a small pool of light on her hands.

“He’s not well,” she said.

“He’s not stupid,” Ji-hoon replied.

“There’s a difference?”

“Stupid climbers die in obvious ways. He’s not dying.”

Min-seo looked up at him. “Then why is he hiding stoves in the glacier?”

Ji-hoon had no answer.

---

At 6,650 meters, the shoulder of Langdak Ri opened into a wide col below the summit couloir. It was the logical place for Camp 2. It was flat, protected from the west wind, and close to the route. The only problem was the rock wall that rose along its northern edge—a black, shattered cliff that occasionally sent down loose stones like a bored child dropping pebbles from a bridge.

The team arrived in the afternoon, exhausted. Dae-hyun ordered the dome tents pitched in the center of the col, away from the wall. The kitchen tent went up first, bright orange against the snow.

Kazuki stopped at the edge of the col and stood very still. He was carrying a coil of rope and two ice screws, and he had not spoken in an hour.

“Not there,” he said.

Dae-hyun turned. “What?”

“The kitchen. Put it against the wall.”

“The wall that sheds rocks?” Dae-hyun laughed, but there was no humor in it. “You want us to cook under a rockfall zone?”

“The wall is clean. I looked at it from below. The loose stones are all in the left funnel. The right side is solid. The center of the col is where the wind drops its load. If the snow slides, it slides there.”

“The snow has not slid here in decades,” Dae-hyun said. “The col is a safe place. You’re being paranoid.”

Kazuki’s expression did not change. “I am being alive.”

He carried his own tent to the base of the wall and pitched it in the angle between two huge blocks. The others put the kitchen in the center of the col.

That night, Ji-hoon lay in his tent listening to the mountain. It made small sounds: cracks, settlings, a low rumble far off that might have been a serac or might have been the earth’s own stomach. At two in the morning, a rock the size of a helmet struck the center of the col. It did not hit the kitchen tent, but it landed six meters away, and the impact was enough to wake everyone. In the morning, Ji-hoon saw the hole it had punched, a dark socket in the snow.

The kitchen tent had been moved. At some point before dawn, Kazuki had dragged it by its guylines to the wall.

Dae-hyun stood looking at the hole for a long time. Then he turned to Kazuki, who was boiling water in the shadow of the rock face.

“Lucky,” Dae-hyun said.

“Yes,” Kazuki said. “The mountain is lucky for us.”

---

The next decision was the one that nearly broke the expedition.

They had fixed two ropes along the first part of the traverse from Camp 2 toward the col, a rising ledge of snow and ice that crossed beneath the hanging glacier. The ropes had been placed to protect a fall on the left side, where the slope dropped away into a 900-meter face. Without them, the traverse was a no-fall zone.

On the third morning at Camp 2, Ji-hoon woke to find Kazuki already on the traverse. He was coiling the fixed ropes and carrying them back to camp.

By the time Dae-hyun saw him, the ropes were stacked in the snow like dead snakes.

“What have you done?” Dae-hyun’s voice cracked with fury.

“The rope will cut the mountain,” Kazuki said.

“Those are our fixed lines. That is our descent. Are you insane?”

Kazuki looked at the coil. “You trust the rope too much. You will walk across the glacier because the rope is there. The rope is a lie.”

“It is the only thing keeping us alive!”

“The only thing keeping us alive is not being under the glacier when it falls.”

Dae-hyun took a step forward. Ji-hoon thought he might hit him. Min-seo put a hand on Dae-hyun’s arm. Lhakpa, the expedition sirdar, watched from beside the pile of gear, his weathered face impossible to read.

“I am taking you off the summit team,” Dae-hyun said. “You will stay here. You will not climb. When we return, we will take the ropes you removed, and you will be sent down first.”

Kazuki nodded slowly. “I will stay here. But the ropes stay with me.”

“You don’t give the orders.”

“The ropes stay with me because they are no longer on the mountain. You cannot put them back. The glacier’s tongue has shifted. The anchor points are gone.”

It was true. Ji-hoon had looked at the traverse that morning. The snow had changed; there was a new fissure near the first anchor, a dark crack running uphill. The anchors might still be there, but no one could be sure without climbing to them. Dae-hyun stood breathing hard in the thin air.

That night, Ji-hoon went to Kazuki’s tent. The Japanese climber was sitting outside on a foam pad, writing in his notebook by headlamp. The sky was clear and very black above the mountain.

“Why did you take the ropes?” Ji-hoon asked.

“Because I did not want to die.”

“That’s not an answer.”

Kazuki looked up. The headlamp light carved his face into shadows. “The hanging glacier is a living thing. It moves. The ropes were anchored to one moment. If you follow a rope, you follow the past. We need to follow the present.”

Ji-hoon sat down beside him. “You’re talking like a monk.”

“My teacher was a monk. He climbed with me in Hokkaido. He said a mountain is a text. You read it with your skin.”

“What does your skin say now?”

Kazuki turned off the headlamp. In the dark, Ji-hoon could hear the glacier groaning far away.

“It says the text is changing,” Kazuki said.

---

The weather window opened on schedule. The forecast called for five clear days, but the jet stream would return after that. The team had one chance.

Dae-hyun had not relented on Kazuki’s punishment. Kazuki would remain at Camp 2 with the gear, while Dae-hyun, Min-seo, Ji-hoon, and Lhakpa would make the summit attempt. But the route was now unresolved: the traverse under the hanging glacier, with its missing fixed ropes, or the left-hand rib that Kazuki had drawn in his notebook.

At four in the morning, the team stood roped up at the edge of the col. The darkness was so complete that the stars seemed to have weight. Kazuki came out of his tent fully dressed, with his harness on and his crampons strapped over his boots.

“You are staying,” Dae-hyun said.

“I am going up,” Kazuki said. “You cannot stop me from climbing the rib. It is not your route.”

“I am the expedition leader.”

“And I am the one who will survive.”

They stared at each other. Then Kazuki turned to Ji-hoon. “You feel it too. The glacier is sick. The rib is the only line.”

Ji-hoon looked at the dark face of the rib. It rose steeply, a black serration against the stars. It was terrifying. But the traverse under the hanging glacier was worse, and with the ropes gone, it was nearly unthinkable. He had been trying not to think about it.

Dae-hyun addressed the others. “We stick to the plan. We go to the traverse. Kazuki can do whatever he wants.”

Min-seo looked at Ji-hoon, uncertain. Lhakpa was already moving, his crampons crunching on the frozen snow.

Ji-hoon felt the cold set into his jaw. He turned to Dae-hyun. “I’m going with Kazuki.”

Dae-hyun’s face tightened. “Then you are both off the summit team. If you climb that rib, you’re climbing alone.”

“We will meet you on the summit,” Kazuki said.

He turned and began walking toward the left-hand rib. Ji-hoon hesitated for one second, then followed.

---

The rib was harder than anything Ji-hoon had climbed in his life. It was not the altitude, though the altitude was enough to make each breath feel like drawing air through a straw. It was the exposure: a narrow spine of ice and shattered rock, with the southwest face dropping away on one side and the north face on the other. The world fell away to clouds and black valleys. Ji-hoon climbed slowly, placing ice screws, watching Kazuki move above him with strange, economical grace.

At a small ledge at 6,900 meters, Kazuki stopped. He chopped a bollard in the ice with his axe, looped the rope around it, and then did something Ji-hoon did not understand: he untied his harness from the rope, then retied it with a loose overhand knot around the main line.

“If I fall, cut the rope,” Kazuki said.

“What?”

“You cannot hold me. The rope will pull you off. Cut it.”

“I’m not going to cut you.”

Kazuki looked at him with something that might have been affection. “Then I will not fall.”

They climbed on. The sun rose behind the summit, painting the snow pink and then white. Ji-hoon’s arms burned, his lungs burned, his mind became a small, clear point of terror and fatigue. He forgot about the others. He forgot about the summit. There was only the next placement, the next breath.

At 7,000 meters, just below the summit snowfield, they reached a small col where the rib merged with the mountain. The view opened. To the left, the standard route crossed the last part of the traverse under the hanging glacier. Ji-hoon could see the fixed ropes that had not been removed—two thin lines across a convex white slope. And above that slope, the hanging glacier hung like a frozen wave, thirty meters high, blue and cracked.

He saw three small figures moving along the traverse.

“They’re early,” Ji-hoon said. He was surprised by the sound of his own voice, high and thin.

Kazuki did not answer. He was looking at the glacier with the same intensity Ji-hoon had seen in the mess tent, the same still attention a cat gives to a bird.

Then the glacier spoke.

It began as a low crack, a sound so deep Ji-hoon felt it in his sternum. Then a white seam appeared across the front of the ice. The seam opened, and the entire lower lip of the glacier detached—a slab the size of an apartment building—and fell.

The sound was not like thunder. It was like the mountain clearing its throat. The slab hit the slope and disintegrated into a boiling white cloud that swept down the traverse. Ji-hoon could see the three figures for a moment longer, tiny specks against the cloud, and then they were gone.

He screamed. The cloud rolled past them, missing the rib by fifty meters, and then the mountain was silent again.

---

They descended through the avalanche debris. The traverse had been erased. Where there had been snow and ice, there was now a field of churned blocks, white and blue and gray, some of them the size of cars. The cloud had settled into a fine, choking powder.

They found Lhakpa first. He was half-buried at the edge of the debris, conscious and groaning, one arm bent at an impossible angle. His eyes were wide with shock. Kazuki dug him out with his bare hands, talking to him in a low voice. Ji-hoon wrapped him in his own down jacket.

Min-seo was farther down, tangled in a rope that had been tied to a snow picket. The picket had held, but the rope had cut into her leg. She was alive, her face white, her lips blue. Kazuki used the first-aid kit from his pack and then sat in the snow and laughed, a terrible, exhausted sound.

Dae-hyun was nowhere. They called his name until their voices were gone. The mountain did not answer.

Ji-hoon thought of the caches Kazuki had buried. He thought of the stove and the first-aid kit, the spare gloves, the headlamp. They were somewhere below. Without them, the survivors would die of exposure before a rescue could reach them.

“We need to get them down,” Ji-hoon said.

Kazuki shook his head. “Not down. We need the door.”

He led Ji-hoon down the mountain, through the thinning avalanche cloud, to the crevasse where he had hidden the red dry bag. It was exactly where he had left it, marked by three small stones in a line. Inside were a stove, a fuel canister, a bivy sack, a medical kit, and a note that said, in small precise characters: *For the second chance.*

They carried the cache back up to the injured climbers. They melted snow. They wrapped them in the bivy sack. They waited, talking to keep them awake, as the afternoon light grew thin.

Lhakpa’s radio had been lost in the avalanche, but Kazuki had a small satellite phone in the cache. He called base camp. A helicopter could not fly at night, but it would come at dawn if the weather held.

That night, lying in the snow beside Min-seo, Ji-hoon asked Kazuki, “How did you know?”

Kazuki stared up at the stars. “I did not know. I only saw.”

“Saw what?”

“The mountain’s text. The glacier had a line. The line was old. When the ice changes temperature, the line changes color. I saw it in the photos. The line had moved. It was ready.”

“Why didn’t you explain?”

“I tried. I am not a good speaker. In Osaka, my teacher said I had a tongue like a stone in a river. The river knows the way, but the stone only knows the water.”

Ji-hoon closed his eyes. The cold was like a blanket now, and his mind had begun to drift. He thought of Dae-hyun under the ice, and he thought of the line in Kazuki’s notebook, the dark line up the rib. He had thought it was madness. But the mountain had known.

At dawn, the helicopter came. It was a small blue shape against the gray sky, and it did not land, but it dropped a basket and a winch line. They lifted Min-seo first, then Lhakpa. Ji-hoon watched the basket swing up over the glacier, small as a toy.

Then the winch came back for Kazuki and Ji-hoon. As they were lifted off the mountain, Kazuki looked down at the broken glacier, the churned snow, the route that no longer existed.

“The mountain is still breathing,” he said.

Ji-hoon did not answer. He was thinking about the stone in the river, and the way some men are shaped by water until they become a kind of knowing.
