The banner was the first thing Yolanda Espinet heard about it, and she heard about it from a sign shop.

It was Friday afternoon, two days before, and she was in the pharmacy at the hospital counting out a blister pack of amlodipine when her phone buzzed with an unknown number and the man on the other end said, "Yes, hello, is this the contact for the Espinet order? The welcome banner. It's twelve feet by three. I just want to confirm before we print — you want 'WELCOME HOME RALPH,' all capitals, and then in the corner, smaller, 'MAMA D AT 80.'"

Yoli put down the blister pack.

"Say that again," she said.

He said it again. Twelve feet by three. WELCOME HOME RALPH. MAMA D AT 80.

"Who placed this order?"

"Curtis. Curtis Espinet. He put you as the contact, that's why I'm calling."

"Right," Yoli said. "Right. Yes."

She hung up and stood there in the med room with the phone in her hand, and then she laughed, once, the way you laugh when a car almost hits you and drives off. Her uncle Curtis. Her uncle Curtis, fifty-six years old, a man who had once bought a timeshare in Orlando on a whim and had to be talked out of it by four separate relatives, had ordered a twelve-foot banner welcoming home her father, who had not been in the same room as this family in twenty-two years.

Ralph Espinet. Eldest son of Dolores Espinet. Her father.

She texted Curtis: *Call me now.*

He sent back a thumbs up.

She texted him again. She called him. It rang out. She called again. She texted: *Curtis. I know about the banner. Call me.*

Nothing. Two hours later, a reply: *Big things happening Sunday!! 😎 Trust me.*

So she did the only thing she could do. She carried it.

She carried it through Saturday, through the errands, through picking up the ice and the extra ice and the Ziplocs for the ice, through going to her grandmother's apartment on Ellesmere to help her choose between the navy two-piece and the maroon two-piece, through sitting on the edge of Mama D's bed while her grandmother told her, for the fourth time that month, about the San Fernando house.

"I tell your grandfather, Sonny, I say, that land is your brother own, and he say, woman, leave me. And the man leave me." Mama D laughed at her own joke and then coughed and then winced, so Yoli got up and got her the water and got her the spray out of her own purse — the little canister of nitroglycerin, which Yoli had kept in her purse for the last three years, first out of caution and now out of habit, the way you keep a house key.

"You all right?"

"I fine, I fine." Her grandmother waved her off. "How much people coming Sunday?"

"Sixty. Sixty-one. Auntie Pearl came in Tuesday."

"Pearl."

"And Maggie flew in from Miami last night. She staying with Ann-Marie. Which is going to be a whole situation by itself."

"Mm," Mama D said. "And you know what Curtis doing?"

Yoli's hands stopped on the zipper of her purse.

"What do you mean?"

"He been calling me all week. He say, Mummy, I have something for you Sunday. He say, you go see." Mama D turned her face to the window, toward the parking lot and the low Toronto sky. "What he bringing? A cake? I don't want no more cake. I have three cake."

That was the thing. Her grandmother did not know. Yoli stood in the bedroom of an eighty-year-old woman with a stented artery and a failing memory for names and an absolute, flinty memory for grievances, and she knew something that her grandmother did not, and she could not ask it out loud because asking it out loud was the same as saying it, and if Curtis had been lying, if he'd been making one of his promises, then Yoli would have put Ralph Espinet's name into the air two days before the reunion and she would have had to watch what that did.

"Probably a cake," Yoli said.

---

The Brimley Road Community Hall smelled like floor wax and about forty years of other people's weddings. Yoli got there at ten with her trunk full of tablecloths.

By one the aunties had arrived and had opinions. Ann-Marie, in cream linen, was worried about the macaroni pie — worried about it in the way that a woman is worried about a pie when what she is actually worried about is her sister. Pearl, up from Trinidad, was slow and delighted and had brought two bottles of her own pepper sauce in her suitcase, wrapped in a beach towel, which she opened on the first folding table like a coronation. Maggie arrived an hour late and said it was the traffic on the 401 and everyone exchanged a look because Maggie had been in the country since Thursday, and Yoli's cousin Simone leaned over to her and said, "Maggie had to do her face. It's an hour of face."

The chafing dishes came out. The pelau steamed under its foil. Kareem, a young pan player Curtis had found on a Facebook group — Yoli had paid him a deposit out of her own pocket on Wednesday because Curtis had gone quiet on that too — set up his tenor pan in the corner and started half-heartedly running scales, and it was actually lovely, a bright metal sound that made the whole hall feel like somewhere else.

The kids ran in circles. Tyrese, who was six, made a fort out of the stacked plastic chairs and had to be extracted. Somebody's speaker was playing Kitchener, then Sparrow, then something more recent that brought the teenagers out onto the floor. The old people sat in a row and passed judgment on the food. People kept arriving, cousins with children Yoli couldn't name, men in pressed shirts, women who hugged her and said, "Yolanda, you're the one who organized it? You're too good, girl, too good," and Yoli took the compliment into herself the way she took everything, and set it on the pile.

Curtis was there. Curtis was everywhere. He had worn a cream suit and a Panama hat, and he was walking through the hall like a man running for office, shaking hands, saying, "Wait and see. Wait and see Sunday. Something special." At four o'clock Yoli cornered him by the drinks cooler.

"You have to tell me."

Curtis looked around. He did a little dance of a look, over both shoulders.

"Yoli, Yoli, Yoli." He put a hand on her arm. "You know what your problem is? You like to control things."

"Curtis."

"Trust me."

"Did he come?"

"He's here. He's right here in Toronto. He been in Toronto since Thursday."

Something happened in Yoli's chest, a sort of dropping, like a step you don't see. "He's been here since Thursday."

"Thursday night. I pick him up at Pearson, he come in on the JetBlue." Curtis shook his head, marveling at himself. "We went for doubles Friday morning. In my car. That man talk for two hours straight."

"Does Mama D know?"

"Nobody know! That's the whole thing!" Curtis looked like a man in love. "That's the whole point. Lou, hear me — the man is lonely. Twenty-two years. He thought everybody hate him. I told him, no, no, no, they want you home, come for the birthday, we go surprise everybody." He beamed. "At five fifteen. Before the cake. I have a slideshow."

Yoli said, "You told him we want him home."

"I told him—"

"Did you ask anyone? Did you ask Maggie? Did you ask Ann-Marie?"

Curtis's face did a complicated thing. He looked past her, at the hall, at the food, at the three generations of Espinets eating rice off paper plates, and his expression was not triumphant anymore. It was the face of a man who had spent two weeks building something out of what he hoped was true.

"Yoli," he said, softly, "the man is my brother."

"You could have said something. To me. One thing."

"You would have said no."

"Yes," Yoli said. "I would have said no."

---

She went out to the parking lot at five o'clock and threw up, quietly, behind her own car, in the sort of way that is not really throwing up but is close enough. Then she went back in and washed her hands and checked the ice.

At 5:10 Curtis was at the front of the hall with a microphone he'd gotten from somewhere, tapping it, and the tap came out of the loudspeaker like a gunshot, and everyone looked up from their food.

"Family," Curtis said.

"Family," he said again, and the whole hall went, in unison, "Eh eh," and put down their forks.

Kareem stopped playing scales. The AC hummed.

Maggie, in cream, folded her arms. Ann-Marie, in cream, folded her arms. Pearl, who did not know anything was happening, kept eating.

"Sixty years ago — I'm joking, Mummy, I'm joking" — a roll of laughter — "the Espinet family come out of a house in San Fernando with nothing but a suitcase and a little piece of land and a mother who would fight the devil for her children. That woman is here tonight. Dolores Espinet. Mama D. Eighty years."

Everyone clapped. Mama D, in her maroon, in the front, with her pocketbook on her lap, waved a hand like a queen who has had enough of it.

"But tonight," Curtis said, "tonight—"

And Yoli, standing by the drinks cooler with a plastic cup of mauby, watched her uncle's face change, and she understood abruptly that Curtis had rehearsed this part and had not rehearsed this part well, and that he was now, in real time, deciding whether to keep going.

"Tonight," Curtis said, "there's somebody coming home."

Maggie said, "What."

"He come a long way. He been away a long time. He made mistakes. We all make mistakes. But he is my brother and I bring him home."

The hall had gone completely silent. There were sixty-one people in it and none of them were chewing.

And then Kareem, who had gotten the signal and was, to give the boy credit, doing his level best, started up on the tenor pan. He was playing "Happy Birthday." He was playing it slowly, and with feeling, and it was the wrong song for the moment and everybody knew it, and Yoli watched her cousin Damian lurch toward the back of the hall with his phone up, filming, because Curtis had asked him to.

The doors at the back of the hall were open. Outside it was still bright, that long August evening light, and there was a person in it.

Yoli watched the doorway.

She had spent two days and two nights building a picture of that door. She had not managed to build a very good one. The man who walked through it was smaller than she'd made him, and thinner, and grayer, and he was wearing a dark suit that did not fit him well and he had a bouquet of flowers in his hand, supermarket flowers, wrapped in plastic, and he was holding them in front of him the way you hold a shield.

He was crying already. Which she had not built into the picture at all.

The room made a sound. It wasn't a scream, exactly. It was sixty-one people all making the same noise at the same time, a sound like a whole room sucking in air, and then, from the front, loud and high, Maggie screamed for real — a short, sharp, theater-scream — and dropped the punch ladle she'd been holding.

The punch went across the table. It went across the front of Ann-Marie's cream linen. Ann-Marie said, "Oh my God in heaven—"

And then the lights went out.

---

They went out because of the breakers, which had been holding a lot of things they did not want to hold: three rice cookers, the coffee urn, the DJ's setup, Kareem's amplifier, the chafing dishes, the industrial AC. Yoli learned about the breakers later. In the dark, what she knew was that sixty-one people had become sixty-one voices in a windowless room.

Somebody knocked over a stack of plates. A child started crying, and then another child. Aunt Pearl said, "Lord, Lord, Lord." Somebody on the far side of the room said, "Where he is? Where the man?" which was the first moment Yoli realized that some of the people in this room had been waiting for this and did not know who it was yet, and that others had known immediately.

She did not run to the door. She ran to the front.

She had her purse on her arm. In her purse was a small canister of nitroglycerin spray and a folded card with the dosage on it in her own handwriting.

Her grandmother was in the dark, in her own chair, and Yoli found her by the sleeve of the maroon two-piece and put her hand on her chest and felt the breathing, which was fast, shallow, ragged, and she said, very calmly, "Mama, it's me. It's Yolanda. Open your mouth."

"Girl—"

"Open your mouth. Now."

She put the spray under her tongue. She crouched in front of the chair. Around them the whole hall was a storm. Above her head, Curtis was shouting, "It's the fuse! Somebody get the man, it's the fuse!" and somewhere Damian's phone light swung across the ceiling in a wild arc and caught for one second the face of a twelve-year-old cousin, tear-streaked and open-mouthed and enormously alive.

"Breathe in," Yoli said to her grandmother. "Slow. I'm right here. Slow."

And then the lights came back.

The whole room was suddenly yellow and everyone was looking at everyone else, and the caretaker, Mr. Fonseca, a small Portuguese man in a windbreaker, was standing by the panel at the side of the hall with his hand still up on the breaker, looking at the room with the expression of a man who wished he'd stayed home.

Mama D's breathing eased. She looked over Yoli's shoulder.

Ralph Espinet was standing in the middle of the hall.

---

The flowers had gotten crushed. The plastic was torn and the petals were scattered on the tile around him and he was holding what was left of them down at his side, and there was punch on his sleeve, and he stood there in the middle of that room while the whole family looked at him.

Then Maggie said, "Twenty-two years."

And it started.

Yoli had prepared herself for the fight. She had prepared herself for somebody to say something and somebody to answer, for the speeches that had been waiting, salted away, all these years, and for these people, whom she loved, who had raised her, to say out loud in front of each other the things she had heard said quietly in kitchens. She had prepared herself for Maggie.

But what she had not prepared for was her father.

"Twenty-two years," Maggie said. "You didn't come to your own father funeral."

"You think I—" Ralph's voice came out wrong, high, and he stopped and tried again. "Maggie, I did not get one phone call from this family telling me my father was dying. Not one. I find out from a man on a job site in Brooklyn."

Ann-Marie said, "You think we didn't call you? We called you twelve times—"

"To tell me to come home and behave myself. Yes. Very good. I know." He was shaking. "Your father tell me in 2002, don't come back to this house. Ask your mother. Ask her."

Yoli's hands were cold. She looked at her grandmother.

Everybody looked at her grandmother.

Mama D sat in the front of the hall with the taste of nitroglycerin in her mouth and her pocketbook on her lap. She did not look at Ralph. She looked past him, at the door, at the parking lot, at the light coming in, exactly the way she'd looked at her bedroom window two days before.

"Sonny had a temper," she said. "I don't know what he tell you. I wasn't there."

"You know what he tell me."

"I know what he tell me," Mama D said, and her voice was very flat. "That's a different thing."

And Ralph Espinet, standing in the middle of a room full of his own family in a suit that didn't fit him, put his hand over his eyes.

It went on for a while longer. Yoli stopped following every line of it. She did what she had been doing since the doors opened, which was standing in one place and watching the whole machine work — Ann-Marie, who had never once in her life been angry out loud, being angry out loud; Pearl, who had been eating, now quietly crying into her napkin; her mother, Bernadette, at the back with Dex, whose presence had gotten lost in the shuffle and who was now the only person in the room holding a plate. Simone got up and started moving the chafing dishes to the back so that at least there was something useful happening. Damian lowered the phone. He had stopped filming some time ago. Yoli loved him a little for that.

And Curtis stood at the front with the microphone down at his side, forgotten, and did not say another word on it. Not one. He stood there with the Panama hat and the cream suit in the noise, and Yoli watched her uncle and understood that he had not known. He had not known and he had not asked and he had done it anyway, out of love, which is a thing that has wrecked more afternoons than malice ever has.

At some point the arguing ran out of fuel, the way arguments do when everybody in them is too related to each other to actually win. People went back to the food. The food was good. That was the other thing nobody ever says about a family in a crisis — that the macaroni pie was, in fact, excellent.

By seven o'clock the temperature in the room had dropped a degree. Someone put the music back on, low. Kareem, who had survived the whole thing standing very still behind his tenor pan with the pan mallets in his hands, was coaxed back into playing by a couple of the younger cousins and he did, and the metal sound went up into the room and made it a hall again instead of a wound.

Yoli found her father on the loading dock behind the hall, sitting on an upturned milk crate with a paper plate on his knees.

The loading dock faced east, over the parking lot and a stand of scrubby trees and the back of a strip mall. The light was finally going. She had never once in her life been alone with him. She had been fourteen when he left. She had a memory of him in a yellow T-shirt, laughing at something on TV. That was the entire file.

She sat down on the next crate over.

Nobody said anything for a minute. He was eating rice with a fork. She'd gotten him the plate. She'd gone and gotten a plate and put food on it and walked out to the loading dock and set it down on the concrete next to him, and he had looked up at her like she'd handed him something enormous.

"I'm Yolanda," she'd said. And he'd said, "I know."

Now he said, "You're the one who has the spray."

"She has angina. She's had it three years."

"You carry it."

"Somebody has to," Yoli said.

He nodded slowly. He had a paper napkin on his knee and he wiped his hands on it with tremendous care.

"I didn't know about her heart," he said.

"No."

"I call at Christmas."

"I know. I'm the one who hands her the phone."

That landed. He stopped with the fork halfway. He looked at the parking lot.

"Yolanda," he said. "I want you to know something, and I don't know how to say it, so I'm just going to say it." He put the fork down on the plate. "I have been trying to come back for eleven years. Every year I think, this year, and then it gets to be January and I'm not there and I think, well. Now it's another year, and now I owe them eleven. And the number gets bigger and it gets worse and it gets harder to pick up a phone." He laughed, without any humor at all. "It doesn't get smaller. That's the thing nobody tells you. It doesn't get smaller, it only gets bigger."

Yoli did not say anything. She looked out at the trees.

"Curtis told me they wanted me home," he said. "He told me on the phone, in May, he said — he said, Ralph, everybody want you home. He said it like it was a fact. Like it was something written down somewhere." He shook his head. "I should have known. But I wanted it so bad I didn't ask a single question. I got on a plane, Yolanda. Two days, I sat in his apartment on Birchmount and I did not ask one question. Because I wanted it."

Yoli said, "That's his fault." And then, because it was also true, "It's also not his fault."

"No," Ralph said. "It's also not his fault."

The door of the hall opened behind them and Mama D came out.

She was slow on the step; she always was now. She had her pocketbook in the crook of her arm and her cane in her other hand, and she came across the concrete of the loading dock in the last of the day in the maroon two-piece and did not look at Ralph at all until she was standing in front of him.

Yoli got up.

"Yolanda, go inside," her grandmother said.

"Mama—"

"Go inside. Tell Ann-Marie to stop crying before her face swell. Go."

So Yoli went. She went to the door and she opened it and she looked back once, and her grandmother was lowering herself, with great dignity and some difficulty, onto a milk crate next to her son.

---

By nine the old people were leaving and by ten the hall was mostly three or four families and the mess. Yoli was at the sink area with a stack of chafing dishes and a trash bag when Simone came over and started drying, without being asked, which is what a good cousin is.

They worked for a while.

"She's still out there," Simone said.

"I know."

"They're just sitting."

"I know."

"They're not talking, they're just sitting." Simone put a dish down. "It's the most Trinidad thing I've ever seen in my life. Two people sitting on a loading dock saying nothing."

Yoli laughed, and it surprised her.

Her father came back in about twenty minutes later, alone. Mama D had gone back to Ann-Marie's car, and Maggie, who was driving her, was standing at the car door with her arms folded and her mouth set, and Yoli saw her father walk over to that car and say something to Margaret, and saw Maggie's folded arms stay folded, and saw her father stand there and keep talking anyway, in his bad suit, until Maggie's arms came down a little. Not open. Just down.

He came into the hall and looked around at the tables and the chairs and fifty pounds of food and the giant twelve-foot banner that Damian had hung on the back wall half an hour ago, crooked, on somebody's tape, and that nobody had looked at directly since. WELCOME HOME RALPH. Printed, Yoli had noticed, as it came out of the box, WELCOME HOME RALP — the printer had cut the H, and Curtis had decided it wasn't worth the argument, and it had been hanging there all evening with its one missing letter like a man with one shoe.

Ralph looked at it for a long time.

"Curtis," he said.

Curtis, who was stacking chairs by the wall with his hat pushed back, said, "Yes."

"You spelled my name wrong."

"I didn't do it. It's the shop," Curtis said. And then he stopped stacking, and he looked at his brother, and his face was doing that complicated thing again. "Ralph. I should have told them."

"Yes."

"You going home Wednesday?"

"Tuesday night."

"Come by before. I'll make you a proper breakfast."

Ralph did not answer right away. He looked at the banner again, and then at Yoli at the sink with the sponge in her hand.

"Your daughter gave me a list," he said. "For your mother. The doctor, the pills, the times. She wrote it out on the back of a receipt." He took it out of his pocket and held it up. "Look at this handwriting."

Curtis looked at it. "That's very good handwriting."

"It's very good," Ralph said.

Yoli turned back to the pan she was scouring and did not let them see her face.

At ten past eleven the last of it was in the car. The chairs were stacked, the food was packed into three coolers and distributed among four houses, the floor had been swept by Simone and Damian with a big push broom while their phones played from a tinny speaker propped on a folding chair. Mr. Fonseca came by to lock up and looked at the room and then at Yoli and said, in his flat accented English, "Some party."

"Sorry about the breaker."

"It always the breaker," he said. "Do it every time with the rice," and he held the door while they carried out the last cooler.

They stood in the parking lot under the pole light, all of them — Yoli and Simone and Damian and Curtis and Ralph — and the night was soft and there were still crickets going in the scrub behind the hall, and the lot smelled like hot asphalt cooling off. Somebody's speaker was still playing, forgotten on a folding table inside the locked hall. Through the glass of the door you could hear it faintly, a soca tune from a long time ago, the sort of song that makes nobody get up anymore but makes everybody a little bit happier in the car.

Ralph said, "I'll call Tuesday before my flight."

"You better," Curtis said.

"I will."

"You said that in 2016."

"I know," Ralph said. "I'm calling Tuesday."

They all stood there a second longer, and then Simone said, "Okay, I go call a Uber," and Damian said, "You have no data," and Simone said, "I have data," and Damian said, "You have no data," and they were gone into that, and Curtis went to his car with the banner tube under his arm, and Ralph went to the car Curtis had rented for him and sat in it for a minute before he turned the key.

Yoli stood next to her own car with her keys in her hand and did not get in.

Her grandmother was going to be eighty for another year. Her father was in a Nissan Sentra in a parking lot on Brimley Road. Her uncle Curtis had spent two weeks and six hundred dollars and a great deal of his own hope on the wrong shape of a good thing, and it had nearly cracked the room open, and the crack, if you looked at it in the right light, was where the air was getting in.

She got in the car.

At the far end of the lot, the Sentra's lights came on and swung around and moved off toward Ellesmere, and Yoli sat there with the engine off and the windows down and watched it go, and after a while she started the car and drove home.
