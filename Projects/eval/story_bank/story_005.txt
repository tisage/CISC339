The hornbill came down on the roof of Akua Pomaa's house a little after noon, and the whole of Duase heard the iron sheets take its weight.

Abena was at the standpipe with two basins. She knew the sound before she turned around. Every child in Duase knew it: a heavy, scraping landing, like someone dropping a sack of cassava from a height, and then the bird's call, a honk that broke in the middle the way a boy's voice breaks. Women at the standpipe went quiet. A goat stopped chewing. Across the lane, old Maame Dufie set down the plantain she was peeling and pressed both palms flat against her knees.

The bird was enormous, black, with a casque on its beak like a second, swollen beak. It walked the ridge of the roof with its claws ticking on the metal, looked down into the compound, and called again.

It was Abena's mother's roof. It was Abena's roof.

She left the basins at the standpipe. Later, someone carried them to the house for her, full, and set them by the kitchen door without saying anything, which was how people in Duase said things.

---

Her mother was sitting on the low stool in the courtyard when Abena came through the gate. Akua Pomaa was fifty-four, broad in the shoulders from forty years of carrying head-loads from the farm, and she had her cloth hitched up over her knees and a knife in one hand and a half-scraped yam in the other. She had not stopped scraping.

"It's still there," Abena said.

"I can hear it." Her mother turned the yam. A long white curl of peel dropped into the bowl. "Sit. You're standing like a policeman."

Abena did not sit. Above them, the hornbill shifted and the roof ticked.

"Ma."

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"Abena." Her mother looked up for the first time. Her face was calm in the way it had been calm at the hospital in Bekwai two years ago, when the doctor told her about the lump and that it was nothing, probably, and then that it was nothing, definitely. "Your brother has two children. Your auntie is in Accra with her husband's people and she doesn't count anymore, not to the bird. You have your work. I am the one."

"Nobody is the one."

Her mother went back to the yam. "Go and greet Opanyin Mensah. Tell him the house of Pomaa has heard."

---

The Row stood at the north edge of Duase, where the village stopped pretending and the forest began. There were forty-one trees in it. Abena had counted them as a child, and she counted them again that afternoon, walking slowly along the line with her sandals in her hand because the ground there was soft and red and she had always walked it barefoot.

Odum and wawa and mahogany, kapok with buttress roots taller than a man, a single sapele so old that its bark had gone grey and ridged like elephant skin. Each one had a name. Not a tree-name: a person's name. Kwame Ofori, who went in the year the river changed course. Afua Kobi, who went in her wedding cloth because she said she would not waste it. Yeboah the Tall, Yeboah the Short. Nana Serwaa, whose tree had a hollow at the base where children hid during games, and who was said to have been a terrible grandmother and a wonderful tree.

Every year, when the hornbill came, someone from the house it chose walked into the forest at night with white clay on their face and a calabash of palm wine in their hands. The drums played until the forest knocked back. The person did not come out. By the next rains there was a sapling at the end of the Row that had not been there before, and it grew the way no tree grew, a man's height in a season, and by the time the person's grandchildren were old enough to hide in its roots it was already huge.

The last tree in the Row was an odum, eighteen years old and as thick through the trunk as a lorry tyre. Its name was Yaw Konadu. It was Abena's father.

She sat down with her back against it. The bark was warm. Above her, the leaves moved in a wind she couldn't feel at ground level.

"The bird came," she told him. "It's Ma."

Leaves. Somewhere deeper in the forest, a monkey shouted at something and stopped.

When Abena was nine, she had watched her father put on the clay. He had made a joke while he did it, something about how at least he would finally be taller than his brother, and her mother had laughed and then gone into the bedroom and shut the door. Abena remembered that he had held her face in both his hands, clay-white, his thumbs leaving pale marks on her cheeks, and that he had smelled of the palm wine he was carrying and the chalk and his own sweat. She remembered the drums. She remembered how long it took for the forest to knock back. Hours. She had fallen asleep against her uncle's leg and woken to the sound, a single huge hollow note, as if someone had struck the inside of the world.

In nursing school in Kumasi, in her second year, she had sat with a man on the medical ward who had been brought in from a village near Obuasi with his kidneys failing. He was sixty-two. His children wanted everything done. He did not. He held Abena's wrist one night and asked her what she thought, and she had heard herself tell him about the Row. Not as a story about trees; as a way of explaining that she had grown up among people who knew that a life had a shape, and that one could choose the end of it. He had died four days later on dialysis, with his children around him, and she had cried in the sluice room afterward, and she still did not know whose side she had been on.

"I'm not going to let her," she said to her father's tree.

The leaves went on moving. Trees were not good at arguments.

---

Opanyin Kwadwo Mensah received her on his veranda. He was the okyeame, the linguist of Duase, which meant that when the chief was too old to talk — and the chief had been too old to talk for six years — Mensah talked. He was seventy-something and still farmed, and his hands, folded on his walking stick, had the knuckles of a man who had pulled a great many weeds.

"The house of Pomaa has heard," Abena said, because her mother had told her to say it.

"Good." He nodded slowly. "Good. We will drum on Friday. I'll send Kofi to your mother about the cloth."

"And the house of Pomaa will not pay."

Mensah did not move for a moment. Then he looked at her properly, as if he had only just noticed which of the Pomaa women was standing in front of him.

"Sit down, Abena."

She sat on the bench across from him. His wife came out with water, saw her husband's face, set down the cups and went back inside.

"You've been in Kumasi a long time," Mensah said.

"Eight years."

"And they've taught you that our way is backward."

"They didn't teach me anything about our way. Nobody in Kumasi has ever heard of Duase." She hadn't meant to say it so sharply. "Do you know what I say, when people ask where I'm from? I say near Bekwai. If I say Duase, they look at me like I've said I'm from the moon. It's not on any map. The trotro drivers don't know it. When I send letters, they don't come. I have to give them to someone traveling."

"Yes," said Mensah. "That is Owura Kwae keeping us."

Owura Kwae. Mister Forest. Half a joke, the way people in Duase said it, and half not. The landlord. The Owner of the trees.

"Keeping us from what?"

"From what is outside."

"I've lived outside. It has hospitals. It has a road."

"Your grandmother's grandmother," said Mensah, and he said it gently, the way you tell a child the same story for the hundredth time, "lived in a town on the Pra river. Men came from the coast with guns and men came from Kumasi with guns, and sometimes they were the same men, and whoever they caught, they sold. She ran with eleven others into the forest, and they walked for nine days, and on the ninth day something in the forest spoke to them. And it said: I will keep you. And she asked what it wanted. And it told her."

"That was two hundred years ago."

"A hundred and seventy," Mensah said. "And in all that time, the one year Duase did not pay — the year your great-great-grandfather's brother hid in the yam store because he was afraid — that year, Asante soldiers found us. They came out of the trees in the dry season and they took nine people. Nine, because one wouldn't go."

"That's a story."

"Yes. So is the bird on your roof."

She had no answer to that. She drank the water.

"Your mother is not sick," Mensah said after a while.

"No."

"She's not old."

"No."

"I understand." He sounded, unexpectedly, as if he did. "When the bird came for my brother's house, he had a son of nineteen and a wife and a farm. He went. I helped him put on the clay." He looked out over the lane, where two boys were rolling a bicycle tyre with a stick. "Bring it to the elders tomorrow, if you want to. They'll say no. But you can say it in front of everyone, and then it will be said."

---

They said no.

They said it under the big shade tree in front of the chief's house, with the chief himself propped up in his chair under an umbrella, eyes half-closed, his mouth working on nothing. They said it with their hands and with their faces and with the long, patient speeches that elders made when they wanted you to understand that they had been patient with you for exactly as long as they intended to be. Maame Dufie said it by turning her back. Kofi Boakye, who Abena had grown up with, who played the master drum now that his father's arms were too weak, said nothing at all. He just looked at her across the circle with an expression she didn't recognize, and after a moment she understood that it was fear, and that it was not fear of the forest.

Abena's brother Kwasi had come from his house on the far side of the village with his two small daughters riding on his hips. He stood behind her and didn't speak, but he didn't leave either.

"Ask Akua Pomaa," someone said finally. "It's her bird."

Every head turned.

Her mother was sitting on the end of a bench with her hands folded in her lap. She had put on her good cloth for the meeting, the green and gold one with the pattern called *Nsoroma*, star. She looked at Abena for a long time.

"My daughter has said what our house says," she said.

There was a noise from the circle, a kind of collective intake of breath. Mensah closed his eyes.

"Akua," said one of the elders. "Think."

"I have thought," Abena's mother said. "I've been thinking since noon yesterday. I'm tired of thinking." She stood up, carefully, gathering her cloth. "Yaw Konadu went for this house eighteen years ago. I think the house of Pomaa has paid enough rent."

She walked home. After a moment, Abena followed, and then Kwasi with the girls, and the meeting broke up behind them into argument, voices rising, someone shouting, someone crying.

That night, Abena found her mother in the courtyard, sitting on the stool in the dark, looking up at the roof where the bird had been.

"Did you mean it?" Abena asked.

Her mother didn't answer for a long while.

"I'm afraid," she said finally. "I was afraid when the bird came and I'm afraid now. It's just a different fear." She reached out without looking and found Abena's hand. "This one, at least, I'm sharing."

---

On Friday the drums played anyway.

Kofi Boakye and three other men sat at the edge of the Row with the talking drums and the big bass *fontomfrom*, and they began at dusk, because that was when they always began. The village gathered behind them, the way it always gathered. There was palm wine in a calabash on a stool at the forest edge. There was a small bowl of white clay. Everything was ready for someone who would not come.

Abena stood in the gateway of her mother's house with her arms crossed. She had expected to have to bar the door. She had pictured herself standing there all night, holding her mother back; she had pictured people coming for her mother with torches, or with gentle hands, which would be worse. But her mother was sitting inside on the bed with the lamp off, and nobody came. The drums went on, steady and patient, a rhythm that meant *we are here, we are waiting*, and under it the deeper voice of the talking drum saying, over and over, the name of the house: *Pomaa, Pomaa, Pomaa*.

Around midnight, Kwasi came and stood next to her. He'd brought two plastic chairs. They sat.

"The girls asleep?"

"Esi is. Adwoa keeps asking why the drums are saying Grandma's name."

"What did you tell her?"

"That it's a song about her." He rubbed his face. "Abena. What happens if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong."

"That's not an answer, that's a—" He stopped. "It's not an answer."

She didn't have a better one. They sat there while the drums went on, and the stars turned, and the cicadas sawed at the dark.

The drums stopped an hour before dawn, not because anyone told them to but because Kofi's hands gave out. Abena heard the silence arrive. It came down on Duase like a cloth thrown over a cage. Then — she would argue about this afterward, with herself and others, about whether she had really heard it — there was a sound from the north, from the Row. Not a knock. A long, slow, exhaling rush, as if all forty-one trees had let out a breath at once.

In the morning, people who went to the Row said every leaf on every tree had turned over, so that the pale undersides faced the sky, and the whole line of them looked silver, like trees in a drought. By noon they had turned back.

That was all. Nothing else happened.

---

For nine days nothing else happened.

People were cold to Abena in the lane. Maame Dufie stopped greeting her mother. Children were called inside when she walked past, which hurt more than she expected. But the farms went on, and the rain came on the sixth day the way it should, and nobody was taken by soldiers, and Abena began to think, carefully, the way you test a healing wound, that it might be over.

On the tenth day, her phone buzzed.

She kept it in the pocket of her skirt out of habit. In eight years of coming home, it had never once had signal in Duase; the bars simply vanished at the bend in the footpath where the cocoa farms ended. She had stopped noticing it. Now it was vibrating against her thigh while she was kneeling in the yard washing her mother's cloth, and when she took it out, wet-handed, the screen showed one bar, then two, and then the messages began to come in all at once. Seventy-three of them. Her friend Comfort from the ward, asking when she was coming back. Her bank. A voicemail from the matron. A video someone had sent of a goat climbing a tree, which made her laugh out loud, alone in the yard, and then stop laughing.

She walked to the Row with the phone held out in front of her like a torch. Three bars. Four.

Her father's tree stood at the end of the line, unmoved, enormous.

"What did you do?" she asked it. Then, more quietly: "What did I do?"

---

The surveyor came out of the forest on the fourteenth day.

He came from the east, from the side of Duase where there was nothing — no path, no farms, no road for twenty kilometers, only forest — and he came with a cutlass in one hand and a yellow GPS unit in the other and two young men behind him carrying a measuring chain and a pot of red paint. He wore a reflective vest over a short-sleeved shirt, and he was sweating through both, and when he stepped out from between the trees onto the edge of Maame Dufie's cassava field and saw the houses and the smoke and the children, he stopped dead and said, in Twi, "Ei. Who are you people?"

His name was Mr. Larbi. He worked for a company called Sunshine Timber, which had a concession on the forest — a document from the Forestry Commission, stamped, signed by somebody's paramount chief in a town Abena had never heard of — and the concession map showed nothing here. No village. No farms. He kept turning the GPS over in his hands like it had betrayed him personally. "I walked this line last year," he said. "This exact line. There was nothing."

They fed him, because whatever else Duase was, it was not a place that let a stranger sit hungry. Abena's mother made him fufu and light soup with smoked fish. He ate like a man who'd been walking since five in the morning, and between mouthfuls he kept looking around at the courtyard, at the roof, at the faces watching him from the gate.

"It's a very nice place," he said finally, almost apologetically. "Very nice. I'll make a note."

He left that afternoon. On the way out, he and his two young men walked the length of the Row and looked up at the trees the way Abena had once seen a butcher at Kejetia market look at a cow.

---

What came after came fast.

A district assembly man arrived on a motorbike with a census form and a sunburned scalp and asked everyone's names and ages and was astonished, then annoyed, then suspicious, to learn that none of them had birth certificates. A pastor arrived from Bekwai with a megaphone and a plan to plant a church. A woman from a mobile network company came with a man who wanted to put up a mast, and paid the chief's family two hundred cedis just for talking to him. Sunshine Timber's bulldozer came, at the end of the third week, cutting a road from the east, red-brown and raw as a wound, and with it came lorries, and with the lorries came men.

And not all of it was bad. That was the thing Abena would think about afterward, the thing she could not make tidy. When old Opanyin Kwaku Agyei collapsed in his groundnut field with his left side gone slack and his speech slurred, it was a Sunshine Timber pickup that took him to the hospital in Bekwai, Abena kneeling in the bed of it holding his head, the driver going so fast over the new road that they both nearly went over the side twice. In Duase, before, a stroke would have been a death. Agyei came home three weeks later, walking with a stick, his mouth lopsided, cursing the hospital food. He sat on his veranda and cursed Abena too, for good measure, and then, when his daughter had gone inside, he beckoned her close and squeezed her hand very hard.

Kofi Boakye bought a phone. Abena saw him walking along the lane holding it up to his face, and when he saw her watching, he looked away, ashamed, and then looked back and held it up like a question. She didn't know the answer either.

Then, at the start of the fifth week, Sunshine Timber's men came to the Row with the red paint.

They painted a ring around each of the biggest trees, at chest height. Thirty-one of the forty-one. Kwame Ofori. Afua Kobi. Both of the Yeboahs. Nana Serwaa with her hollow. The grey sapele. And Yaw Konadu, at the end of the line, too young to be the biggest but big enough.

The village came out and watched them do it. Nobody stopped them. Nobody knew how. The men worked quickly and didn't look at anyone, and when they were finished they climbed back into their lorry and drove away down the new road, and the red rings stood out on the bark like a rash.

Maame Dufie walked up to her husband's tree — Yeboah the Short — and put her palm flat on the paint, and stood there. After a while she started to cry, a high, thin sound, like a kettle.

Abena felt the whole village not looking at her.

---

She heard her mother go out that night.

She had been lying awake on the mat listening to the lorries on the new road — they ran at night now, carrying logs from somewhere further east — and she heard the courtyard door, the scuff of her mother's sandals, the gate. She lay still for a count of ten. Then she got up and followed.

The moon was half-full. Akua Pomaa was walking north through the lanes with her cloth wrapped tight around her shoulders, and in one hand she was carrying a calabash. When she reached the Row, she knelt at its eastern end, beside her husband's tree, and set the calabash down, and began to rub white clay onto her face from a little bowl she'd hidden in her cloth.

"Ma," Abena said.

Her mother didn't startle. She kept smoothing the clay across her forehead, down over her cheekbones, the way you'd put on cream in the morning.

"Go home, Abena."

"You can't."

"I can. It's what was always going to happen. I just made it late." Her mother's voice was very steady. "It's my fault. I should never have stood up in that meeting. I let you carry it. I wanted to see what would happen if somebody said no, and now I've seen it."

"It's not your fault."

"Then it's yours, and that's worse." She finished with the clay. In the moonlight, her face was a mask, a stranger's face. She picked up the calabash. "Go home."

She walked into the forest.

Abena went after her.

---

She had been in this forest a thousand times. She had gathered snails here and firewood and *nsenia* leaves for her mother's soup. She knew the paths. But she had never been in it at night, and she had never been in it like this, following a white face that bobbed ahead of her through the dark, and within a hundred steps she understood that there were no paths anymore, or that the paths had all decided to go somewhere else.

The trees were wrong. Not frightening — wrong. They stood too evenly. The spaces between them were too regular, like columns in a church, and the further in she went, the more she had the feeling that she was not walking between trees but along something, the way a beetle walks along a sleeping animal's flank. The ground hummed faintly under her bare feet. The air was cool and thick and smelled of rain that had not fallen yet.

Ahead of her, her mother stopped.

Abena came up beside her. They were standing at the edge of a clearing she had never seen before and would never find again, a perfect round space roofed over with branches so high and dense that the moonlight came through in single threads. In the center was nothing. Absolutely nothing. A patch of bare earth. And Abena understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, that the nothing was the point, that the Owner was not in any one place in the forest but was the distance between trees, the shape of the spaces, the dark itself held in a particular way. And it had noticed them.

Her mother lifted the calabash.

*No*, said the forest.

It did not say it in one voice. It said it in forty-one voices, coming from every direction at once, from up in the canopy and from under the ground, young and old, men and women, a chord of voices that should have been a cacophony and was not. Abena heard Nana Serwaa's croak. She heard someone laugh. She heard — so close she nearly turned around to look — her father, saying *no, Pomaa, no*, in exactly the tone he'd used when her mother tried to carry too much.

Akua Pomaa's arms began to shake. The palm wine slopped over the lip of the calabash and ran down her wrists.

"Please," she said. "Keep us."

*It is too late for that.* The voices weren't angry. That was the worst of it. They sounded tired, the way Abena had heard old patients sound at three in the morning. *What is seen cannot be made unseen. They have walked here. They have measured. They have put it in their machine.*

"I'll pay," her mother said. "Take me. I'll pay double. Take me and Abena both, then—"

"Ma!"

*Put it down, Pomaa.*

Her mother's arms fell. The calabash hit the ground and rolled and lay on its side, and the palm wine soaked into the bare earth and was gone.

Abena stood there with her heart going like a bird against glass, and when she could speak, she said the only thing she could think of.

"What do you want?"

Something in the clearing shifted. The threads of moonlight leaned, all together, as if the canopy above them had been pushed by a hand.

*For a hundred and seventy years*, said the forest, said her father, said Nana Serwaa and Afua Kobi and Yeboah the Tall, *we kept you.*

A long, long pause. The ground hummed.

*Now you must keep us.*

---

They came for the trees on a Thursday, which was a good day for it, Abena thought afterward. Thursday was Yaa's day, the earth's day.

They came at seven in the morning in two lorries and a pickup, eleven men with chainsaws and ropes and wedges and a foreman named Asante who was, as it happened, from a village not far from Bekwai, and who looked at the crowd gathered along the Row with a kind of resignation, as if he had done this before in other places and had learned that it always went one of only a few ways.

The whole of Duase was standing in front of the trees.

Not in a crowd. In families. Each house had gone to its own tree in the grey dawn and stood with its back to the bark. Maame Dufie and her daughters stood in front of Yeboah the Short. Opanyin Mensah stood, leaning on his stick, in front of the kapok that was his brother. Kofi Boakye had brought the master drum and set it down in the roots of his great-grandfather's mahogany and sat behind it, not playing, just resting his hands on the skin. Old Agyei had made his daughter carry a chair to his mother's tree so he could sit, and he was sitting, with his lopsided mouth set in a line, and his stick across his knees.

At the end of the Row, in front of Yaw Konadu, Abena stood with her mother on one side and Kwasi on the other, and Kwasi's two daughters sitting on the roots behind them, sharing a bag of groundnuts.

Abena's phone was in her hand. It had been her mother's idea, in the end. In the five days since the clearing, Abena had called everyone she could think of. Comfort from the ward, who had a cousin who wrote for the *Daily Graphic*. A lawyer from Kumasi who had once been her patient, a skinny old man who had been brought in with pneumonia and had, while she changed his drip, lectured her for an hour on customary land tenure. The district assembly man, who had come back, mostly out of curiosity, and who was now standing a little apart from everyone with his census forms clutched to his chest as if they might protect him. Two of them had come. The journalist had promised to come, and hadn't, but she'd said to film everything, so Abena was filming everything.

Foreman Asante looked at the phone. He looked at the families. He looked at the red rings on the bark, thirty-one of them, some of them half-hidden now behind the bodies of children.

"Madam," he said to Abena — because, she supposed, she was the one holding the phone — "I have a paper."

"I know," she said. "So do we." The lawyer, the skinny old man, already sweating in his suit, stepped forward with a folder. Abena had no idea what was in it. She had a strong suspicion that he didn't entirely know yet either. It didn't matter. It was paper, and they had it.

Asante sighed through his nose. Behind him, one of his men pulled the starter cord on a chainsaw, just once, and it caught and roared in the morning stillness, and every bird in the Row went up at once in a great clattering cloud.

Nobody moved.

Abena felt her mother's hand find hers. Kwasi's arm was against her shoulder. Behind them, she heard Adwoa ask Esi in a whisper what the loud thing was for, and Esi say, *Shh, it's for nothing*.

The chainsaw idled. The man holding it looked at his foreman. The foreman looked at the lawyer's folder, and at the phone, and down the long line of people standing in front of the trees with their backs to the bark, not shouting, not singing, just standing, old men and pregnant women and children eating groundnuts, and then back at Abena.

Then Kofi Boakye began to play.

Not loud. A soft, patient rhythm on the master drum, the one that meant *we are here, we are waiting*. And then, under it, the talking voice, speaking names: *Ofori. Kobi. Yeboah. Serwaa. Konadu*. One after another, all the way down the Row.

Foreman Asante closed his eyes. When he opened them, he made a small, tired gesture, a flick of two fingers, and the man with the chainsaw let it die.

"I'm going to call the office," Asante said. "That's all I'm doing. I'm going to call the office."

He walked back to the pickup with his phone to his ear, and the morning was quiet again, and the birds, one at a time, came back.

---

It did not end that Thursday. Things like that do not end. There were letters, and hearings in a building in Kumasi that smelled of floor polish, and a second visit from Sunshine Timber with more men and a policeman, who stood at the edge of the Row for an hour looking increasingly embarrassed and then went home. There was an article in the *Graphic*, small, on page eleven, with a blurry photo of Opanyin Mensah. The mast went up. The church went up, a little blue building with a tin roof that the pastor's congregation, all four of them, painted themselves. Duase acquired, for the first time in its history, a name on a map — spelled wrong.

Abena did not go back to Kumasi that year. She wrote to the matron and the matron wrote back, and they argued, and eventually the district health directorate agreed to post a community health nurse to Duase on a trial basis, and when the letter came — delivered, astonishingly, by a postman on a motorbike — it had her name on it.

When the end of the harmattan came again, and the season of the Counting with it, the village waited.

No hornbill came. There were hornbills in the forest, everyone heard them, but none of them landed on a roof. Days passed. People watched the sky.

On the night when the drums would have played, Kofi Boakye went to the Row at dusk anyway, and sat down at the master drum, and began. After a while, others came. Not to watch: to stay. They brought mats and lamps and pots of soup. They sat down in the roots of their family trees, the grandmothers and the farmers and the children who were supposed to be in bed, and they kept the drums going in shifts, and they talked, and some of them slept. Nobody walked into the forest. Nobody needed to.

Abena sat with her back against her father's tree. Her mother was asleep beside her with her head on a folded cloth. Somewhere to the east, very faint, she could hear the grinding of a lorry on the new road, carrying logs from some other forest, some other place, one that did not have a Row, or had not had one for long enough. The phone in her lap showed four bars.

Toward dawn, the drums slowed. The lamps had burned low. The whole line of trees stood dark against a sky going slowly grey, and then — she was sure of it this time, she would not argue with herself about it later — every leaf in the Row turned over at once, silver side up, and stayed like that, shining, for as long as it takes to breathe in and out.

Then they turned back, and the light came, and Duase woke up where it was.
