When Akua first saw the house from the road, she thought of an old tooth pulled halfway from a jaw—pale clapboard that had been scrubbed raw by salt and sun, its shutters jagged with age. It sat on the highest part of the cliff where the land shrugged itself into the sea, a crooked house with a wide verandah overlooking Sankofa Bay. People called it Nsoroma House, the House of Stars, because lights sometimes climbed its windows at night, like a flock rising against the dark. Other people called it the House of Names, because they said it held names like it held the stories that gave them weight, and that if you walked in and left without calling something by its true name, the house would keep it.

Akua was twenty-six, newly returned from Accra with the kind of suitcase one only carries for a while: a pair of trousers, a heap of shirts, a few notebooks, and a flattened photograph wrapped in a page torn from a ledger. The photograph had been tucked into the cardboard of the envelope her mother had sent with plantain and gari—small domestic gifts for a daughter who had been away too long. The photograph was the thing she carried like a secret: a man beside a motorcycle, an easy grin, the teeth a gap in the middle that made him look younger than he was. He was her father.

She grew up on a dry diet of stories told in hushed lines between mouthfuls. Her mother, Maame Serwaa, had been careful about the facts. Akua would ask what happened, and Maame Serwaa would steady the frying pan and say, "The sea keeps some things, and the land keeps some things. We do not know which is kinder." Then she would fill the hour with cooking and the radio and work so that asking felt like a breach. When Akua was ten, the man in the photograph—Agya Kojo Mensah—had taken a job in Keta, or Accra, or possibly both. The rumor moved like smoke: he had been taken by the sea; he had run; he had been swallowed. Officially, he had simply gone. Maame Serwaa folded grief into rice and pressed it into shapes, and then she taught Akua to make palm wine cups from coconut shells, taught her the exact bend of a gathering hand so that people would not say how lonely she was.

Sankofa Bay had memory built into it. It was a place where the sea crept in and left things: bottles with messages, names carved on driftwood, pieces of cloth threaded through mangrove roots. At the head of the bay, where sand became rock, Nsoroma House squatted and kept watch. Growing up, Akua had been told the house was where the town put its things it could no longer bear to remember—the quarrels that had broken families, the debts that had swallowed men like traps, the children who had been given away. At night, kids dared each other to touch the gate. Older men told a sonorous, slightly different history every week at the beach bar. The house had made a reputation out of selective forgetting.

On the day she returned, the sky was the color of old brass and the air smelled of roasted corn and the sea. Her phone, which had stopped when she left, suddenly filled with messages from friends who had been her companions at the university—a cluster of folk from completely different lives who had become the same through shared rooms and shared exams and cheap beer. There was a thread called "NIGHT @ HOUSE" where Kofi had sent three skull emojis followed by a photograph of the house, taken days earlier by a drone. Under the photograph Kofi had written, "One night. Who's scared?"

Akua almost did not answer. There is a certain selfishness to returning home: it is a place where you can find your feet without asking them to dance. But secrets take their toll in silence. For months she had dreamed of names that would fit into the gash where her father once was, and spending one night in a place known for keeping names felt less like a dare and more like necessity. She told herself she wanted to see if the house knew anything about him. It was an excuse for the quiet question she had been carrying like fruit in a sack.

She walked to the market to find kola nuts, because this would be a night for ritual as much as bravado. At the stall an old woman wrapped a string of red beads around the nuts and laughed as Akua inspected them. "For strength," she said in Twi. Akua bought two for each of them and tucked them into a pocket.

The group reconvened at dusk behind the fishing sheds, where the town's light pooled in the varnished hulls. There was Kofi—broad-shouldered, forever halfway into a joke—carrying a thermos of gin someone had confiscated from a festival. Yaa was there with a lantern that smelled of oil and citrus; Yaa had the flat stare of someone who had to keep score. Kojo, who thought of the world in lists and answers, had a pack of cigarettes and a notebook. Efia came with her guitar slung like a human lance; Efia's songs could make even a stone feel less lonely. And Nii Ato arrived last bouncing a pair of fireworks he’d smuggled home from Tema.

They all laughed like a bargain being made the moment they looked at the house. The verandah lay in shadows like the lip of a mouth. The gate was rusted, and the lock hung from it like jewelry. There was a sign nailed to the front announcing the owner's name—Nana Bekoe—but the wood was old and the letters blurred. Nobody alive paid rent there anymore, except for the wind.

They entered with the clumsy ceremony of people who are friends and who also imagine themselves brave. For the first hour it was all a party: they drank from the thermos and ate roasted maize, singing, clapping, and testing the floorboards with their weight. Efia's guitar filled the rooms with a crooked melody that made the old shadows lean in and listen. Kofi told a ghost story he had bought online and adapted to fit the house, about a thrown-away son who would come back to reclaim names. They grew louder, as if noise could be a halo warding off the night's thin teeth.

Then, when the bottle was emptier and the town's distant reggae fell away, the house began to answer them in a quieter voice. It started with the sound of footsteps across the room above—the heavy tread of a man walking from the far end. Kojo swore he felt a draft like a hand across his neck. A light, small and steady as a firefly, danced at the far end of the verandah. Nii Ato, with the firework in his pocket, swore it was someone with a torch. They called up, "Hello?" with the confidence of people accusing a stranger of theft, but the sound they received was the thin, parcelled hush of the house.

They found a stair that gave a sigh as they took it, and at the top they found a corridor lined with doors the color of old lemons. The rooms held furniture that had been allowed to sleep in the dust: a child's cot missing its mattress, a wardrobe with a crack like a closed mouth. On a desk lay a fountain pen, rusted but intact, a pile of envelopes tied with string. Efia, who had hands practiced by baths and chord changes, lifted the string and unfolded a letter written in a hand that had once been young. The letter was addressed to "Brother," and it spoke of a debt, of a ship, and of promises that sometimes only the sea kept. It ended with a name—Kwaku—and the ink had been splashed with salt.

Akua's fingers were going numb. She kept thinking of her father, of the way he had hummed under his breath when he was fixing something, the way he had tucked his thumb under the strap of his satchel. She had expected to be resolute and logical. It was comfortable to be that way. The house made nonsense happen slowly. A photograph pinned to a wall by a nail—an older, sepia print of a group of men in suits—had, in its bottom right, a thin curl that looked like her father's grin.

She walked door to door, her friends making their explorations like a pack of small, brave animals. The sound of the sea was low, an old clock that had stopped but continued to tick in memory. On a table in what might have been a study she found a battered cassette player and a cassette labelled with a name she almost did not read: KOJO M. MENSAH. Her chest tightened because Mensah was the same family name she bore, and because Kojo herself was one of the names that had once stretched along the town's palm-wood benches. When she pressed play, the tape filled the room with a man's voice—a man's laugh—then a song. It was a reel of a voice she knew as well as she knew the outline of a storm cloud: her father, treble and raw, singing a line about the sea and a woman who kept the pot warm. The song was old and private, but hearing it in that place made it tangible and ugly and beautiful all at once. It made the absence sensible.

"That's your father," Kojo said; his eyes were bright as if his generation had found a god in a cassette player. He spoke with a kind of precise wonder reserved for people who think histories are lists to be checked off.

Akua did not answer. She knelt, braced a hand on the cassette deck, and listened as the voice of a man she had half-remembered filled the air like smoke. On the tape he spoke about leaving: he spoke of a job in Tema that would pay more than the fishing, he spoke of a sister who needed teeth pulled, and he spoke of a debt in the back of his throat that he could not swallow. The voice was steady, the sort that tied your troubles into knots and told you to keep walking until the rope frayed.

It is brittle to expect certainty of a place like that. For every answer the house gave, it gave two doors that closed without explanation. There was a trunk under a bed, half-swallowed by shadow. When Nii Ato forced the lock with a borrowed crowbar, the hinges sighed. Inside were objects layered with other people's choices: a sailor's cap that smelled of the sea and old sweat, children's shoes, a ledger fat with names. The ledger was filled in neat columns: date, name, reason. Reason was a handwritten word that soured in its brevity—"gone," "left," "sank," "owed." Next to one name, there was a note in a different hand, hurried and pinched: "Take care of the ones she left. —N." The page had a smear of something brown and dried.

They read the ledger like thieves reading a will. Each of them found names that opened like doors, and each name had its own small, humiliating story. Kofi's grandmother was listed as having "left" in 1983, a note that someone had made no further explanation of. Yaa's name had been there, too, a young girl who had been given away and whose line had ended. The house, if it was a closet for memories, had a room of things the town didn't mention in broad daylight.

At some point, laughter stopped being an armor and turned into a language like prayer. Efia had been quiet for a long time; now she lit a candle and set it in the little room where the wind came in thin as a blade. The flame shivered and steadied.

Someone started to hum. A small tune slid into the air like someone calling a bead of memory into being. The house replied by opening a window—not physically—but in the way houses hold space, with the air going cold and the shadows rearranging themselves. Akua felt a presence at her back that made her neck prickle. She turned and saw, for a heartbeat, the reflection of a man in the darkened glass. He wore a motorcycle jacket, his smile wide in the same gap-toothed way she remembered. For one breath the reflection was him, standing where he used to stand in the kitchen of a house several miles away. Akua reached out, and the glass was nothing but glass.

"Akua?" Yaa's voice came like a careful wish. "Are you all right?"

Akua could not answer because she was thinking of the way her mother had folded cloth and stuffed it into drawers, the way she had tried to make a life that the town would not pity. Memory is a wild thing. It surfaces whether you call it or not.

They slept in fits and starts. The house was good at making small realities. At three in the morning, Kojo woke them all with a shout. He had been to the kitchen. There, beneath jars with labels in a handwriting from another era, he had found a stack of photographs sewn together with thread. Each photograph was of a person standing on a shore, one foot on sand, the other almost falling forward. They had a look on their faces that was not quite pain and not quite joy, more like the exact stare of a person who has been asked to remember something they would rather not. When Kojo laid them on the table, Akua recognized the man in the third photograph—her father—standing beside a child who had her eyes.

"No," she said, but the word folded back into her chest because she had never learned what to do with a living image of a ghost. Here he was, smiling in a setting that smelled of salt and diesel, her father's hand thrown over a boy, as if the photo were announcing heirs.

The house did something then that had nothing to do with the television dramas of their youth. It did not scream or make them see rotten faces. Instead it wound them around a story that would not be told plainly. There came a slow sound from the verandah: a rhythm of fingers on wood, as if somebody had been sitting there and tapping out a code. They went out and found a small wooden boat tucked under the overhang—a child's boat with paint gone pale. Its hull was gouged with the initials K. M. M.

On the boat lay a child's shoe the color of mango skin and inside the shoe, wrapped in a piece of cloth, a folded page. When Akua opened the page, the paper released a scent like tobacco and lilac and a promise that used to smell like certain kinds of mornings. It was a list of names—names they recognized—and next to one his name, in a handwriting like a man who paused often, was the word "SAVED."

Akua wanted a rational explanation. She wanted a story that had tidy pain and a tidy hero. The house would not be tidy. It offered instead a collage of things that had happened, items that slotted into each other like mismatched teeth, and between them it left the slimmest cracks through which an escape could be glimpsed. In one of those cracks she saw an event in wisps: a man at a dock, orders shouted, a crowd, a crate sliding—then a slip, a splash, someone reaching. The flash was too small to be truth and too bright to be a lie.

"Maybe he saved someone," Kofi said, perhaps reading what they all wanted to read. "Or maybe he was saved. Or he was the one who couldn't be named."

Akua's throat closed. She had been carrying stories that made sense because they were the dull, safe kinds. A man who ran is easier than a man who wades deep into the dark for someone else and is changed by what he touches. Her father, she had thought, was simply a hole that could be filled by rumor. Now the ledger and the tape and the photo made a mosaic that did not fit into empty spaces. He was a part of a sequence of choices, some brave, some foolish. The house had, in its slow indifferent way, forced her to see the shape of him as a person with hands and debts and epiphanies.

The old woman found them at the kitchen table, or perhaps the house led them to her as a matter of hospitality. She was sitting in the doorway as though she had been waiting for a long time and had the right to be there. Her skin was the color of roasted yam. Her hair was a white halo. She wore beads that jangled like small bells when she moved. They called her Maame Naa because everyone did; children folded their knees when she walked by. She was the kind of person the town had left unremarked for years, a seamstress of other people's memories.

"You should not be here," she said, but there was neither judgment nor scolding. Her voice was a basket of old proverbs. "This house is greedy."

"We're just staying for the night," Kofi said, as though the house were a guesthouse on the beach. "We didn't mean—"

"Names are not things to be left on tables," Maame Naa said. She looked at Akua with a long, bright attention. "You carry a heavy one."

Akua did not answer. It felt like the moment before the sea breaks, when sounds tighten into one high note. Maame Naa moved without hurry, took the cassette from Akua's hand, and sat so Akua could see her face close. The lines at her mouth were not there from worry but from laughter, because people laugh a great deal to keep the world from falling apart.

"This house keeps names people do not want," she said. "It keeps names like they are coins. But the house is not cruel. It is a place for things the town promises to forget. Men did business here. Women left babies on the steps. Children were given to other towns. Names pile like fish in a basket, and at night, they speak to each other."

"Is my father here?" Akua asked, the words at once an admission and a demand.

Maame Naa's fingers touched the cassette player and the thread of the photo as though she were reading seams on fabric. "He is here in pieces," she said. "Names do not sit in one room forever. They move. They trade places."

"Then tell me," Akua said. "Tell me in a way that men and women like the government and the sea could understand. Tell me if he is alive."

Maame Naa held her gaze, and for a moment Akua saw towns unfold: men carrying things on their heads, a woman falling asleep mid-humming, a child making pap with too little water. "There is a thing," she said slowly. "A way names are kept. The house ties them in bundles. Sometimes names are rescued. Sometimes names are given away. Your father—he saved a name and, perhaps, he gave one away. He did not drown because he could not swim. He was not taken by the sea because seas do not take those who listen. He went into a place where people unspoke their names to survive. He returned with a coin that was not his. That is all that a small woman like me can say."

It was not the clear answer Akua wanted. It was not a map she could follow to a graveyard or a courthouse. It was something porous and jagged. The honesty of Maame Naa was that she offered an observation. The house, she said, was not fate. It was a repository. It archived what the town refused to hold.

They made no tidy procession of conclusions that night. Instead, one by one, each of them went to their private corners and had small, private reckonings. Kofi found a shoebox in the attic full of newspaper clippings—names of men lost to the mines; his own name in a request for money for a funeral. He sat on the floor and read the clippings until dawn carved its thin fingers through the shutters, and he cried in a way loud men do, with sobs that became a new way of laughing.

Yaa found a ledger page where someone had written, in a child's squiggle, the promise to never leave. She held the paper as you might hold a sleeping child, and she imagined a version of herself who had never become someone who kept lists for other people.

Efia took the guitar to the verandah and played until the strings hummed like a net catching stars. Her songs braided the names together, and for a while the house listened and ticked like a satisfied clock. The river of names outside the windows did not stop. People had not changed the house's habits in a generation.

Akua, when she could not bear staying inside any longer, walked the path that threaded down to the edge of the bay. The night was sharp with salt. The moon kept statistics in silver: highs and lows, the slow incline of waves. She had on the collar of her father's jacket; it smelled of oil and faint rain. She held it like a talisman. At the waterline, where the sand was glass-smooth, she knelt and drew a circle with her shell. She framed a name with thumb and forefinger. If one whispered a name to the sea, would it echo back? Would the beach return what it had taken?

"Akua," said a voice.

She turned. It was Maame Naa again, a lantern in her hand, and beside her a bundle tied in cloth. The old woman did not look surprised to see the girl at the waterline. She seemed, in fact, unsurprised by anything at all.

"Do you know how the house works?" Akua asked.

Maame Naa squatted beside her and untied the bundle. Inside lay a small driftwood charm carved in the shape of a boat. Its grain was worn soft. "Names can be kept because someone is willing to keep them," she said. "There are things that require looking after—memories with mouths. Someone must water them. That is the guard's job."

"Is it a person?" Akua said, incredulous. "Who would keep a house like this? You?"

"Who else?" Maame Naa said and laughed like a lid being tilted off a pot. "No. I keep them because when I was young, I went into the house and found names lying like stolen eggs. I wrapped them in cloth and I promised I would look after them. Promises are stronger than laws in some towns. We wrap names so they do not scatter. Sometimes people come to take what is theirs. Sometimes they do not. That is all."

Akua wanted to argue. She wanted to ask the sort of pointed questions that make order out of fog. Instead she asked a smaller question, the one that had folded her up for years. "Is my father— did he do something to be kept?"

Maame Naa looked at her like a woman who had seen many small lives and many choices. "He did something," she said. "He held someone else's name. He could not throw it away. Holding other people's names keeps a man from living only for himself. Sometimes that is a noble thing. Sometimes that is a foolish thing. The house does not judge. It only keeps."

"Why would anyone give away a name?" Akua asked, because the idea of a person giving away a piece of themselves felt worse than any punishment.

"Because there were not enough mouths to feed," Maame Naa said. "Because a woman fell ill and a child had to be left with another family. Because loyalty can be complicated. Because sometimes the water rose too quickly. Because hearts do not always do the right thing at the right time. The house keeps what hearts cannot carry."

They sat knee to knee in the sand and the tide measured everything with the patience of an old ruler. Akua thought of her mother stirring pots and of the quiet way she had re-shelled her grief. She thought of the ledger—the names like ledger marks on a life—and of the cassette tape that had been a container of her father's voice. She had wanted the neatness of a map: where is he? who took him? What happened? Instead there were half-truths braided with choices. It was both smaller and larger than what she had feared.

"Can we take something?" she asked Maame Naa at last. "Something that will prove what happened?"

Maame Naa reached into the bundle and pulled out a small sheet of paper. On it was written a name in a different hand: "Agya Kojo Mensah." The handwriting slanted the way her father's did. Beneath was a crusted note: "Took the boat to save Ibukun. Returned with a child's shoe and one more name. Left again to keep the balance."

Akua's fingers closed over the note so hard it crumpled. The details were few—less like confession than like a photograph of an instant—but it was proof enough that her father had been an actor in something neither wholly noble nor wholly base. He had been in the middle of a story. He had been close enough to the sea to feel the spray.

"Why would he leave again?" Akua asked. Her voice was thin and brittle. She thought: would she want to leave if she had gone away to keep something and returned with the proof and then realized the proof meant she could not go home? What would she do if she found that the man she loved had chosen the life of a man who would always be half at sea?

"People leave because the balance requires it," Maame Naa said. "When you hold a thing that is not yours, you have to keep its weight. Some cannot. They go to places where their name will not be needed. They think it is mercy. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is not."

Akua had come for closure. She had imagined a tidy revelation and a tidy result. The house refused tidy. It offered instead a rawness that tasted of sweat and old mango. She held the paper like a small lantern.

"You can take this," Maame Naa said. "But remember, names are not trinkets. If you carry him now into the town, he will be a living thing there, and people will have to decide whether they will keep him for more than a night. Keeping is a long business."

Akua thought of Maame Serwaa, of the way the woman had folded grief like clothing into a drawer. She thought of the nights she had slept alone in the house in Accra, of all the small things there were to fill with the name of a person. She thought of the photograph that had begun this journey. She chose, instead of taking the paper as triumph, to fold it once and tuck it into her shirt near the heart. She would not parade a story and expect it to satisfy. She would bring it back and place it where it would be seen and then let the town decide. That was a responsibility that felt like an heirloom.

Dawn came with thin fingers, the kind that untie knots instead of breaking them. They left the house in a silence that had been earned. No one stole anything except for small things the house offered willingly: a button, a scrap of cloth, the name on a piece of paper. The town took them back without fuss. The ledger was the house's to keep. The house did not fling itself into the sky. It stayed, a tooth in the jaw of the cliff, and its windows glittered with a tired, satisfied light as if it had been told a thing it liked to hear.

They scattered afterwards like flung seeds. Kofi walked the beach with his shoebox under his arm until the tears had washed his face clean enough that laughter could settle into it again. Yaa went to the chief's office and handed over a page from the ledger to a man whose face had been in many town photographs. Efia played a new song at the market about taking names and carrying them like fire; it became a thing that people hummed while they shucked cocoa. Kojo sent a message to his father that night, the message that had sat unread in his phone for months. It was a small, hasty amends that tasted of salt.

Akua went home with the folded paper next to her skin. Her mother was in the courtyard, a pot balanced on the fire, the familiar shapes of the small domestic world the woman had kept with stubbornness. When Akua stood in the doorway and took the paper out of her shirt, Maame Serwaa did not cry. She took the paper with the hands of a woman who had been practicing for grief. She read it in the square of morning like someone deciphering a child's handwriting. Her face was a ledger of its own.

"He kept a child?" Maame Serwaa said finally, and it was not accusation. It was only a place to begin. The woman had lived with a man who had slid sideways out of her life, who had left knots for her to untangle. Now here was an explanation that threw more heat on the pot.

"He held a name," Akua said. "He left because it was heavy."

Maame Serwaa put the paper down on the table and went to the cupboard. She took out the photograph of the man on the motorcycle that Akua had carried with her, and she placed the two items side by side—one visual, one scriptural—like combining two halves of a remedy. She traced the teeth, the grin, the familiar crease at the eyes. She did not look away from it the way one might from something that stung.

"You tell me why you think this matters," she said finally.

Akua found herself sitting down like a child being asked to recite a lesson. "Because he was a man my mother loved," she said. "Because I thought maybe he had been taken by something awful, and maybe he had, but maybe he had been a man who had been trying to hold two things at once. Because I want to know him for what he did, not only for what his leaving did. Because the house made me see that names are shared, and sometimes that sharing is messy."

Maame Serwaa nodded, and the pot simmered with the same modest heroism it always had. There was no sermon. There was only the quiet work of making food for people who needed to keep on living, and that was an answer in itself. Akua thought of the house and the ledger and the woman who kept it; she thought that some places, like some people, are repositories, and that sometimes recovery begins by naming things out loud and choosing who will carry them for a while.

The town did not suddenly become a place of healed wounds. There were still things unsaid around the chief's table and arguments over land. Not every name found its claimant that week. But names had been pulled from the house and set in motion, and motion has a way of unsettling old habits. When people passed Nsoroma House from then on, they did not always look away. Some of them stopped and laid a hand on the gate as if greeting an old relative who had been sleeping in the sun. Children dared each other less often and listened more. Maame Naa kept the house, but she opened it more often to those who wanted to look at their own things, and the town found itself flushing small things like voices back into life.

A year later, Akua found herself standing at the edge of the bay with her father's photograph in her wallet and a small charm in her pocket that Maame Naa had given her. She had taken a job in town teaching at the primary school. It was not the life she had imagined in Accra, but it felt like glue; she liked how words could stick to each other in the classroom. Sometimes she thought of the house and the ledger and the way the cassette had played, and sometimes she thought of a particular line in a song her father had sung: "we will be the ones to name ourselves."

The name, in the end, was not a sentence. It was a thing that demanded response. It could be taken, crumpled, hidden, or wrapped and carried. That was the house's cruel and generous lesson. For Akua it meant carrying a photograph to her mother and letting its presence change the contour of their small conversations. For Kojo it meant making a phone call. For Kofi it meant returning clippings and then sitting with his grandmother to write down her stories. For Efia it meant playing a song again in the market and seeing people stop to listen. For Yaa it meant filing the page at the office where a clerk might have once been too tired to look.

Akua sometimes walked up to the cliff where Nsoroma House sat and stood for a while in the light. The house looked the way things look that have been left to themselves—true and weathered and stubborn. Sometimes there were lights in the windows, and at night the house hummed like a place that had swallowed a great many whispers and was trying to make sense of them. She could not say she had found the man who had been her father. She could not say whether he was somewhere in the world loving another life or washed into a place she could not name. But she could hold the piece of paper in her hand and feel the memory like a coin, warm from the pocket.

Names, she learned finally, were not proof alone. They were invitations. They asked you to do something in the world upon hearing them. The house had offered them up like a table and expected that someone would sit down and set more things upon it: a photograph, a song, a confession, a small kindness. That was all people had in the end: small things set on a table, and the courage to place them there in plain sight. The town had found a way to set down some of its weight, just a little, and now the house, in its patient way, kept being a house rather than a cave for ghosts.

At the end of the year, Akua went back to the house once more, not to sleep but to pass the gate. Maame Naa was there sweeping the verandah. They talked like two women who had been in the same room of a house for too long. Akua drew her fingers across the wood. "Do you ever get tired of keeping things?" she asked.

Maame Naa smiled and flicked her broom. "Some days," she said. "But names are like fish. If you leave them to rot in a basket, they will make the place smell and nothing will be left. If you keep them, you can feed a child. We are just trying to feed."

Akua pressed her ear to the verandah rail and heard the house in its slow breath. It was not an answer about one man's fate. It was a quiet truth: that people keep each other in strange ways, and that sometimes the most generous thing a town can do is to make space for the messed-up ways of being human.

She left the house with a new patience. The ledger would remain closed for a while longer, a thing that belonged to a town and a woman and a cliff. But now when she walked away she did not feel bereft in the same old animal sense. She had enough scraps to shake meaning out of. She had names to call when things grew dark. She had a photograph that would not evaporate.

Back in her kitchen, the pot simmered, and Maame Serwaa hummed while she shelled beans. The photograph sat on the table like a small altar. Sometimes Akua took it into her hands and pressed her forehead against its glossy face and whispered, "Kojo," like a prayer or a statement. The house at Nsoroma continued to do what houses do: keep, offer, close, and open. It did not give back everything. It never had. But it had shown them the shape of the holes, and that was something—no less than a truth and no more than the work to be done next.
