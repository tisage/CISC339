He woke with water on his face and the ship already leaning, and for four or five seconds he lay in his bunk thinking the air conditioning had leaked again. Then the floor came up and hit the bunk and the water on his face turned out to be water on everything, and he was up and pulling on his boilersuit with the wet still on his skin and the alarm not yet sounding, which was the worst part — no alarm. He had been at sea twenty-six years and had never once heard a general alarm sound on a ship that was actually going down. It only ever sounded afterward, in the reports.

On the stairs the list was already forty degrees and getting worse. He went up the outboard side, hand over hand on the rail, and the *Sveti Nikola* groaned the way a ship groans when its cargo has turned to soup in the hold and shifted to one side and decides, all at once, to finish the job. Iron ore fines out of Saldanha Bay, bound for Singapore, thirty-eight thousand tonnes, and somewhere in the last four hours of the gale it had liquefied and gone walking.

He got to the boat deck. Two of the Filipino ratings were already there, and the third mate, and nobody was shouting, which was worse than shouting. The starboard lifeboat was hanging in its davits at an angle that made it useless. The raft canisters on the rail were still there.

Joel Bacani, the bosun, was at the rail with an axe, forty-nine years old, three daughters in Iloilo, and he was trying to cut the lashings on the second canister because the first had gone into the water and not inflated.

"Stipe," Joel said, and then the deck went out from under them.

Later, when he had time to think about it — and he would have a great deal of time — he would remember that the water was warm. Everybody always said that. You went in expecting cold and it was warm, bathwater warm, and the night was black and full of things bumping against you that were not people.

He got to the raft. He did not know how. He was in it, and a wave put him on the floor of it, and he got the canopy up and the sea anchor out, and then he looked and there was Joel about twenty meters off with his arm up and going under. Stipe went in after him. That was the truth of it and he never told anyone, because in two minutes in that water he lost everything he had ever had — the strength in his hands, the feeling in his legs — and he got within three meters of Joel Bacani and then a swell took Joel away and Stipe lay in the water and watched the light on the water where Joel had been and it stopped being a light on the water.

He got back in the raft. He didn't know how he did that either.

There were four men in the raft when he finally got the canopy closed. By morning there were two. By the second day there was one. The other one — a Filipino oiler whose name Stipe never learned — died in the afternoon of the second day with his head in Stipe's lap, and Stipe held him until he stopped, and then, because there was nothing else to do, he let him over the side, and said the words he could remember, which were not many, and which he said wrong.

Nine days. The raft pack had a handheld VHF in a plastic bag, and a torch, and two flares, and six sachets of water, and a fishing kit. He rationed the water and drank his own urine on the sixth day and threw it up on the seventh. He saw one ship, far off, a container ship's profile with the boxes stacked above the hull, and he burned a flare and the flare went up and hung there and the ship did not change course. He watched it for an hour until it went below the horizon. He thought, clearly and without drama: *right. So that's what that is.*

On the ninth morning there was a smell of guano and green things, and at noon he came over a swell and there was black rock and a white beach under it and birds, more birds than he had ever seen, going up off the cliff in a column like smoke.

He fell out of the raft in knee-deep water and crawled up the sand and lay with his face in it and slept for what turned out to be nineteen hours.

---

The island was about six kilometers long and three wide, a ridge of black basalt with a gully on the west side that held a spring. There was a beach of black sand and above it a flat shelf of rock, and on that shelf a stone building with no roof and a chimney still standing, and beside it a rusted iron boiler the size of a car, and a line of iron rails running down into the water, green with verdigris, hanging over nothing.

There was a sign over the door, painted on a plate of iron. Most of the paint was gone. What was left said: *HOLMES & BRIGHT — GUANO — LONDON.*

He read it twice and laughed, because he had spent nine days dying of thirst and here was a sign from a company that had died of greed a hundred and thirty years before him, and the two of them were the same joke.

The spring was good. There was tussock grass and a kind of low tree with iron-hard wood and saltbush and, in the gully, ferns and a stand of taro gone wild. The birds were terns and boobies and frigatebirds, and there was a rookery on the eastern cliff that stank to heaven, and green turtles came up on the beach at night.

He drank and threw up and drank and kept it down. He ate a raw egg from a tern's nest and threw that up too, and then kept the second one down. On the second day he found a crab and ate it raw and did not throw it up, and he sat on the black rock with the shell in his hands and cried for about a minute, which he put down to salt.

On the third day, in the gully, he found the garden.

Sweet potato, in rows. Taro, in a wet patch, clearly tended. Cassava. A papaya tree that had been staked against the wind. Rows of something he did not recognize, and a fence of driftwood and fishbone and wire that had been built and rebuilt many times.

He stood there a long time and did the arithmetic. Iron rails and a Welsh chimney were one kind of thing. Rows were another kind of thing.

He went back to the spring. He took off his boilersuit, which was stiff with salt and had begun to come apart at the shoulder, and he washed in the seep, naked, shivering, and while he was doing that he looked up and there were four of them standing in a line on the ridge above him, thirty meters off, and one of them was holding a machete.

---

The one with the machete was a man of about forty, tall, with a scar that ran from his ear down under his jaw, and he did not hold it like a man who had ever used one for anything but cane. Behind him was a young man, thin, with a bad shave and a hard face. Beside them was a woman of maybe thirty in a man's shirt. And in front, slightly apart, was an older woman — short, gray at the temples, in a dress made out of a flour sack with the printing still on it, standing with her hands folded, not like someone waiting to be afraid but like someone who had already done her worrying.

The older woman said, in English, slowly: "Are you alone."

"Yes."

"How many were with you."

"Twenty-two crew. I'm the one."

"Where is your ship."

"Gone. Nine days ago. South of here, I think." He was aware of his nakedness in a way that seemed, suddenly, ridiculous. "I'm sorry. My clothes."

"Put them," she said. "Then come."

He put them on. The four of them came down the ridge in a line and did not put the machete away. They walked him up the gully past the garden and over the ridge and down into a place he had not seen from the shore, a hollow between the ridge and a spur, sheltered from the west, where there were huts built out of stone and driftwood and corrugated sheeting and blue tarpaulin, and a stone cistern cut into the rock with a wooden lid, and a fish-drying rack, and a long low building that was the old guano station's storehouse with a new roof on it.

There were people. He counted, because counting was what he did. Nine, ten, eleven, with the four of them. Two of them were children. An old man sat on a bench against the storehouse wall with a blanket over his knees. A woman with a small girl on her hip stood in a doorway. A boy of about thirteen stopped in the middle of carrying a bucket and stared at him.

Eleven people, on a rock in the middle of the Indian Ocean, with a garden and a fish rack and a chimney with a sign that said LONDON.

The older woman stopped him in the middle of the yard.

"My name is Rahwa," she said. "You will sleep there." She pointed at a hut at the edge of the settlement, on the seaward side, away from everything else. "You will not leave that place at night. In the morning you will come and work. If you go to the ridge or the beach without someone, we will know. Do you understand."

"I understand."

"What is your name."

"Stipe. Stjepan Kovačević."

"That is one name or two."

"It's two. Where I'm from you get two. I'm Croatian. From an island. Vis." He said it and watched her face and there was nothing in it. "It's in the Adriatic."

"I don't know it," Rahwa said.

"No," he said. "People generally don't."

---

They fed him that first night and every night after: fish, taro, sweet potato, a green stew of things he could not name, and once a week a tern's egg, and once, when a turtle came up and they took it, a portion of meat that he ate without asking questions.

For the first three weeks he was not a prisoner but he was not a person either. He slept in the hut at the edge. He worked. He worked well and he knew it, and he knew that working well was the only currency he had, so he spent it — on the fish rack, which was rotten at the joints, and on the cistern lid, which had warped, and on the seawall below the storehouse, where the sea had eaten into the corner of the foundation and where two men had clearly been making a bad job of it for years.

The tall man with the scar was Abdelrahim, Rahim. He was Sudanese, from Omdurman, a mason's son, and he was the fisherman and the builder and he spoke about four hundred words of English and used all four hundred on the seawall. He watched Stipe lay stone the way a man watches another man do his job, and by the fourth day he had put the machete away, and by the tenth he had begun to leave Stipe alone with the work.

The young man was Isaias. He was twenty-seven, he had been eighteen when the boat went down, and he did not speak to Stipe at all for two weeks. Then one afternoon he came and sat on the wall while Stipe was mortaring and said, "How many people in your country."

"About four million."

Isaias thought about it. "And how big."

"Small. Smaller than this island, some ways. In other ways not."

"What does that mean."

"It means it's an island. Everyone either leaves or stays and thinks about leaving. You know what that's like." He tapped the wall. "You've got the same thing here. Give it twenty years and the ones who can walk will walk and the ones who can't will talk about the ones who walked."

Isaias looked at him with an expression Stipe could not read. Then he said, "There is nowhere to walk to," and got up and left.

The old man in the blanket was Kidane. He was sixty-seven and he had maybe five percent of his sight left, a gray blur with light in it, and he sat on the bench outside the storehouse every day from mid-morning to dark, and he talked.

He talked in Tigrinya mostly, and in English when he remembered, and he held a notebook on his knee, a diver's log he said had come off a yacht — heavy paper, waterproof, the word *DIVE* printed on the cover and a table inside for depth and time, and in the table, in pencil and charcoal and once in what looked like ink made from something boiled, he had written out names.

"Read it to me," he said, the fourth week.

Stipe took the book. "It's not in letters I know."

"Then I will read it and you will hear."

He read out thirty-one names. He read them slowly, in groups, and between the groups he said things. Where they were from. What they had done before. One who had been a nurse in Kassala. One who had been at university in Khartoum and had a brother in Toronto. Two brothers from a village near Keren who both died in the first winter and were buried in the same hole because there was not enough strength in the community to dig two.

When he was finished he closed the book and held it on his knee with both hands.

"Forty-three left the coast," he said. "Twenty-two came to this shore. Eleven are here. That is not arithmetic. That is a story, and the story is not finished, and I am the one who has to hold it, and I am going blind."

"Why you?"

"Because I can't do anything else," Kidane said, "and because I am the oldest, and because I am not afraid of the dead. Everybody here is afraid of the dead. You are afraid of the dead. I hear it in you."

That was the fourth week. That night Stipe lay in the hut at the edge and listened to the sea and thought about Joel Bacani's hand going under, and about the container ship that had not turned, and about the fact that he had gone into the water after Joel and had not reached him, and he thought, tiredly, that the old man had it backwards. He wasn't afraid of the dead. He was afraid of being the one who was still here.

---

The little girl was Hodan. She was nine and she had been born on the island, in the storehouse, in the rain, with Rahwa and her mother Fatuma and a woman named Sara, and she had never seen a road or a car or a house with glass in the windows or more than eleven people at once.

She decided, around the fifth week, that Stipe belonged to her.

She followed him. She corrected him in the island's language, which was a soup of Tigrinya and Arabic and Sudanese and Somali and English and about thirty words that existed nowhere else on earth. She brought him things: a shell, a bird's skull, a piece of blue glass worn round by the sea. She asked him questions in a relentless monotone — *what is snow, what is a train, what is a horse, what is a church, what is a mother* — and when he answered she did not listen, she was already asking the next one.

He taught her to skip stones because she asked what everything was and he had run out of ways to explain water. He taught her to swim, in the rock pool below the spring, and Rahwa stood on the lip of the pool the whole first time and watched, arms folded, and did not say a word.

He taught her four words of Croatian and she taught them back to him wrong every time, on purpose, and laughed. *Kruh. More. Sunce. Sun. Voda. Water. Bog.* God. She liked that one. She said it constantly and incorrectly and it made Fatuma laugh, which was a rare thing to see, and after that Stipe noticed that Hodan did the things that made her mother laugh, and he stopped teaching her words.

He asked her once, on the beach, throwing stones: "What do you call this place?"

"Holmes," she said.

"Holmes."

"From the sign. The man who writes on the sign." She pointed at the storehouse wall. "It's the name."

"It's a stupid name."

"Yes," Hodan agreed, comfortably. "It's the name."

---

The sixth week, Rahwa came to the seawall and stood and watched him for a while, and then said: "Walk with me."

They went up the ridge. It took forty minutes and she did not stop once, and at the top, where the wind came across from the east and the whole island lay below them, black and green and small, she turned and looked at him.

"Your ship," she said. "They will look for you."

"For a week. Maybe two. Then the insurer writes a letter and the company files the crew list and it's finished. Nobody looks for a second engineer for two months." He said it flatly, and then heard himself, and did not take it back.

Rahwa looked out at the water. "They searched for us," she said. "For a long time. I think."

"No," Stipe said. "They didn't."

"You don't know that."

"I know the sea."

"So do I," Rahwa said. "Now."

He let that sit. Below them a frigatebird went by, low and slow.

"In the first year," she said, "we lit fires on the ridge every night for four months. We burned everything we could carry up here. We made the fire big. In the second year we saw a ship, east, and we lit the fire, and they saw us. They turned." She held up one hand flat and then rotated it slowly, a ship coming round. "They turned toward us. For one hour. We stood down on the beach, all of us, the ones who could stand. We could see the smoke. We could see the bridge. And then they turned away, and they went on, and they did not come back, and they did not send anyone, and that is the whole of it." She put her hand down. "So I don't want to hear what you know about the sea."

Stipe said nothing.

"After that we stopped," she said. "We planted. We built the roof. Kidane wrote his book. Hodan was born. That is nine years. It is not a prison. It is a place where we are alive."

"And if a ship came now?"

"Then a ship came." Rahwa's eyes were on the water. "We would be eleven people on a rock, and then we would be eleven people in a room in a country we don't know, being asked where we are from, and they would separate us. That is what happens. They separate you and they ask you where you are from and you tell them and it isn't anywhere anymore." She turned to him. "You think I don't want to go home."

"I don't think anything."

"You think I'm a woman who has been sitting on a rock for nine years and gone strange. I was a teacher in Asmara. I had a husband and a son and a house with a fig tree in the yard and I have not been strange for one single day. I know exactly what I am doing." She started down the ridge, then stopped on the first step and said, without turning, "But I know what you are doing too. You have been here six weeks. If you wanted to be found you would have found a way to be found."

He had not told her about the radio.

---

He told her two days later, because she had asked the question and he could not leave it standing.

He brought the VHF up from where he had buried it, in the dry sand under the rock shelf, wrapped in the plastic bag from the raft pack. He put it on the table in the storehouse. Rahwa, Rahim, Isaias, Miriam, Sara, Fatuma. Kidane on his bench by the door.

"It's a radio," he said. "Marine VHF. Line of sight — flat water, from up on the ridge, maybe sixty, seventy kilometers to a ship on the horizon. Maybe more on a good day. The battery is about half. Maybe better. It's been kept dry." He turned it over. "There's a distress button. If I hold it, it transmits a position to anything within range. Satellite too, if the satellite system is listening, but I can't count on that."

Nobody said anything.

"Why have you not done it," Rahwa said.

Stipe looked at the radio. He was aware of Isaias, who had not looked away from it once since it came out of the bag.

"Because I don't know what it does," he said. "I turn that on and a ship comes and a helicopter comes and a man in a uniform comes and starts writing things down, and then it's not mine anymore." He put his hand flat on the table next to the radio. "That's the honest answer. It's not a good one."

"You could have done it anyway."

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

"No."

Rahwa looked at him for a long moment and then reached out and took the radio and put it in the cloth bag she carried, and put the bag over her shoulder.

"This is not yours," she said. "Nothing here is yours. You eat our food and you sleep under our roof and you have been fixing our wall for six weeks and it is a good wall, and none of it makes you one of us, and none of it makes this yours." She walked to the door. "But I am not going to lock a machine in a box like a child. You know how it works. When it is time, you will use it. I will tell you when it is time."

Behind her, Isaias got up and walked out into the light and stood with his back to the storehouse for a long time.

---

The seventh week, Kidane said: "You didn't use it because of us. Or because of you?"

It was evening. The old man had a bowl of the green stew and he was eating it with a spoon, very slowly, and Stipe was mending a net on the ground next to the bench.

"Both."

"Which is more."

"Does it matter?"

"To you it matters," Kidane said. "To me it doesn't. I am asking to see how you lie." He ate a spoonful. "At the beginning, on the boat, there was a man who said he had a brother in Sweden, and he said it every day, in the morning, to everyone. He said: my brother will find me. And when the water came and he was in the water he was still saying it. He said it until he stopped." He set the bowl down. "That is not a bad way to die. But it was a lie, and everybody knew it, and it made the rest of us smaller, because it meant he was not with us, and he was the only one who got to be somewhere else."

"So what's the not-lie."

"There is no not-lie. There is just: here, and now, and the people who are here." Kidane turned his face toward Stipe, the gray eyes unfocused, the light from the west on his cheekbone. "You have a daughter."

"Petra."

"How old."

"Nineteen. Zagreb. Studying chemistry, which I don't understand." Stipe's hands went on with the net. "I've got a photograph in my wallet, but the wallet went with the ship. I've got about half her face. I've been trying to put it back together for two months and I can't do the mouth."

"You will."

"Will I?"

"No," Kidane said. "Probably not. But you will keep trying, and that is what a father is."

---

The eighth week, on the third day, Hodan fell.

She was after eggs on the cliff path above the rookery, a place she had been told a hundred times not to go, and the tussock gave way and she went down about four meters onto the rock shelf below and landed on her left arm.

Stipe heard it from the seawall. He heard Fatuma's voice, which was not a voice he had ever heard raised, and he was running before he understood what he was running for.

The arm was broken above the wrist. It was not a clean break. The bone had come through the skin.

They carried her down to the storehouse, and Rahwa cleared the table and put her on it, and Sara, who had been a nurse in Kassala for eleven years before she left, took one look and went white and said quietly, in Arabic, *this is beyond me.*

They set it. Stipe held the girl's shoulders, and Rahim held the arm above the break and Rahwa held it below and Sara pushed the bone back under the skin, which was the worst thing Stipe had ever watched and he had watched a man die in his lap. Hodan screamed until she stopped screaming, and then she was quiet in a way that frightened him more.

They cleaned it with boiled water and with a bottle of iodine that had come off a container wreck five years before, and they splinted it with two pieces of a broken oar and bound it with strips torn from a shirt, and Rahwa sat by the table all night, and by the next afternoon Hodan had a fever of a kind that Stipe had seen before, in the Philippines, on a ship, in a man who had cut his foot on a hatch and who had gone into the ship's hospital and come out three days later with one fewer foot.

By the third day the smell was coming through the bandage.

Rahwa came and found him on the seawall. She stood in front of him and she was crying, without any change in her face at all, tears coming down as if she had not noticed.

"Tell me what it does," she said.

He told her. The truth: a ship within VHF range hears the distress alert with the position; the ship relays it; a rescue coordination centre somewhere in the world picks it up; if there is anything within a hundred miles it comes, and if there is not, it might be a day, it might be two, it might be a week, and it might be nothing at all, because the Indian Ocean is very large and the button is not a prayer.

"If they come," he said, "they will bring a doctor and a helicopter. If they don't come, it doesn't matter either way."

"And if they come," Rahwa said, "they will find eleven."

Stipe looked at her.

"She is nine years old," Rahwa said. "And she has never seen a television. And she thinks Holmes is the name of the world. And they will take her arm off in a hospital in Réunion and give her a plastic one and put her on a plane and put her in a room and ask her where she is from and she will say Holmes, and they will write that down, and then someone will have to explain it to her." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, once, and her expression did not change at all. "Get the radio."
