The census man came up the ghat road on a Tuesday in the heat before the rains. Sharada Deshpande knew it was a Tuesday because the vegetable woman came on Tuesdays and the two of them arrived at her gate together, the vegetable woman with her basket of brinjals and the young man with a tablet in a plastic sleeve and a lanyard printed with the national emblem.

"Government of India," he said, holding up the lanyard as though she might ask to inspect it. "Population Enumeration. I'm sorry, Aaji, it won't take long."

She let him into the front room because the sun was cruel at that hour and he looked like a boy who had been walking since dawn. He was perhaps twenty-five, with a careful moustache and a shirt that had been ironed that morning and had given up somewhere along the way. His name was Imran Shaikh. He was from Satara, he said, and the district had sent him up to Sonvad because the last enumerator had not finished the job, and nobody seemed to know why.

She gave him water in a steel tumbler and then, because it was what one did, tea. He drank both with the gratitude of someone who had not been offered either at the previous three houses.

"Head of household?"

"Me."

"Name?"

"Sharada Prabhakar Deshpande."

He typed with two thumbs. "Age?"

She told him. He typed it.

"Marital status?"

"Widow."

He murmured something apologetic and typed. The ceiling fan turned. From the kitchen came the smell of the dal she had left on the low flame, and from outside the vegetable woman's voice telling Banu Pathan next door that the tomatoes were not expensive, it was Banu's expectations that were cheap.

"Question seven," Imran said. "How long have you been residing at this address?"

"Since I married," Sharada said.

"And the year of your marriage?"

"Nineteen—" she said, and stopped.

It was not that she didn't know. She knew it the way she knew the shape of her own front teeth with her tongue. She had worn a green Paithani with a peacock border, and Prabhakar's uncle had arrived late from Kolhapur with a garland already wilting, and it had been the year that—the year—

The digits were there. She could feel them, the way you can feel a word in a dream. She reached for them and they moved, politely, out of her hand.

"Nineteen," she said again.

Imran waited, thumbs raised.

"Write a long time," Sharada said.

He smiled, as if she had made a joke, and then saw that she had not. "It needs a number, Aaji. Approximately is fine. Twenty years? Thirty?"

She opened her mouth to say *forty* and found she could not tell whether forty was more or less than the truth. It was like trying to judge the depth of a well by looking at the water.

"Come back after lunch," she said. "I'll find the papers."

---

She did not find the papers.

She found the steel trunk under the bed where the papers were meant to be, and inside it she found her wedding photograph in its cardboard folder, the two of them standing stiffly in front of a painted backdrop of a lake with swans. She found Prabhakar's service book from the Postal Department, the cover soft as cloth. She found the ration card, three electricity bills, her son Nikhil's school leaving certificate, and a letter from her sister in Nagpur that began *Shardu, you never write anymore*.

Every one of these had a date. She could see that they had dates; the dates were printed or written in the places where dates go. She held the electricity bill up to the window and looked at the line that said BILL DATE and read the numbers aloud, one by one, and by the time she reached the last digit the first had already gone soft, like a sugar cube in tea.

She sat on the edge of the bed with the trunk open at her feet.

It was not frightening, exactly. That was what she would remember later, when she tried to explain it to Nikhil. It was like discovering that a room in your house had always had a fourth wall you had never once looked at. You were not frightened of the wall. You were frightened that you had never looked.

---

Imran came back at three. She told him she was still looking. He nodded and asked if he might sit on her veranda for a little while, because the houses on the lower lane did not open until the sun dropped and he did not like to stand in the road.

"Did they answer?" she asked. "The others. Question seven."

He looked at his tablet. He had the expression of a boy who had been told something by an elder and had decided, out of respect, not to believe it.

"Mostly they said a long time."

"Mostly."

"Mr. Gokhale at the post office said he has been there since the old postmaster passed." He scrolled. "The tea-stall man said since his father. The lady with the hens said—" He glanced at the wall between Sharada's house and Banu's. "She said, *Ask Sharada, she was here when I came.*"

"And when did she come?"

"She said she came after her wedding."

"And when was that?"

Imran put the tablet face-down on his knee. "Aaji," he said, "I am not supposed to fill this column with *a long time*. There's a validation. It won't let me submit."

---

That evening Sharada went next door, which she had done every evening for longer than she could say. Banu was in the yard throwing broken rice to the hens, her dupatta knotted at the back of her neck. She was a broad woman with a laugh that carried across three lanes, and she was not laughing.

"He asked you too," Banu said.

"He asked everyone."

"I told him to ask you."

"I know. He told me."

Banu threw a last handful and dusted her palms. "When did Rafiq's father die?"

Rafiq was Banu's husband. His father had lived with them in the back room, a thin, cheerful old man who had sat on the step every evening and pretended not to watch the hens. Sharada remembered his cough. She remembered bringing kheer for the third-day prayers.

"Some years," she said.

"Some years." Banu sat down heavily on the step where her father-in-law had sat. "My Ayaan is in Dubai. I talk to him on the phone. He has a daughter, did you know? She is learning to read. I said to Rafiq, how is it possible, Ayaan was just here throwing stones at your cycle tyres. And Rafiq said, Banu, he is thirty-one."

She looked at Sharada with an expression Sharada had never seen on her before, a kind of naked arithmetic.

"Thirty-one. I said it back to him. Thirty-one. And then I went in to make chapatis and when I came out I couldn't remember what number he'd said."

---

By the end of the week it was the only thing anyone in Sonvad talked about, which was strange, because by the end of the week nobody could have told you how long a week was.

That was the second thing people noticed, once they had noticed the first. Days arrived in their proper order—the milkman, the vegetable woman, the Friday namaz drifting across from the small mosque near the bus stand, the Sunday market with its pyramids of onions—but they did not stack. Each one sat beside the last like a bead on a string with no knot at the end. You could count them if you tried, but you had to try, and the effort felt like pushing something uphill.

The sarpanch, Dattatreya Jadhav, called a meeting in the gram panchayat hall. He was a big, mild man who had won every election anyone could remember, which was now a sentence people said aloud and then fell quiet after. Sixty people came, sitting on plastic chairs and on the floor. The ceiling fans did not reach the back rows. Imran sat to one side with his tablet, because Jadhav had asked him to, and because by now he had nowhere else to be.

"It's simple," Jadhav said, in the voice he used for disputes about boundary walls. "There are records. We have records. Birth register, death register, land records. We go to the records and we count."

The birth and death register was kept by Jadhav's clerk, a young woman named Swati Pawar who wore her hair in a tight plait and had a reputation for exactness. She brought the register in from the back room, a ledger the size of a paving stone, bound in red cloth gone the color of dried chilies.

She opened it on the table. People craned forward.

"First entry," Swati read. "Birth. Male child, to Shripad and Malati Kale. Date—"

She stopped. Her finger stayed on the page.

"Read it," Jadhav said.

"I'm reading it," Swati said. Her voice was very calm. "I can see it. It's in my own handwriting."

"Your handwriting? This is the first entry."

"Yes."

"You're twenty-six years old."

Swati looked up at him. "Am I?" she said.

Nobody laughed. In the back row, an old man named Kashinath, who repaired radios and had a stall near the bus stand, said, quite loudly, "My wife's name is Sunanda," and then sat very still, as though he had needed to check.

---

Sharada did not sleep that night. She lay on her side on the bed she had shared with Prabhakar and looked at his side of it, at the dent in the mattress that had not quite filled in, and tried to work out how long a dent took to fill.

He had died in the monsoon. She was sure of that. She remembered the rain coming through the hospital window in Satara, the nurse slamming it shut with the flat of her hand, the sound it made. She remembered the long jeep ride back up the ghat with his body wrapped in white in the back and Nikhil sitting beside it holding his father's feet through the cloth, though nobody had asked him to. She remembered the smell of the wet sandalwood at the cremation ground and how it would not catch, and how Pandu the tea man had come running with a can of kerosene and nobody had said anything, because what was there to say.

She remembered all of that so clearly that it seemed it must have been last year. Perhaps the year before.

She got up at four and went to the kitchen and took the calendar off the nail. It was the free calendar the Laxmi Kirana Stores gave out every Diwali, with a picture of Ganpati on a lotus. She looked at the year printed across the top. She held it there, in her eyes, and walked with it to the front room, and opened the trunk, and took out Prabhakar's death certificate, and looked at the year on that.

She stood between them, the calendar in one hand and the certificate in the other, saying both numbers aloud, over and over, as if she were carrying two pots of water up a hill and could not put either down.

Then she said, "Subtract," and the numbers went, both of them, like birds off a wire.

---

"The phone," Imran said.

He had come to her veranda again, as he did most afternoons now. She no longer asked him whether he had finished the lower lane. He wore the same shirt, washed in Banu's yard and dried on her line, and his moustache had become something closer to a beard. He was eating a chapati rolled around potato bhaji, which Sharada had not exactly offered him but had put on the plate beside his chair.

"What about the phone?"

"Your son. He's in Pune, you said. Pune is outside." He said *outside* the way the people of Sonvad had begun to say it, as if it were a country. "Call him. Ask him."

She had thought of this. She had thought of it at four in the morning with the calendar in her hand, and she had not done it, because she was afraid of what she would hear, and also because she had a feeling that she could not quite name, that Nikhil was busy, that he had always been a little impatient with her on the phone, that he would think she was going senile.

"You call yours," she said. "Your supervisor. Ask him how long you've been here."

Imran put the chapati down.

"I've been here five days," he said.

"Have you?"

He took out his own phone. It was a cheap Android with a cracked screen. He looked at it for a long moment. "The date is wrong," he said. "It's showing—that can't be right."

"What is it showing?"

He turned the screen toward her. She looked at it and could not read it. Not because the numbers were blurred, but because her eyes slid off them the way water slides off a lotus leaf.

"Call him," she said.

He called. It rang a long time. Sharada heard a man's voice, tinny and loud, and then louder, and Imran said "Sir, sir," and "Yes sir," and "I know, sir," and "No sir, I didn't—" and then held the phone a little away from his ear while the voice went on.

When it was over he sat without moving.

"He said I was due back on the twenty-second," he said. "He said they've been calling. He said my mother has been calling the office. He said today is the—" He stopped. He looked at the phone again and then put it face-down on the floor, as he had put the tablet face-down that first day. "Seven weeks," he said. "He said I've been gone seven weeks."

The fan turned. Next door, a hen complained.

"It's been five days," Imran said again, quite softly, as if he were apologizing to someone.

---

She called Nikhil that night.

He answered on the fourth ring, a little breathless, the television loud behind him. "Aai? Everything okay? It's late."

"Everything's okay," she said. "I wanted to ask you something."

"Ask."

"How long ago did Baba die?"

There was a silence on the line, and then the television went quiet, as though he had walked into another room.

"Aai."

"Just tell me."

"Are you feeling all right? Did you take your BP tablet?"

"Nikhil. Please."

He let out a breath. She could picture him: his father's shoulders, his father's habit of rubbing the back of his neck when he was about to say something he didn't want to say.

"Eleven years," he said. "In July it'll be eleven years."

She sat down on the floor of the kitchen. She didn't decide to. Her legs simply made the decision and she went along with them, her back against the cool steel of the cupboard.

"Aai? Are you there?"

"Eleven," she said.

"Aai, what's going on?"

"How old is Mihir?"

Mihir was his son. She had held him at the naming ceremony. He had a birthmark the shape of a cashew on his left ankle, and he had screamed when the priest dropped water on his head, and she had thought, *oh, he's like Prabhakar, he hates being surprised.*

"Mihir is nine," Nikhil said. "He's in fourth standard. Aai, you came for his birthday. In December. You brought him the cricket bat."

She looked down at her hand and saw that she was holding the phone so tightly the knuckles had gone yellow.

"I don't remember the cricket bat," she said.

---

She did not tell him about the census, or the register, or the post office. She said she was tired and had been reading old letters and had got confused, and he said, in the gentle, slightly too-loud voice people use for the elderly, that he would come up next weekend, that he would bring Mihir, that she should drink some warm milk and sleep. She agreed to all of it. She said good night. She held the phone in her lap for a long time after the screen went dark.

*Eleven years.* She said it once, out loud, into the kitchen.

She could hold it. That was what surprised her. It had come from outside, through the phone, in her son's voice, and it stayed. It sat in her chest like a stone she had swallowed, and it did not dissolve.

And then, behind it, the rest began to arrive.

Not memories exactly. She had the memories already; she had always had them. What arrived was their weight. The eleven monsoons since the one with the rain through the hospital window. Eleven Diwalis lighting the lamps alone on the veranda. Eleven years of Gokhale at the post office, which meant eleven years of letters going out with the wrong postmark, because Prabhakar had been the one who changed the date dies in the steel stamp every morning, a little ritual, his thumbnail working the brass wheels, and after him nobody had. She had known that. She had known that and never once asked what date the stamp said. Eleven years of Nikhil calling on Sundays and then on alternate Sundays and then when he remembered. Eleven years of Mihir growing from a screaming infant into a boy who played cricket, and she had been there, apparently, at the birthdays, she had brought a bat, and she had come home up the ghat road and set it all down at the edge of town like a bag too heavy to carry in.

Eleven years of her husband's side of the bed staying exactly as unfilled as it had been the first night.

She understood, sitting on the kitchen floor, why the town had been so comfortable. It was not that nothing hurt in Sonvad. It was that nothing hurt *more*. Grief stayed the size it had been on the first day. So did love. So did everything. You could live inside it forever and never once have to feel how long forever was.

She wept then, for a long time, in a way she had not wept at the cremation ground, or in the jeep, or on the first night. When she was done it was nearly dawn, and her back ached from the cupboard, and she noticed that the ache felt old.

---

By the second week—Sharada had begun counting, with a pencil, a mark on the kitchen wall beside the calendar every night—the town had split, though not in any way you could have drawn on a map.

Some people had called outside. Kashinath the radio man had called his brother in Wai, who had told him, sounding baffled, that Kashinath's wife Sunanda had been dead for six years and that Kashinath had come to her funeral. Kashinath had shut his stall and sat for three days in front of the shuttered shop, his radios lined up behind him like children, and on the fourth day he had opened again and put a small photograph of Sunanda above the soldering iron where everyone could see it, and when people asked about it he told them.

Swati Pawar had taken a bus down the ghat to Satara and sat in the district collectorate with her register for an entire afternoon, comparing entries with the district copy, and come back with a list written in ballpoint on the back of an old ration form, which she pinned to the notice board of the gram panchayat. It listed every birth and death in Sonvad, with dates. People came and read it. They stood in front of it the way you stand in front of a shrine. Some of them wept. Some of them laughed. One old woman read it all the way through twice and then said, to nobody, "So I've been angry with my sister-in-law for twenty-two years," in a tone of great wonder, and went to the sister-in-law's house, and the two of them were seen that evening sitting on the same charpoy, not speaking, sharing a plate of bhel.

Other people did not call. Other people did not read the list. Dattatreya Jadhav, at the next meeting, said that he did not see what the fuss was. People were healthy. Children were in school. The water tank was full. If no one had noticed the time passing, perhaps that was because Sonvad was a place where time passed well.

"You've been sarpanch for thirty-four years," Swati said from her seat beside the table. "I checked."

"And has anybody complained?"

"Nobody could count high enough to complain."

There was laughter at that, but not the kind Jadhav liked. He adjourned the meeting.

Banu did not call Dubai. Sharada asked her about it one evening across the wall, while Banu was shutting the hens in.

"What would I say? *Ayaan, how old are you?* He'll think his mother is mad."

"You could say, *Ayaan, tell me about your daughter.*"

Banu latched the coop. She stood with her hand on the wire.

"If I know how old he is," she said, "then I know how long since he was here. And then I know how long until—" She shrugged. "Rafiq's father sat on that step until he didn't. As long as nobody counts, he is still almost on that step. Do you understand?"

"Yes," Sharada said, because she did.

"You're counting."

"I'm counting."

"Is it better?"

Sharada thought about the kitchen floor and the cupboard and the stone in her chest.

"It's heavier," she said. "I think heavier is better. I'm not sure yet."

---

Imran stopped coming to her veranda on the twelfth mark.

She went looking for him on the thirteenth and found him at the tea stall by the bus stand, sitting on the bench with his tablet in his lap, watching the road. Pandu had given him a glass of cutting chai and he had not drunk it. A skin had formed on the top.

"The bus is at four," he said, without looking at her.

"You're going."

"My supervisor called again. They've reassigned the block. Someone else is coming to do Sonvad." He laughed. "He said, *What did you do up there for two months, Shaikh, count the goats?*" He turned the tablet over in his hands. "I've done thirty-one households. Thirty-one. I could have done three hundred."

"You've done thirty-one well."

"I haven't done any." He tilted the screen toward her. The form for her household was still open on it, the cursor blinking in question seven. "I couldn't finish one. Not one. All of them say *a long time*." He looked at her, and she saw that he was frightened, properly frightened, in a way he had not been even on the day of the phone call. "Aaji, I tried to remember my mother's birthday this morning. I couldn't. I know it's in Shravan. I know she makes sheera. I couldn't get the day."

Sharada sat down on the bench beside him. She took the glass of chai out of his hands, flicked off the skin with a fingernail, and gave it back.

"Drink it," she said. "Then give me that."

He drank. She took the tablet.

It was not difficult to use. Nikhil had made her learn his old phone the last time he visited—or a time he visited; she was no longer sure which—so that she could see photographs of Mihir. She found the field with her thumb.

*How long have you been residing at this address?*

She had worked it out with a pencil on the back of the Laxmi Kirana calendar, three nights running, writing the numbers down as she went so they could not escape. The year of the green Paithani. The year printed above the Ganpati on the lotus. The subtraction, done longhand, carrying the one, the way she had taught it to thirty years of children at the zilla parishad school. She had copied the answer onto a scrap of paper and folded it into her blouse, and she had checked, every morning, that it was still there.

She took it out now and unfolded it and looked at it. The number held. She typed it in.

The field turned from red to green.

She handed the tablet back to Imran. He looked at the screen, then at her.

"It accepted it," he said.

"Good."

"Is it right?"

"It's right."

He looked at it a while longer. Then he touched SUBMIT, and the little wheel spun, and a message came up that said, in English and in Marathi, *Record saved successfully. Thank you for your participation.*

"One," Imran said.

"One," Sharada agreed.

---

The bus came up the ghat road at a quarter past four, groaning in low gear, its roof stacked with sacks and a bicycle tied on with rope. It was the red MSRTC bus that came twice a day, morning and afternoon, and turned around in the dust by the tea stall and went back down. Sharada had taken it a hundred times. A thousand. She had never, until this moment, thought of it as something that came from somewhere and went somewhere else.

Imran picked up his bag. He stood in front of her and touched her feet, quickly, the way young men do when they're embarrassed by it, and she put her hand on his head.

"Tell your mother you're sorry," she said. "Don't explain. Just tell her."

"I'll tell her."

"And find out her birthday."

He laughed, wet-eyed, and climbed on. He took a seat at the window. The conductor rang his little bell and shouted the destinations—*Medha, Kudal, Satara*—and the driver began to back the bus around in the dust.

Sharada stood by the tea stall and watched it turn.

She thought about the kitchen wall and its thirteen pencil marks. She thought about Nikhil's promise to come up next weekend, which she knew, now that she was counting, was a promise he had made on the phone fifteen days ago. She thought about a cricket bat she did not remember buying and a boy with a cashew-shaped mark on his ankle who was nine, who was in the fourth standard, who would be ten in December whether or not anybody in Sonvad noticed.

She thought about Banu's father-in-law, almost on the step.

The bus finished turning. It began to move.

Sharada had her purse with her, because she always had her purse; she had a little money in it, and her BP tablets, and her spectacles, and the folded scrap of paper with the number on it. She did not have a change of clothes. She did not have the bus fare to Pune, or Nikhil's address written down, though she was fairly sure she could find it in the phone.

"Pandu," she said, "tell Banu to feed my cat."

Pandu, wiping glasses, looked up. "You don't have a cat, Sharada-tai."

"Then tell her I've gone to see my grandson."

She walked to the bus and knocked on the door with her palm, and the conductor, who had seen everything, shrugged and swung it open for her and did not stop the bus. She hauled herself up the steps with his hand under her elbow. Imran half rose from his window seat, astonished. She waved him down and sat across the aisle, her purse on her lap.

The bus went down out of Sonvad on the ghat road, past the cremation ground and the small mosque and the last of the houses, past the board that said in faded paint SONVAD GRAM PANCHAYAT WELCOMES YOU, which she had walked past so many times she had stopped seeing it.

At the first hairpin bend she felt it begin.

It was not dramatic. It was like stepping out of cold water into air: a slow thickening, a weight settling on her shoulders, her knees, the backs of her hands. She looked down at her hands and they were her hands, the same spots, the same ring she had never taken off, and they were also, suddenly, the hands of a woman who had been a widow for eleven years. The bus swayed. Her body knew how old it was. She could feel every one of the years arriving, the way you feel each step when you have been carried up a staircase and are now walking down.

She did not try to put them down. She sat with her purse in her lap and let them come.

Across the aisle, Imran had closed his eyes. His lips were moving. After a moment she understood that he was counting—something, anything, the bends in the road perhaps, the white-painted stones along the edge—to make sure the numbers stayed.

Sharada looked out the window at the valley opening below them, brown and gold in the late light, the river a thin bright thread at the bottom of it, a long way down. She took the scrap of paper out of her purse and unfolded it, and looked at the number, and folded it back up, and held it.

The bus went on down the mountain, and she counted the bends with him.
