Zofia Kaczmarek was up to her elbows in ice water when the phone rang. It was 6:10 in Miód’s kitchen, the sun not yet over the roofs of Kazimierz, and she was shocking wild garlic leaves to keep their color. Her sous-chef, Iga, called through the pass. “Szefowa, the ministry again.”

She wiped her hands on her apron. The restaurant couldn’t afford to ignore the ministry. The mortgage on Miód was five months behind. Last year’s Michelin guide had omitted her for the second time. She had forty seats and a menu built on her grandmother’s recipes, and that was perhaps not enough anymore.

Katarzyna Wysocka’s voice was crisp. “Zofia, I need you at Wawel on Friday. The Spring Table. Chef Eklund had a heart attack in Stockholm.”

“I’m sorry to hear that.”

“He’s alive. But he cannot cook. We need two chefs to replace him.”

Zofia felt a pulse in her throat. “Two?”

“You and Viktor Sárosi.”

She said nothing.

“Zofia?”

“I’d rather not.”

“You haven’t heard the fee.”

“I heard it.”

“Eighty thousand złoty for the week, plus expenses. And we’ve already confirmed the Michelin inspector.”

Zofia closed her eyes. “Why him?”

“Because the theme is Vistula and Danube. A Polish chef, a Hungarian chef. You’re both among the best. The Commission asked for you both by name.” Katarzyna paused. “I know you two have history. I don’t care. This is one dinner for a hundred and twenty people, the presidents included, and it will be televised. You can hate each other after.”

Zofia looked at the silent stove. She thought of the bank letter on her desk, unopened. “Fine. But he stays out of my section.”

“I’ll send the brief.”

The line went dead.

She stood for a long time with her hand on the phone. Iga watched from the pass, her eyebrows raised. Zofia shook her head and went back to the garlic. The wild green leaves floated in the ice water like little drowned things.

Viktor arrived Friday with two black knife rolls and an assistant named Bence, who looked seventeen but moved like a veteran. Zofia had brought Iga and Jacek, her pastry cook. The kitchen at Wawel was a long stone vault with copper pots and modern induction burners retrofitted into old iron frames. It smelled like cold stone and lemon polish.

Viktor set down his knives. “Zofia. You look well.”

“Don’t.”

“I was going to say I’m sorry about Eklund. I’m not here to fight.”

“You’re here because the ministry pays well and you like your face on television.”

He gave a small, tired smile. “Did you read the brief?”

“I read.”

They stood at opposite ends of the central island like boxers before a bell. Katarzyna swept in, iPad glowing, and briefed them on the timeline: seven days to create a twelve-course menu, five days of testing, one night for the Spring Table. The theme was “Two Rivers, One Table.” They would use only ingredients from Poland and Hungary—no tropical fruit, no tuna. Every course had to be paired with a narrative, she said, and every plate had to carry both countries somehow.

Zofia opened her notebook. She had already sketched courses: smoked eel and apple, żurek poured over sour rye bread, pike with sorrel, roast duck with slow-braised cabbage, buckwheat and forest mushrooms, plum cake with walnut cream. Simple, grounded, true. She had cooked versions of these for years, but not like this.

Viktor had an iPad with diagrams. He proposed crispy duck heart, beetroot ash, horseradish snow, paprika gel, a “Danube fog” of smoked water, a dessert of Tokaji wine and white chocolate made to look like river stones. “We need theatre,” he said. “The room will be full of people who’ve eaten everything.”

“They’ve eaten foam too,” Zofia said. “They haven’t had my grandmother’s żurek.”

“I can respect your grandmother and still use a water bath.”

“Maybe you should open a lab instead of a restaurant.”

Katarzyna raised a hand. “You have two days to present a unified menu. I don’t care how you get there.”

When she left, Viktor exhaled. “Can we at least agree on the first course?”

“Eel.”

“Fine. But not with apple. With quince, pickled, and a black garlic crumb.”

“I don’t use black garlic.”

“You will by Sunday.”

They stared at each other. Iga quietly began unpacking knives.

The first two days were war. Zofia made her żurek base from rye flour, water, and garlic, letting it ferment in a corner of the fridge. Viktor tested his “river stones” dessert, filling the kitchen with nitrogen vapor. They argued over whether the duck should be roasted on the crown or broken down and cooked sous vide. Viktor called her pierogi “dense little envelopes of nostalgia.” She called his paprika gel “a crime against paprika.” Bence and Iga became intermediaries, translating menu ideas past the insults.

On Sunday, they had a menu. It was not a miracle. It was an armistice.

The first course would be smoked eel with pickled quince and rye crumble—Viktor’s texture, Zofia’s acid. The second was a cold buttermilk soup with chive oil and cucumber spheres. Third, żurek poured tableside over roasted potatoes and marjoram. Fourth, Viktor’s duck liver, but cured with Tokaji and served on a brioche that Zofia insisted be baked in a wood oven. And so on. Twelve courses, each a mixture of her earth and his air.

That night, after Iga and Bence left, Zofia stayed to feed her sourdough starter. Viktor was in the corner, polishing glasses.

“Can I ask you something?” he said.

“No.”

“Did you ever wonder why I didn’t speak up?”

She turned. “At Nordlys? You didn’t speak because you got the job.”

“That’s what you think.”

“I know what happened. My sauce was salted. You were the only other person on the pass. The inspector tasted it and left. Chef Elling promoted you. I was fired.”

Viktor set down the glass. “I didn’t salt your sauce, Zofia. I know you don’t believe me. But I didn’t.”

“Then who did?”

He hesitated. “I’ll tell you after the dinner.”

“That’s convenient.”

“Maybe. But if I told you now, you’d walk out, and we’d lose this kitchen. And I need this too.”

Zofia said nothing. She watched him for a long moment, then turned back to her starter. It bubbled faintly, alive.

The next day, they went to Stary Kleparz together. It was Viktor’s idea, or perhaps Katarzyna’s; Zofia didn’t remember. The market was bright and crowded. She bought wild mushrooms from an old woman in a wool shawl who had sold to her grandmother. Viktor bought fresh paprika and a string of dried kolbász from a Hungarian deli on a side street. They walked through the flower stalls, not speaking, carrying cloth bags.

“What is that?” Viktor asked, pointing to a jar of dark honey on a table.

“Buckwheat honey from the Beskids,” Zofia said. “I use it on the duck.”

“Let me taste.”

The vendor handed him a wooden spoon. He tasted, closed his eyes. “Good. It has a bitter edge.”

“That’s the point.”

“I’ll use it in the glaze.”

“Fine.”

For a moment, they were just two chefs buying honey. It was disorienting. Zofia had carried her anger for ten years, and now it sat beside her like a passenger, not a driver.

Back at the castle, the argument over the duck resurfaced. Viktor wanted to cook the breasts sous vide, then sear. Zofia wanted to roast the whole duck on a bed of root vegetables. They compromised: the legs were braised slowly in hay and hard cider, then crisped; the breasts were cooked sous vide, then finished over coals. The dish became “Duck Three Rivers” and it was better than either plan.

On Wednesday, the combi oven failed in the middle of a test run. The error light blinked. Bence swore in Hungarian. Viktor pulled the lamb from the oven and looked around.

“What would your grandfather do?” he asked Zofia.

“Dig a hole and line it with hot stones and hay.”

“We’re not digging a hole in Wawel.”

“No. But we can line a cast-iron pot with hay and slow-cook it on the induction.”

They did. The lamb emerged tender, tasting of smoke and meadow. Viktor plated it with a reduction of sour cherries and told the rest of the kitchen, “This is better than my original.” Zofia didn’t smile, but she didn’t argue.

Later that day, they tested the dessert. Viktor had built a dark chocolate cylinder filled with Tokaji cream, meant to look like a stone from the Danube. When they unmolded it, the chocolate cracked. He stood frozen, tweezers in hand.

Zofia looked at the mess. “You need more cocoa butter, and you need to temper it at night, not in this steam.” She took one of her grandmother’s plum preserves, warmed it, and poured it into the crevice. “Call it a river stone with plum lava. Don’t fight the break.”

“River stone with plum lava.” He said it softly. “Actually, that’s better.”

That night, they sat on the back steps, eating cold pierogi from a container. The Wawel courtyard was dark. He asked about her grandmother. She told him about the old house in the Beskids, the smoke of the wood stove, the way her grandmother never measured salt but always knew.

“What happened to her?” he asked.

“She died last year. The restaurant is hers, really. I just run it.”

“She’d be proud.”

“Don’t.”

“I mean it.”

Zofia didn’t answer, but she let the silence sit.

On Thursday afternoon, they ran the entire menu for Katarzyna and two ministry officials. It was the first time all twelve courses went out in order. The table in the tasting room was set with white linen and small flags. Zofia and Viktor stood by the pass and watched the officials taste, their faces unreadable.

When they finished, Katarzyna set down her napkin. “It’s good. But the third course, the żurek, is too heavy. The bowl is too much bread. By the fourth course, I was struggling.”

Zofia’s jaw tightened. “That’s how it’s served.”

“Maybe in a bistro,” Viktor said quietly. “But not at a state dinner.”

She turned on him. “You were the one who wanted narrative. The bread is the narrative.”

“The narrative can be a tuile,” he said. “Serve the żurek in small ceramic cups with a rye tuile on the side. You keep the sour, the smoke, the marjoram. But the guest can breathe.”

Zofia wanted to argue. But she had watched the officials push their bowls away half-finished. “Fine. Small cups, rye tuile.”

Katarzyna nodded. “That’s what I want to see.”

After the officials left, Viktor leaned against the counter. “That wasn’t an attack.”

“I know.”

“Your soup is the best thing on the menu. It just doesn’t need a whole loaf to prove it.”

Zofia wiped down the pass. “You don’t have to manage me.”

“I’m not managing you. I’m trying to make sure they remember the taste instead of the weight.”

She didn’t respond, but that evening she tested the rye tuiles three times until they shattered cleanly under a spoon.

That night, the kitchen was quiet again. Viktor sat on a low stool, tying a new apron string. Zofia was polishing copper pots, though they were already clean.

“My restaurant is failing,” she said without looking at him. “If this dinner doesn’t work, I lose Miód.”

Viktor didn’t answer right away. Then he said, “Mine isn’t failing, but the star is in danger. The guide wants to see I can do something besides foam and ash. Something with a pulse.” He paused. “This dinner is my answer.”

Zofia set down the cloth. “So we both need it.”

“Yes.”

“Then we won’t fail.”

For a moment, it was not kindness, exactly. It was something more useful: two people who had stopped pretending the stakes were different.

On Friday morning, the day of the dinner, an inspector from the Ministry came to check the kitchen. She was a small woman with grey eyes and a tablet, and she walked through the stations without smiling. Katarzyna pulled Zofia aside. “There’s a problem. The Hungarian president’s wife is allergic to sorrel. You have sorrel in the pike course.”

“We can replace it with spinach and lovage.”

“Will it still work?”

“It will work better,” Viktor said, stepping in. “Lovage has more depth. We’ll keep the acid from white wine.”

Katarzyna nodded and left.

At noon, the bread delivery was missing. The wood-oven brioche for the duck liver course. The supplier had sent the wrong order to the wrong castle. Zofia called three bakeries; none could produce it in time. Viktor took Jacek and Bence and drove to a bakery in Kazimierz that owed Zofia a favor. They came back two hours later with forty warm brioche rolls wrapped in linen. No one asked how.

At five, the first guests began to arrive. Zofia put on a clean jacket for the pass. Viktor tied his apron. They looked at each other.

“Ready?” he said.

“Don’t talk to me while I’m plating.”

“That’s my line.”

Then the tickets came.

The kitchen fell into the rhythm they had built. Iga called orders. Bence worked the sauce station. Jacek plated cold courses. Viktor and Zofia moved around each other like two currents in one river.

First course: smoked eel, pickled quince, rye crumble. Plates went out clean. Second: buttermilk soup, chive oil. Third: żurek, poured quickly from copper pots into small ceramic cups with rye tuiles. Zofia checked every cup. “More marjoram,” she said twice.

Viktor’s duck liver course was next. The brioche was warm, the liver silky. He plated with pickled cherries and black pepper. “Two minutes,” he said. “We’re on time.”

The pike course required the change they’d made with lovage. Zofia tasted the sauce. It was deep and green, better than the original. She nodded. The plates went out.

By the eighth course, the room was quiet, which the servers said was a good sign. The lamb with hay and sour cherries went out, and a murmur came back.

At the tenth course, the dessert crisis began early. Viktor’s river stones had been chilled, but the kitchen’s temperature had risen, and when Bence unmolded the last tray, half the cylinders broke.

“Fuck,” Viktor said.

Zofia looked at the tray. “How many whole?”

“Twenty. We need forty.”

“Then we make twenty new plates and use the broken ones for the rest. The plum lava covers the cracks.”

“The broken ones look broken.”

“Then make them look like the river after a storm. Call it ‘Floodplain.’”

He stared at her, then laughed—a short, startled sound. “Floodplain. God.”

He pulled the plum preserve. Zofia moved to the pass. They reassembled the dessert as something new: dark shards, plum ooze, white chocolate sediment, a quenelle of buttermilk ice. It looked like a landscape after the rivers had risen and receded. It was the best course of the night.

The kitchen exhaled. Iga leaned against the counter.

The final course, a small walnut cake with Tokaji caramel, went out clean. No one spoke. They listened to the applause through the stone wall.

At the end, Katarzyna came into the kitchen. Her face was flushed. “The president asked for the recipe for the żurek. The Hungarian delegation asked where they could go to cry after the dessert. The inspector has requested a private word with you both.”

Zofia wiped her hands. Viktor straightened his collar. The inspector was an elderly man with white hair and a black notebook. He stood in the doorway, gazing at the wrecked kitchen.

“That was the best meal I’ve had in this country in twenty years,” he said. “You two should not like each other. It gives you an edge. But don’t let it blind you.” He looked at Zofia. “The lovage was a risk.” Then at Viktor. “The river stone was better broken.” Then he left.

Katarzyna paid them and said there’d be more events, if they were interested.

After the guests were gone, the kitchen was quiet. Iga and Bence went to a bar. Jacek left to call his wife. Zofia and Viktor sat on the stone steps outside the kitchen, drinking beer from the castle’s supply.

“Tell me now,” she said.

Viktor looked at the dark courtyard. “At Nordlys, Chef Elling salted your sauce. I found out two years later when he was drunk in Copenhagen. I went to him and asked. He said he did it because he thought I was the better chef, and he needed an excuse to promote me. He said he’d do it again.” Viktor’s voice was flat. “I left his kitchen that night. I never worked for him again. But I never told you. I should have.”

Zofia’s hands were still around the bottle. “Why didn’t you?”

“Because I was ashamed. Because I wasn’t brave enough to tell you, and because I benefited from it. I didn’t salt your sauce, Zofia. But I let you carry it.”

She was silent for a long time. The moon was over the castle wall. “You should have told me.”

“I know.”

“I lost the job. I lost a year. I almost left cooking.”

“I know.”

She drained the beer. “But I didn’t leave.”

“No. You didn’t.”

They sat there. Somewhere, a nightjar called. The city hummed below the hill. Zofia thought of the kitchen at Miód, the worn wooden table where her grandmother had kneaded dough. She was angry, but the anger felt different now—less like a blade, more like a stone she could set down.

“You can come to the restaurant tomorrow,” she said. “We’ll cook something without a theme.”

Viktor looked at her. “Is that a peace offering or a review?”

“Call it a river after the flood.”

He laughed, and for the first time in ten years, they were just two cooks in a quiet kitchen.
