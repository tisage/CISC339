# CISC 339 — Final Report Guide
### Reading a Real Research Paper & Writing a Short Research Report

---

## 1. What This Assignment Is

Instead of a traditional cumulative final exam, your final assessment for this course is to **read one real, current research paper** and write a short **research report** responding to it.

The goal is not to produce something publication-quality. It's to give you a genuine taste of what reading and engaging with frontier AI research actually feels like — and to connect it back to your own interests, whether that's cybersecurity, data science, or something else entirely.

You will:
1. Choose **one** of two papers (both written by your instructor, both recent)
2. Read it and look up a handful of related papers for context
3. Write a short report answering a fixed set of questions, in your own words
4. Post your report publicly on **ResearchGate** (free account)
5. Submit the PDF + your ResearchGate link on Blackboard

---

## 2. Choose One Paper

Both papers are in this folder. Pick **whichever one is more interesting to you** — there's no "right" choice, and your major doesn't lock you into one.

### Paper A — `Paper_A_Synthetic_Spam_Detection.pdf`
**"Can Synthetic Spam Beat Real-World Detectors? Evaluating LLMs' Dual Role in Spam Generation and Detection"**
Wang, Zhou, Chen — ICISSP 2026

This paper studies large language models playing **both sides** of a security problem: using LLMs to generate realistic spam/phishing-style text, and using LLMs to detect it. It's directly relevant if you're interested in **cybersecurity, adversarial ML, spam/phishing detection, or AI safety**.

### Paper B — `Paper_B_SAGE_Literary_Evaluation.pdf`
**"SAGE: Hierarchical LLM-Based Literary Evaluation through Ontology-Grounded Interpretive Dimensions"**
Wang, Zhou — arXiv preprint (currently under peer review)

This paper builds a system that uses LLMs to evaluate creative writing — things like short stories — along structured literary dimensions, instead of just asking an LLM "is this good?" and trusting the answer. It's directly relevant if you're interested in **NLP, LLM evaluation methods, creative AI, or how to make LLM judgments more rigorous and explainable**.

> Note: Paper B is currently under peer review and not yet formally published. That's a normal part of the research process — you're welcome to say so in your report, and even to note anything you think is unclear or could be explained better, since that's exactly the kind of feedback the peer-review process is for.

## 3. Format Requirements

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Length** | 2–3 pages (not counting the reference list) |
| **Format** | PDF |
| **Citation style** | Your choice — APA, MLA, IEEE, or Chicago are all fine. **Be consistent** throughout the document. |
| **Minimum citations** | At least **6 total**: the 1 paper you chose, plus **at least 5 additional related papers** you find yourself |
| **Reference list** | Required at the end, formatted in your chosen citation style |
| **Language** | Written in your own words — see the AI-use policy in Section 5 |

### Finding your 5+ related papers
You don't need access to paid journal databases. Good free starting points:
- [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com)
- [arXiv.org](https://arxiv.org) (for ML/AI papers)
- [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org)

A good related paper is one that's actually relevant — something the paper you chose cites, something that studies a similar problem, or something that gives you background on a method or concept the paper uses. Skimming abstracts is a perfectly normal part of this process; you don't need to deeply read all 5.

## 4. Required Questions to Answer

Structure your report around these questions. You don't need to use these exact headings, but every question below needs to be clearly addressed somewhere in your report.

### Part 1 — Understanding the Paper
1. **What problem is this paper trying to solve, and why does that problem matter?**
2. **What is the paper's main method or approach, in your own words?** (Explain it like you're describing it to a classmate who hasn't read it — not by quoting the abstract.)
3. **What did the paper find or conclude?** What were the key results?

### Part 2 — Your Reaction and Connections
4. **Does this paper connect to your major, your career interests, or something you're personally curious about?** If yes — where specifically could you imagine using an idea from this paper, and why? If no connection comes to mind, it's fine to say so — but explain what *did* stand out to you and why.
5. **What's one thing in this paper you found surprising, confusing, or that you disagreed with?** A real reaction, not a generic compliment.

### Part 3 — Looking Forward
6. **Where do you think this line of research is headed next?** What's one open problem, follow-up question, or future application you'd be curious to see explored?

## 5. Using AI to Help You

You're encouraged to use AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) the way you'd use them for any other assignment in this course — **as a research and drafting assistant, not as a replacement for your own thinking.**

**OK to do:**
- Ask AI to help you find and summarize candidate related papers before you decide which 5 to cite
- Ask AI to explain a confusing passage, term, or method from the paper in simpler language
- Ask AI to give you feedback on a draft you already wrote, or help you fix grammar/flow
- Ask AI to help you format your citations correctly

**Not OK to do:**
- Copy-pasting AI-generated paragraphs directly into your report as your answers
- Asking AI to "write the report for me" and submitting that output with minimal changes
- Citing a paper you (or the AI) never actually looked at

The required questions in Section 4 are about *your* understanding and *your* reaction — that's not something AI can do for you. If two reports read like they came from the same AI conversation with the names swapped, that's a problem.

## 6. Posting to ResearchGate

[ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net) is a free, public platform where researchers share their work. Posting your report there is part of this assignment — it's a low-stakes way to experience what sharing research work publicly actually feels like.

**Steps:**
1. Go to [researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net) and create a free account (a student/school email works fine).
2. Complete your basic profile (your name, institution = your university, and your major/field).
3. Click **"Add new"** → **"Research"** (or the "+" / upload option on your profile) to upload a new publication.
4. Upload your PDF. For the title, use something like:
   *"CISC 339 Final Report: [your own title] — A Response to [Paper A / Paper B's title]"*
5. Under publication type, choose the option closest to **"Preprint"** or **"Technical Report"** (not "Article" — this isn't a peer-reviewed publication).
6. Once it's posted, copy the URL of your ResearchGate publication page — you'll need it for your Blackboard submission.

This should take about 10–15 minutes total.

## 7. How This Will Be Graded

| Component | What I'm looking for |
|---|---|
| **Understanding of the paper** (Q1–3) | You can accurately explain the problem, method, and findings in your own words — not just repeat the abstract |
| **Citations & references** | At least 6 total citations, consistently formatted, genuinely relevant to the topic |
| **Personal reflection** (Q4–5) | A real, specific reaction — not a generic "this was interesting" |
| **Forward-looking thinking** (Q6) | A thoughtful, specific idea about where this research could go next |
| **Format & clarity** | 2–3 pages, proofread, organized, easy to follow |

This is not graded on whether your report sounds like a "real" published paper — it's graded on whether you genuinely engaged with the material and can show it.
