By two o’clock the rain had not yet fallen, though all morning it had been practicing.

Clouds shouldered one another over the green back of the mountains, dragging their bellies low enough to snag on the breadfruit trees. The sea at Anse La Raye was a hard, bright blue, too bright to trust. Every so often a wind came up from the bay and worried the ribbons tied to the pew ends, making them slap and flutter like trapped fish.

In the dressing room of the old cocoa estate, Yseult Antoine sat with her wedding dress pooled around her waist and her hair pinned halfway up, while her cousin Naomie pressed cool fingers against her scalp and said, “Hold still, Zuzu. You moving like lizard.”

“I not moving.”

“You breathing too hard.”

“I should stop?”

“If you could manage it, yes.”

Yseult laughed, but it came out thin. In the long mirror propped against the wall she saw the laugh lift her cheeks and vanish. She looked like somebody performing the role of a bride in a film whose ending she had forgotten.

The room smelled of talcum powder, hair spray, damp wood, and the vanilla syrup Auntie Philomène had dabbed behind Yseult’s ears “because real perfume does fight with heat.” Bougainvillea and ginger lilies filled two blue buckets in the corner. Her shoes—ivory satin, entirely unsuitable for any terrain in Saint Lucia—waited beneath a chair. On the back of the door hung her mother’s veil, which had yellowed faintly at the edges, like paper that had survived a fire.

“Your phone making noise again,” Naomie said through a mouthful of hairpins.

“Let it make.”

“Is probably Kai.”

“If he wanted to tell me something important, he should have thought of it before the day reach.”

Naomie’s eyes met hers in the mirror.

“What?” Yseult asked.

“Nothing.”

“Say it.”

“You taking jokes too serious today.”

“I getting married today.”

“That is when people does make jokes. Otherwise they start to bawl.”

Outside, someone tested the microphone with a thud and a shriek. “One-two, one-two. Check, check.” Then the opening bars of a soca song boomed across the courtyard before cutting off abruptly. From somewhere below came the sound of Yseult’s aunties objecting in chorus.

“No, no, no, not that one for church people!”

“It not church, is estate!”

“Father Michel here already. Have respect.”

Yseult closed her eyes. When she opened them, she found herself looking not at her face but at the small enamel pendant resting in the hollow of her throat. It was blue, chipped in one corner, shaped like a little door. Her grandmother, Man Toinette, had worn it every Sunday pinned to her dress, saying it was French and therefore too good for everyday, though the clasp was cheap and had always stained the cloth beneath it.

A little door. Yseult touched it with her thumb.

“You sure you want that one?” Naomie asked. “The pearls nicer with the dress.”

“This one.”

“Man Toinette would’ve tell you the pearls nicer.”

“Then she should have lived to advise me.”

Naomie swatted her shoulder, gentle but offended on behalf of the dead. “Don’t speak so. Especially today.”

Especially today. Everything had been especially today since dawn.

Especially today, don’t quarrel with your sister.

Especially today, let Auntie Philomène think she is in charge of the seating.

Especially today, don’t tell your father’s new wife she tied her fascinator like a napkin.

Especially today, don’t remember that your mother had cried in this same veil for reasons no one explained until years later.

Especially today, don’t think about all the doors you had closed in the name of opening this one.

Yseult had slept badly. At four in the morning she woke to a rooster screaming like it had discovered murder, and beside her in the bed Naomie snored into a pillow, one arm flung over the empty space where Kai might have been if tradition and Auntie Philomène had not sent him to sleep at his brother’s house. Yseult had lain awake listening to the dogs, the far-off trucks on the hill road, the sea working at the stones. She thought of the bakery they were meant to open in October, in the narrow shopfront in Castries with the rusted burglar bars and the cracked blue tiles. Antoine & Flood, Kai had said, drawing the name with his finger in flour on her kitchen counter. Or Flood & Antoine if you want to start a war with your family.

She thought of how much money they did not have, and how cheerfully they had pretended otherwise. She thought of Kai’s hand on the back of her neck when he passed behind her in a room. She thought of the email from Martinique sitting unanswered in her inbox, subject line: Félicitations—Stage Culinaire, Fort-de-France.

Three months under Chef Marceline Rivière. A stipend. A room above the pastry school. The chance she had applied for on a reckless Tuesday after an argument with Kai about butter costs and family obligations and whether “soon” was a plan.

She had told no one. Not Naomie. Not Auntie Philomène. Not Kai.

By seven, the house had filled with women carrying garment bags and advice. By ten, her father had arrived with his wife and stood in the yard looking uncertain beside a rented SUV, as if he had accidentally come to the wrong wedding and was too polite to leave. By noon, Kai’s mother, Miss Edwina Flood, had sent over a covered dish of saltfish accra “in case the bride faint from not eating,” and Yseult had eaten three, standing in her slip.

Now there was one hour until the ceremony.

One hour until she walked beneath the silk-cotton tree where the chairs were set out facing the sea, and took Kai’s hands, and said yes in front of two hundred people, some of whom had crossed water for the privilege of judging her dress.

Naomie twisted another section of hair. “After I finish this, I going to check the cake. Your sister say the top tier leaning.”

“The top tier always leaning. That is its personality.”

“Zuzu.”

“What?”

“Are you happy?”

The room went quiet around the question. Even the microphone outside chose that moment to stop squealing.

Yseult looked at her cousin’s face in the mirror—round, serious, powdered with a shine of sweat at the upper lip. Naomie was only two years older, but marriage and two children had given her a way of looking at people as though she had known them when they were bones.

“Yes,” Yseult said.

Naomie waited.

“I am.”

“Good. Because your face like you going to court.”

“I’m concentrating.”

“On what?”

“Not sweating through a four-thousand-dollar dress I did not pay four thousand dollars for.”

Naomie smiled and tucked the last pin into place. “There. Look.”

Her hair had been gathered high at the crown and threaded with tiny white stephanotis. Without the veil, she looked less like her mother than she had feared. More like herself, though an expensive, fragile version of herself who would not survive a bus ride or a sudden laugh.

“I need five minutes,” Yseult said.

Naomie’s smile thinned. “For what?”

“To breathe. You yourself say I breathing wrong.”

“Breathe here.”

“With you counting?”

“I don’t trust brides.”

“Naomie.”

Her cousin sighed. “Five minutes. Don’t go far. Don’t let nobody see you. Don’t sit down anywhere dirty. Don’t cry. Don’t climb stairs. Don’t—”

“Five minutes,” Yseult said, already reaching for the robe hanging on the chair.

She wrapped it over the bodice and lifted her skirt in both hands. The dress whispered against the floorboards. At the door she paused, listening for aunties. When the corridor was clear, she slipped out.

The cocoa estate had once belonged to a family whose portraits still hung in the main house, pale faces darkened by mildew, eyes following guests toward the bar. Now it hosted weddings, corporate retreats, and the occasional school tour. The dressing room was in the old overseer’s cottage, up a slope from the boiling house and the ruined sugar mill. Below, the courtyard had been transformed by money and cousins: white chairs, palm fronds, lanterns in glass jars, a wooden arch wrapped in orchids, speakers disguised with banana leaves. Beyond it all, the sea flashed through the trees.

Yseult did not go toward the courtyard. She went behind the cottage, where a narrow path cut through hibiscus and cocoa trees to the old drying shed. She knew the estate from childhood picnics, from teenage dares, from the year after her mother died when Auntie Philomène had taken her every Sunday to “walk off sorrow” until Yseult learned sorrow could walk faster than either of them.

The path was damp. Her satin shoes were back in the room, so she went barefoot, the hem of the dress gathered to her knees, toes sinking into red earth. A lizard skittered across a stone. Somewhere a man laughed—Kai’s brother, maybe, that bright bark of his. She thought of turning back. Then she heard Kai’s voice.

It came from the drying shed, low but distinct through the slats.

“Jules, stop telling me what I should have done. I know what I should have done.”

Yseult froze.

The shed stood ten yards ahead, its open side facing away from her. She could see only the rear wall, patched tin, a gap where one board had come loose. She took one step closer before she understood she was hiding.

Jules said something she could not catch.

Kai answered, sharp. “Because if I tell her before, she would say no. You know Zuzu. She would say no before I finish explain.”

Yseult’s fingers tightened in the dress.

A gust of wind moved through the cocoa leaves. She heard the creak of a chair.

Kai spoke again, quieter now. “The ship leaving Tuesday. That part cannot change. Six months, maybe eight. I send the money back, we clear the deposit, we keep the shop. By Christmas everything straight.”

Jules murmured.

“No, she don’t know. I was going to tell her after.”

After.

The word moved through Yseult as if someone had opened a window in a storm.

“She going to vex,” Kai said. “Let her vex. Once we married, she have to see I doing it for we. She always fighting by herself like she don’t have nobody. I tired of that. I tired.”

Jules said, louder, “And the apartment?”

“Sublet to my cousin. Already done.”

“Without asking her?”

“She would say no to that too.”

Yseult stepped back. A twig snapped beneath her heel.

Inside the shed, both men fell silent.

“Who there?” Kai called.

She did not answer. She turned, lifting the dress too late to keep mud from catching the lace, and ran.

Not gracefully. Not the way brides ran in advertisements, hair streaming, face luminous with romantic certainty. Yseult ran like someone escaping a kitchen fire—barefoot, half-blind, dragging silk through wet grass, breath tearing at her throat. Branches snatched at the robe. A pin came loose from her hair and pricked her neck. She heard Kai call her name once, but not loudly, as if he could not believe it was her.

Zuzu.

She hated the softness of it in his mouth at that moment.

At the fork in the path she should have gone left toward the cottage. Instead she went right, down past the old mill wheel and into the grove of mango trees. There was a service gate beyond the grove, and beyond that the road curving toward the village. She did not know where she planned to go until she saw her father standing beside the gate, smoking.

He was not meant to be smoking. Auntie Philomène had told him twice. He cupped the cigarette behind his hand like a schoolboy when he saw her.

“Yseult?”

He had never called her Zuzu. Even when she was small, even when she sat on his shoulders at Jounen Kwéyòl, he had said her full name carefully, as though it were breakable.

She stopped a few feet from him, panting. The robe had come loose. Mud streaked the underlayer of the dress. A white flower fell from her hair and landed between them.

Her father dropped the cigarette and crushed it beneath his shoe. “What happen?”

She tried to speak. Nothing came.

He looked over her shoulder. “Somebody hurt you?”

The question startled her so badly she laughed. It sounded close to a sob. “No.”

“Then what?”

She bent forward, hands on her knees, gulping air. “Kai—he—” She could not finish. If she said it, the day would split open. People would come running. Auntie Philomène would demand names and blood. Miss Edwina would faint into the accra. The priest would clear his throat. The cake would lean farther and farther until it collapsed.

Her father took a step toward her, then stopped, perhaps remembering he had given up the right to gather her easily. He was a tall man, lean, with silver in his beard and a tailor’s careful posture. He had returned to Saint Lucia five years ago after fifteen years in Guadeloupe, returned with apologies and a second wife and a French stiffness that sat badly on him at family parties.

“Come,” he said. “There is a bench there.”

“I can’t sit. The dress.”

“Yseult.”

“I can’t sit!”

The sharpness of her voice shocked them both. A mynah bird lifted from the fence and vanished into the trees.

Her father nodded slowly. “All right. Don’t sit.”

She looked past him to the road. A minibus rattled by, painted green and yellow, music spilling out. For one clean second she imagined climbing into it. The driver would not refuse a bride. She would ride into Castries in her muddied gown, walk to the bakery shopfront, unlock the door with the key still hidden beneath the loose tile, and stand inside the empty room until the sea took the island or somebody came to find her.

“Did you ever,” she said, and stopped.

Her father waited.

“Did you ever know you were leaving before you told Mummy?”

His face changed. Not dramatically. A shutter moved somewhere behind his eyes.

Auntie Philomène had told the story so many times it had become family weather. Yseult’s father had gone to Guadeloupe for a two-week job repairing hotel furniture and returned three months later to collect clothes. He sent money when he could. He called on birthdays. He loved them, people said, as though love were a ferry ticket he had misplaced.

He looked down at his polished shoes, already dusted red from the path.

“Yes,” he said.

Yseult felt the word land.

“How long?”

“A month.”

She made a small sound.

“I told myself I was waiting for the right time.”

“And when was that?”

He gave a tired half-smile. “After.”

There it was again. After. A country men seemed always to be traveling toward, leaving women at the port with bags they had not packed.

Yseult turned away.

Her father said, “I am not Kai.”

“I know that.”

“And he is not me.”

“I know that too.”

“You sure?”

She hated him then, a little, for seeing too much. Hated that his mistake gave him wisdom in this exact shape. Hated that she wanted it.

From the courtyard came a burst of steelpan, then applause. Guests arriving. The wedding was moving without her, setting its great decorated wheel in motion. Someone would be looking for her soon.

Her phone buzzed in the pocket of the robe. She pulled it out. Naomie.

She declined the call.

Another buzz. Kai.

She stared at his name until the screen went dark.

“What do you want to do?” her father asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Then don’t move until you know.”

“That sounds simple.”

“It is not.”

“No.”

He removed a clean handkerchief from his pocket and held it out.

“I not crying,” she said.

“You sweating.”

She almost smiled. She took it and pressed it beneath her eyes anyway, careful not to disturb the makeup Naomie had labored over. The cloth smelled faintly of starch and tobacco.

From uphill someone called, “Zuzu!”

Naomie’s voice. Alarmed now.

Yseult turned toward it, then toward the road, then back to her father. “I need to talk to him.”

“Yes.”

“But not there. Not with everybody watching.”

“I can bring him.”

“No.” She shook her head. “He must come because I ask, not because you fetch him like a goat.”

Her father’s mouth twitched. “All right.”

She dialed Kai before she could change her mind.

He answered on the first ring. “Zuzu. Where are you?”

“At the service gate by the mango trees.”

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“I can explain.”

“You will. Come alone.”

She ended the call.

Her father stepped back as though creating a room around her. “I will stand over there.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

He moved to the shade of a mango tree and turned his face toward the road. Not leaving. Not intruding.

Yseult stood by the gate in her mud-stained gown and tried to slow her breathing. The clouds pressed lower. A drop of rain struck her bare shoulder, then another. Not rain yet. A warning.

Kai came down the path at a half-run, then slowed when he saw her. He wore a cream linen suit, waistcoat unbuttoned, tie loosened. His hair, freshly lined that morning, was already frizzing at the temples in the humidity. He looked handsome and frightened and so familiar her chest ached.

He stopped six feet away. He saw her father under the tree and swallowed.

“Zuzu.”

“Don’t call me that just now.”

He flinched. “Yseult.”

“Say it.”

He rubbed both hands over his face. “I took a contract on the Belle Aurélie. Cruise yacht, not one of them big floating prisons. Six months. Good pay. Very good. They needed a chief steward who could handle guests and repairs. Jules put in my name.”

“When?”

“They confirm last week.”

“And you were going to tell me after we married.”

He looked down. “Yes.”

“And the apartment?”

“I sublet it to Davian from September. Only September to March.”

“Without asking me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He spread his hands, helplessly, angrily. “Because every time we talk money, you get quiet like I accuse you of something. The shop deposit due in two weeks. The oven man want half up front. Your aunties adding chair covers and rum punch fountains and God knows what. My mother’s pressure tablets not cheap, and she pretending she don’t need them. Your father offer money, you say no. My brother offer loan, I say no. Everybody proud and broke. So I find a way.”

“A way that removes me.”

“A way that keeps the bakery.”

“Our bakery?”

“Yes.”

“With you gone on a boat?”

“For six months.”

“Maybe eight.”

His jaw tightened. He looked toward the trees, then back at her. “Maybe eight.”

Thunder rolled far away, not loud but broad, like furniture being dragged across heaven.

Yseult let out a breath. “You said I would say no before you finish explaining.”

“You would.”

“You said once we married I would have to see.”

“I said it stupid.”

“You said it true.”

He said nothing.

The first real rain began, sparse and heavy, darkening the dust around her feet.

Kai took one step closer. “I was scared.”

The words, simple as they were, seemed to cost him. He stared at the ground between them.

“I was scared you would look at me and see a man who couldn’t manage. All them ideas I had—charter boat, sound equipment, then the shop with you—and everything always almost. Almost enough. Almost ready. I didn’t want to be another man promising you soon.”

“So you chose to be a man promising nothing.”

His face twisted. “That is not fair.”

“No?”

“No.” His voice rose, then dropped again when he glanced at her father. “No. I love you. I want the thing to work.”

“What thing?”

“You and me.”

“What else?”

“The shop. The house. Children if we—”

“Stop.” She held up a hand. “You listing furniture in a room that not built.”

His eyes shone, whether from rain or tears she could not tell. “Then help me build it.”

She laughed once, bitter. “With what information?”

He winced.

The rain thickened. In the distance, someone shouted for plastic sheeting. The steelpan cut off mid-phrase. The smell of wet earth rose strong around them.

Yseult’s phone buzzed again and again. She ignored it.

Kai looked at the screen in her hand. “They waiting.”

“Yes.”

“I know I wrong.”

She closed her eyes. The sentence should have satisfied something. It did not. It sat in the mud between them, small and insufficient.

When she opened her eyes, she found her father still under the mango tree, back turned now, giving them the privacy of his refusal to disappear.

“There is something I didn’t tell you,” Yseult said.

Kai’s face went still.

“I applied for a stage in Martinique. In Fort-de-France. Pastry school. Three months.”

“When?”

“April.”

“You get it?”

She nodded.

“When you found out?”

“Three weeks ago.”

He stared at her as though she had spoken in a language he once knew but had forgotten.

“I wasn’t going to go,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because we were getting married. Because of the shop. Because Auntie Philomène already ordered napkins with our initials. Because I thought if I told you, you would hear leaving.”

“I am hearing leaving now.”

“Yes.”

He looked away. Rain ran down the side of his face. “You were going to tell me when? After?”

The word struck both of them. For a moment, absurdly, they almost smiled. Then did not.

Yseult wrapped the robe tighter over the bodice, though it was soaked now and useless. “Maybe I wanted you to ask me to go. Maybe I wanted you to tell me I was foolish to give it up. Maybe I wanted you to notice I was folding myself smaller.”

Kai’s shoulders dropped. “I noticed you were far away. I thought it was me.”

“It was.”

“Oh.”

“It was also me.”

The rain became serious. It drummed on leaves, on the tin roof of the drying shed up the hill, on the white chairs in the courtyard. Guests squealed and laughed as they ran for cover. Somewhere Auntie Philomène was certainly blaming the rental company, the government, and the devil.

Kai looked toward the sound. “What do we do?”

Yseult followed his gaze. Through the trees she could see flashes of white fabric, men carrying chairs, children dancing in the rain because children had more sense than adults. Two hundred people had dressed and perfumed themselves to witness certainty. The uncertainty was standing barefoot at the service gate with mud on its hem.

“I can’t marry you in an hour,” she said.

Kai shut his eyes.

The rain filled the silence.

When he opened them, he nodded once. It was a small movement, but it seemed to pass through his whole body. “All right.”

“I don’t know what that means for us after today.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want you to go on that ship without us deciding what we are.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want to give up Martinique because I am afraid of people talking.”

“Then don’t.”

She searched his face for resentment and found it there, yes, along with fear, along with love, along with the stubbornness that had made her want to kiss him the first time he argued with a fish vendor over the correct price of dorado. None of it canceled the rest.

He said, “I should have told you.”

“Yes.”

“You should have told me.”

“Yes.”

The service gate creaked in the wind. Her father turned then, as if some change in the air had given him permission. He looked from one to the other but did not speak.

Yseult lifted the soaked hem of her dress. “We have to go tell them.”

Kai gave a short, disbelieving laugh. “Now?”

“You have a better plan?”

“I was hoping lightning strike me.”

“Stand by the silk-cotton tree. Good chance.”

This time his laugh broke open properly, and despite everything, hers came with it. For a second they were simply two wet people in ridiculous clothes at the edge of a ruined plan.

Then Naomie appeared at the top of the path, holding an umbrella that had turned inside out. Auntie Philomène was behind her, skirt hitched to her knees, face set for war.

“Yseult Marie Antoine!” Auntie Philomène shouted over the rain. “If you dead, say so!”

Naomie saw Yseult’s face, then Kai’s, and stopped running.

“Oh,” she said.

Auntie Philomène arrived puffing. “What ‘oh’? Why the dress dirty? Why the groom down here? Why your father hiding like obeah man? Father Michel asking if we starting with the rain or waiting out the rain. The cake—never mind the cake. Somebody talk!”

Yseult looked at Kai.

He looked back. “Together?”

She considered. “Together.”

They walked up the path side by side, not touching. Her father followed a few paces behind with the useless umbrella Naomie had handed him. Auntie Philomène muttered prayers, accusations, and instructions to nobody in particular. Naomie carried Yseult’s train, though it was beyond saving, and said quietly, “You all right?”

“No.”

“Good,” Naomie said. “That sound honest.”

By the time they reached the courtyard, the wedding had dissolved into a wet, noisy village. Guests crowded under tents and verandas. Men in good shirts stood ankle-deep in puddles, rescuing programs from the mud. Children slid on the wet grass. Miss Edwina Flood sat beneath a canopy with a plate of accra balanced on her lap, eyes narrowed as she watched her son approach. Father Michel held his Bible under a plastic bag. The three-tier cake leaned heroically on a table near the bar, one side sagging, sugar orchids melting petal by petal.

The microphone still worked. Unfortunately.

Yseult stepped beneath the main tent and took it from Jules before he could ask. The feedback squealed, and every head turned.

Two hundred faces. Some beloved. Some curious. Some already arranging the story for later.

Kai stood beside her. Rain dripped from his hair onto his collar.

Yseult had imagined many versions of herself speaking at her wedding: tearful, radiant, funny, composed. None wore a muddy dress and her grandmother’s chipped blue door around her neck.

She held the microphone with both hands.

“Everybody,” she said.

The tent quieted slowly.

“Thank you for coming. Thank you for dressing so nice in weather that clearly had other plans.”

A few people laughed cautiously.

She looked at Auntie Philomène, who had one hand pressed to her chest as though physically holding in an opinion.

“Kai and I have to talk about some things we should have talked about before today.”

A murmur passed through the guests, quick as wind through cane.

“So we not getting married this afternoon.”

There. The day split. Gasps, whispers, one child saying loudly, “I knew it,” before being shushed.

Kai took the microphone from her, not pulling, asking with his eyes. She let him.

“This is not because anybody do anybody wickedness,” he said, then paused. “Well. Not the dramatic kind.”

More laughter, uneasy but real.

“We sorry for the confusion. We sorry for making allyou come up here in rain for something that not happening how we planned.” He looked at Yseult. “But the food cook already. The bar open already. The band already paid, I hope.”

Jules shouted, “Half!”

Kai grimaced. “Half paid. So please stay. Eat. Dance if you want. Cuss us softly, not too loud. We will understand.”

He handed the microphone back to Jules quickly, as if afraid he might say more.

For three seconds nothing happened.

Then Miss Edwina Flood stood. She set down her plate, walked through the crowd, and came to Kai. She was a small woman with a church hat shaped like a white hibiscus and an expression that had humbled bank managers. Kai lowered his head like a boy awaiting a slap.

She slapped his arm, not hard.

“You foolish?”

“Yes, Ma.”

“You make the girl stand in rain?”

“Yes, Ma.”

“You eat today?”

“No, Ma.”

She turned to Yseult. “You?”

“No, Miss Edwina.”

“Then both of you foolish.” She looked at Auntie Philomène. “Philomène, where you put the plates?”

Auntie Philomène’s mouth opened, closed, then opened again. “By the buffet, where else?”

“Well, move yourself. People hungry.”

It was not forgiveness. It was logistics. In families, logistics often arrived first and made a path for everything else.

The band, sensing survival, began to play something soft and old. Not wedding music. A bolero Yseult’s grandmother used to hum while shelling peas. The rain eased to a silver curtain beyond the tent.

People did stay. Not all—one of Kai’s cousins left in a temper, and two of Auntie Philomène’s church friends departed with faces of injured dignity—but most stayed. They ate stewed chicken and green fig salad, roast breadfruit, callaloo, accra, plantain, and the leaning cake, which finally surrendered at four-thirty and was served in generous, shapeless slabs. Children ran between the tables. The older men drank rum and pretended not to gossip. The younger women did not pretend.

Yseult changed out of the dress into the simple yellow shift she had planned to wear after the reception. Naomie unpinned her hair, one flower at a time, and said nothing until the last pin was out.

“You frighten me,” Naomie said.

“I frighten myself.”

“You want me to beat him?”

“No.”

“You sure? I wearing flat shoes.”

Yseult leaned against her cousin. “Not today.”

Down in the courtyard, Kai sat with his mother beneath the silk-cotton tree. Miss Edwina was talking steadily, one finger raised. Kai listened with the posture of a man being peeled.

Yseult’s father came to her near the bar just before sunset. The rain had washed the sky clean, and the sea held the last light in broken pieces.

“I am leaving,” he said. “Madeleine have a headache.”

Yseult nodded. “Thank you for staying.”

He shifted. “I did not do much.”

“You didn’t leave.”

The words came out before she could soften them. He accepted them without flinching.

“No,” he said. “Not today.”

He kissed her cheek, lightly, almost formally. Then he pressed something into her hand: his handkerchief, folded around a cigarette he must have decided not to smoke.

“For next time you sweating,” he said.

After he left, Yseult walked to the edge of the courtyard where the grass dropped toward the darkening sea. Her bare feet were stained red from the earth. The pendant at her throat felt cool now.

Kai joined her after a while. He had removed his jacket and tie. In one hand he carried two pieces of collapsed cake on paper plates.

“Your aunt say if we not eating it, she carrying it home and charging us emotional damages.”

Yseult took a plate. “Which aunt?”

He gave her a look.

“Fair.”

They ate standing side by side. The cake tasted of nutmeg, almond, and rain.

Kai said, “I called Jules. Told him I can’t confirm the ship yet.”

“You didn’t have to do that tonight.”

“I had to do something tonight.”

She scraped icing with her fork. “What did he say?”

“He say I mad. Then he say they might give me till Monday.”

“And if they don’t?”

“Then they don’t.”

She nodded.

“I am not saying that to impress you,” he said. “I am saying because it is true, and I am trying that now.”

She looked at him. The band had shifted into a quadrille, and Auntie Philomène was forcing Father Michel to dance. The priest moved with surprising lightness. Laughter rose beneath the wet trees.

“I am going to Martinique,” Yseult said.

Kai nodded slowly. “When?”

“September.”

“For three months.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The word was rough, but it held.

“I want the shop,” she said. “But not if it means we both become liars to feed it.”

Kai looked out toward the bay. Fishing boats rocked in the harbor, their small lights coming on one by one. “Then maybe the shop waits.”

“Maybe.”

“I hate maybe.”

“I know.”

He smiled without showing teeth. “You live in maybe.”

“I visit.”

“You have citizenship.”

She nudged him with her shoulder before remembering they were not touching. The contact startled them both, then settled.

Behind them, the bandleader announced that since there was no official first dance, he was taking requests for unofficial foolishness. Someone shouted for soca. Someone else shouted for zouk. Auntie Philomène shouted for dignity and was ignored.

Kai set down his plate. “Can I ask you something?”

“Yes.”

“Not to marry me.”

“Good.”

“Can I dance with you at our not-wedding?”

Yseult looked down at her yellow dress, her muddy feet, the chipped blue pendant. She looked at Kai, at the space between apology and promise where they were standing.

“One song,” she said.

The band began a zouk-love tune so sentimental half the guests groaned and the other half cheered. Kai held out his hand. She took it.

They danced beneath the tent, not as bride and groom, not as husband and wife, not even as people certain of the next morning. Around them, the wedding-that-wasn’t loosened its collar and became a party. Miss Edwina ate cake with Auntie Philomène. Naomie danced with her umbrella. The children spun until they fell laughing into the damp grass.

Kai’s hand rested at Yseult’s back, careful, asking. She let it stay.

Above the estate the clouds broke apart, showing a thin bright seam of evening sky. The old silk-cotton tree dripped steadily onto the empty wedding chairs, each one shining in the after-rain as if newly made.
