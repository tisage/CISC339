When Sigrid Hauge drove over the bridge that arched into Eidsund, the town lay under a late-April sky the color of wet tin. Boats were moored like sleeping animals along the quay, and the smell of fish and diesel lifted in faint ribbons when she rolled down the window. Ten years away had changed her so much she sometimes felt like a foreigner in her own skin, but the place itself had kept its small predictable rhythms: a church bell, the crooked barber's shop with its green door, the bakery that still closed at two for the afternoon coffee.

Her mother waited on the steps of the terraced house, the wool shawl Sigrid had knitted for her years ago around her shoulders, the same shawl now patched at the elbow. Solveig Hauge waved with a hand that trembled less than it had the first winter Sigrid had moved to Oslo; there was a steadiness about her these days. "You're later than the bus, girl," she said, but her voice was pleased.

Sigrid looked at the familiar face and saw, more than anything, the line that cut between her brows whenever someone said the town had changed for the better. "Traffic," she lied, though there was never traffic in Eidsund.

The reason for her return was small and precise: a slip on the stone path had taken the edge off Solveig's hip and the doctor had said bedrest, physiotherapy, nothing to worry about long-term. Sigrid had booked a week of leave from the institute, packed as lightly as she could—her laptop, a battered coat, field notebooks—and come back to steady the household for her mother. The outside world in Oslo thinned to a few emails and a voicemail she listened to and erased.

On the second evening she walked down to the harbor. The light had gone thin and blue, the gulls were loud. There, at the end of the wooden jetty, stood Morten Lind, hunched into his jacket against the wind, hands in his pockets. She knew his profile as surely as she had known the curve of the sea: long nose, a mouth that habitually closed itself into a straight line when he was thinking, the square jaw he had inherited from his father. He had been the sort of boy who climbed to the top of everything and could, at any age, be put to work. He had stayed in Eidsund and become mayor; she had left and become a scientist. Time had shaped them differently but not apart.

They greeted each other like people who had been rehearsing a reunion in their heads for years. There was an awkwardness—the sort that gathers around shared histories—and underneath it a steady warmth. They walked the quay and spoke of trivial things—her mother's fall, the awful weather last winter, the new schoolteacher who used to live in Trondheim. When Morten asked after her work she told him, as if the words came from a safe distance: "I'm still with the marine lab. We do monitoring, site assessments. You know—fish, water quality."

He nodded, a tilt of acknowledgement, and in that motion Sigrid felt both the ease and the distance between them. He told her, in a way that was casual but with an edge, about an increase in complaints from fishers: cod with lesions, crustaceans dying in areas they had always been abundant. "We thought it might be the temperature," he said. "Or red tide. But there's more. We've had rumours."

"Nuisance rumours," Sigrid said. She wanted to smooth his concern. "There are always rumours."

"When they start hauling up blackened kelp and dead juvenile fish, that's a different kind of rumour." He stopped, looked at the horizon where houses stood like white teeth. "Rune says it's nothing. But people ask. It keeps coming up."

Rune Lind—that was a name Sigrid knew. Rune ran Eidsund Fisk & Prosess, the processing plant with the corrugated roof and the long pipe that jutted into the inlet. The plant had been the town's heart for decades. It let a family eat in good years and in bad; it painted the quay with nets and lanterns and the clatter of boxes. For many, saying anything against the plant was like saying there was no god on Sunday.

Sigrid smiled and tried to tuck her fingers into her pockets to hide their small tremor. "You should get someone to test," she suggested, the scientist in her warming up. "It might be nothing, but with the nursery grounds near the south shallows—"

"We can't send the county people in every time someone sees a dead crab," Morten said. "People don't want to go through more investigations. They are tired." He kept his voice light; his eyes were not.

A week had room enough for a lot of things. Sigrid set up her field kit in the garage—an icebox, sample jars, gloves, a hand-held probe that read salinity and temperature. The equipment was smaller than her memories of it, and yet it gave her a sense of rightful competence. By the third morning she had a routine: collect three samples at the mouth of the inlet, at the quay, at the shallows near the nursery beds. She walked the shore at low tide and noted black-streaked kelp, limp with not much smell but with the sense of being poisoned.

She ran the tests with the care of a ritual. The numbers blinked cold and dry on the display: pH within normal, oxygen levels marginal, temperatures not significantly out of range. But there were oddities—the assays for certain processing chemicals, for dyes used in scaling and brine processing, returned results Sigrid did not expect. She took a second, and then a third sample. The lab equipment in Eidsund would not give her the full analysis; for that she needed the institute in Oslo. She called in a favor.

When the courier arrived two days later with the printed results, Sigrid sat at the kitchen table and read the page as if it had come in a foreign language. There were elevated readings of a compound used in the plant's cleaning process, a phosphonate stabilizer that, in high concentrations, damages fish gill tissue and interferes with larval development. In low doses it lingered, binding to the silt and releasing slowly. It was not a chemical meant to make headlines. It was the kind of thing that killed subtly, cumulative and hidden.

Her phone pinged. A message from Rune: we should meet. He used the ellipsis he always used, an attempt at easy familiarity. She wrote back, because curiosity was a muscle she could not leave unused.

They met at the plant's office, a little room with a table that had seen better days. Rune was larger than Morten, broader in the shoulders, his hair cut short like a man always ready to get his hands dirty. He offered coffee and a smile that did not reach his eyes.

"Sigrid," he said. "Nice to see you back. Mom said you were in town."

"You haven't changed," she said automatically; people like Rune rarely did. He laughed and folded his hands together. "So, what brings the big city to little Eidsund?"

She told him. She told him about the samples and the assay and the chemical she had found. He listened until the end, and then his mouth tightened.

"You understand what this would mean for us," he said finally. "If there's anything from the plant—"

"You can't be—" She had to stop herself from saying what she had once thought plainly: that the plant was the town, that to close it was to shut the heartbeat of Eidsund. Instead she sounded like a scientist: "We need to be sure. If there's a leak, you should trace it. If it's coming from you, there's remediation. If not, that's also important."

Rune tapped the desk. "Morten told me you'd be back," he said. "He said you left with high ideals."

Sigrid's throat went dry. "I didn't leave with anything but my own head," she said. She had not meant to sound defensive. Rune's smile trembled.

"Look, we're not monsters," he said. "We process fish. We pay wages. If there's something, it's terrible. But closing the plant…people won't forgive you."

Sigrid thought of the women who stacked fillets at three in the morning, of men who mended nets with fingers that had no sense of gentleness. She thought of the nursery beds, of smolt that struggled in their first months like children learning to walk. Her work had taught her the cruelty of systems—how damage finds its way down the food chain in ways ordinary folk could not see until a crisis took them by surprise.

"Will you work with us to test?" Rune asked. "On our side? Have the samples run through the county, get neutral people?"

Sigrid almost said yes. The suggestion would preserve everything that was precious in this town. It would soothe unease. But there was that brief prick of memory like a fossil; in university she had learned that a neutral test in a closed system could be arranged. She had seen companies commission studies to confirm what they wanted. "I can go with you," she said carefully. "But you should also let me send samples to the institute."

Rune's jaw shifted. He seemed to understand the implication. "It will look like we were hiding something," he said.

"Or you'll have cleared your name," Sigrid countered. "There is precedent. We can—"

That night she lay awake and considered the problem as if it were a map. There were lines she could trace to outcomes: tell the county and trigger an investigation that might reveal the plant's improprieties; keep the information private and work with Rune to commission a local test; sit on the evidence and say nothing. Each line had consequences. The town was threaded through by blood and marriage and favors; a single report could rend the fabric.

Morten came around the next day with a thermos of coffee and the look of a man who meant to arrest a conversation before it became something else. "What did they say?" he asked.

Sigrid poured him coffee. "There's a phosphonate compound in the water. It's used in cleaning and in processing. It's not inherently dramatic, but at the levels in the shallows it could explain the lesions and the juvenile losses."

Morten's face closed in on itself. "Have you told Rune?"

"Not fully," she admitted. "But I gave him the basics."

"Rune will do damage control," Morten said. "The plant's been getting by on thin margins the last few years. You know that."

"I know." She realized she knew more than she wanted. People had called the plant a stubborn ox: too big to fail and too old to lean on change. Morten rubbed his temple. "If this goes public—people will lose jobs."

"And if it doesn't?" She could feel patience evaporating. "And if the fish die over years because chemicals build up? What then? The town will still be poor. Just poorer, for a different reason."

He swallowed. "You're not one to not say what you think. When we were kids—"

"When you broke your arm on the school dock and I told your mother you fell off the cliff." He smiled at the memory, and it softened him for a heartbeat. "We were always honest with each other."

"Are we, though?" she said. "Are we honest now when it matters?"

He looked at her like someone trying to find the right tool for a job. "Sigrid," he said, "I can't lose this town."

She heard him, and she also heard the sound of the sea beyond the jetty—a steady, indifferent wash. The choice became clearer in the hum of the moment: To protect a man and a community from the immediate pain of truth, while letting a slow sickness ripple through the coastal life, or to propel a rupture that would bruise everyone at once but might save the fjord.

Weeks folded into each other. People came to the shop window with rumors. A fisher's wife held up a small net filled with dead crabs and asked what Sigrid knew. Children who had dredged the south shallows came home with sores. Morten organized town meetings, voices raised over the future. Rune asked for time and discretion. Sigrid ran more tests; she compared them with historical data. She wrote reports she did not send.

The moral architecture of a small town makes secrets into seams that bind you to one another. Many people came to her in half-phrases that tested the edges of confession and accusation. "You're not thinking of stirring up a county inspection, are you?" old Mrs. Karlsen asked in the post office. "You know what happens when they come; regulations, forms, people out of jobs."

"It might be a necessary pain," Sigrid told her. "Only it seems to me that it's the sort of pain that people are less likely to survive."

People's livelihoods were the currency by which loyalty measured itself. Rune's employees came to the plant on mornings when the sky was the color of cold glass, and they expected their wages. They did not see what the water carried home at night. If Sigrid had lived her life as Morten did, she might have made a different calculation. But she had seen the long arc of pollution in other places—a chain of incremental losses until one day there was nothing left but stone and a rumor. Her training told her that slow damage could be worse than a sudden collapse because it allowed denial to take root.

On a Thursday in late May, Sigrid received an email from Oslo: the institute had completed its full panel. The file attachment felt heavy on the screen. She opened it and read with a nakedness she had expected to feel more when the results first came in. The phosphonate levels at the south shallows were consistently above background; deposition patterns indicated a point-source outflow entering the inlet, not diffuse runoff. The compound found in the samples matched the formula used at Eidsund Fisk & Prosess.

She printed the pages, folded them, and put them in her bag. The printer's whirr sounded obscene in the small house. The world narrowed to the rectangle of paper and the room's small familiar objects—the mantle clock, the knitting basket, the photograph of her mother on a younger day. Truth, she thought, is a kind of blade. To expose it was to cut something. She had always believed one should prefer clean cuts to hidden infections, but the practice of that belief had never been this intimate.

That afternoon Morten came by, his face weathered, someone who had lived with the town carved into him. He sat and watched her fold the pages while his hands found the edge of the table. "Is this the paper?" he asked.

She slid it toward him. He read in a way that suggested he was trying to graft new language onto an old understanding. When he had read enough, he put his hands over the paper as if to keep it from blowing away.

"If this is public," he said, "they'll point to Rune. They will call him careless, and then they will call for him to step down. They will call for the council to open our books. People will be hungry."

"And they may also have healthier children," Sigrid said. "And fish that can come back."

"You just see what will live and die in a set of numbers. You don't see what it does to a town when the plant closes." There was no accusation in his voice; only a kind of exhausted pleading. "If it is the plant—work with us to fix it. Give us a chance."

He was asking her to be merciful in a way that would alter the shape of her life. It was an intimate plea, made intimate by the fact that the man whose life she might end was the man she had—at one time—imagined a life with. In the years after she left, they had written to each other like retained pieces of the town: half-laughing, occasional, an exchange at holidays. They had not, in truth, remained entirely apart. In those weeks at the quay, he had kissed the shell of her ear under a sky that had the taste of coming rain, and it had been like a promise.

Morten's hand closed around the paper. "You can take this to the county inspector," he said. "You can leave me in the dark and say nothing. But tell me first so I can warn Rune. Help us control the narrative."

"Control the narrative." The phrase bounced in Sigrid's chest. It sounded like a euphemism for covering up. "Do you understand that if we 'control' it and the county finds evidence later, the penalties will be harsher? It's better to do this cleanly."

"Or we do it quietly and fix it." His voice softened. He reached for her hand but drew it back, as if some unseen etiquette forbade the contact. "Don't be the one who does this to the town, Sigrid."

She did not reach for his hand. Her fingers curled under the table. "I cannot not do my work because it breaks something fragile between us," she said. "That's not mercy. It is cowardice."

"Or it is the trade-offs a small community makes to survive," he countered. "We all do things."

There, compact and ordinary, was the meaning of his words: complicity as a form of communal survival. Sigrid thought of other places she had worked, where compromise had been a tool and a weapon. She had been younger then and believed in the sharpness of truth; now she knew that truth and honesty were stitches on a garment that sometimes needed to be rethreaded. Her head said one thing and something else—a memory of a small girl by the school dock, throwing pieces of bread to gulls—sat in her chest, querying.

That night, she dreamt of the sea rising through the town streets like a slow generous thief. She woke with the taste of salt in her mouth and a certainty that bred in her like a seed.

In the morning she went to the county office. It was a gray building, bureaucratic and stern, with an inspector who had cold eyes the color of glacier meltwater. "We receive this in good faith," he said, smoothing the pages like a prayer. "We will open an investigation."

Between the meeting and the call to Rune and the whispers that leaked like little fishbones through the town, the world of Eidsund narrowed until each person was under an interrogative light. Rune's face in the days that followed wore the look of a man unmade by shame. He denied on television that there had been a conscious decision to discharge chemicals. He said the plant followed regulation, and then did not have the data he claimed. His employees clustered in the break room and tried to find words that would not betray fear.

When the inspector's men came with their clipboards and their tape measures, the plant offices were in a small uproar. Machines stopped while they took readings. The community gathered in clusters and spoke in the measured voices of people who believed that the truth, when unveiled, would either break them or set them free. Morten moved through the crowds like a man walking a tightrope, keeping an eye on the inspector and on Rune and on Sigrid, whose presence he noticed and sometimes avoided.

At noon a woman named Hedda stood in the doorway of the bakery and had a seizure of anger she could not hold back. "If the plant goes," she said, her voice raw, "who will feed my kids? Who will pay for school clothes? I can't have that." Her words hung in the air like a defiant flag.

For Sigrid, the hearings were a formal enactment of the decision she had made. Evidence was presented, and Rune's paperwork was found wanting. It was not a single intentional criminal act, the inspector explained; it was a pattern of neglect, of corners cut and of compliance forms that had been filed with errors that stacked like a crooked foundation. The final report recommended fines and a temporary shutdown until a remediation plan was in place. It was, as these things go, the sort of public humiliation that looked like justice and tasted, to many, like punishment.

The town felt the impact in shapes: sudden layoffs, a diminished buzz at the quay, the closure of the bar on slow nights. The council called special meetings and tried to thread aid into the cracks; lottery grants, fundraisers, offers of retraining. But there were losses that no public policy could soften—people who had worked at the plant their whole lives, whose muscles knew the rhythm of the conveyor belts, who found their day changing into a strange blankness.

Morten's role in the catastrophe became a marrow of gossip. He had approved a zoning exception five years earlier to let the plant extend its outflow pipe; he had sat on council minutes that, in hindsight, looked like small capitulations to convenience. He had, his critics said, allowed profit to exceed prudence. He had not been personally responsible for the discharge, but he had carried the authority that allowed the plant to expand. A statute of responsibility is not always the same as guilt, but in a small town the two often kiss as closely as siblings.

When the council convened to consider a vote of no confidence in the mayor, the room smelled of coffee gone cold and the thought of more than one life. Rune cried in the back while his older sister, wearing a plate of spectacles that made her look stern, spoke of jobs and of loyalty. Morten sat like a man who had been built for other things: leadership in a town that is mostly filial duty and practical compromise.

The vote passed, measured by a small margin. Morten's hand shook when he spoke his resignation. There was no spectacle. He did not lose his temper or make a grand plea; he simply said he had made decisions that, in hindsight, had been mistakes.

Sigrid watched him resign with a coldness she did not intend. A part of her wanted to soften the blow; another part, made of long nights of data and cold lab lights, felt the inevitable weight of consequence. When she saw him after—the two of them on the jetty, wind cutting into their faces—Morten stood with his back to the sea and did not look at her.

"Why?" he asked, and the word contained the question of a man who had endured the slow erosion of his life in public squares.

"Because the water was sick and I knew how to check it," she said. "Because if I didn't, it would keep getting sicker."

"But you did not have to make it this way," he said. He was pleading now, not for himself but for the town as if the town were a single being. "You could have worked with us. Let us fix it before the papers came."

"Fix it how, Morten?" She looked at him in a way that felt like stripping. "By us paying for remediation? By us sweeping it under in exchange for wages? How many times do you think they'll come back and find another hole because it was faster to patch than to rebuild?"

He swallowed. "Do you not see what you've done?"

"I see what I have done," she replied. "I see what will be. And I know I'm on the right side of the water, even if it breaks things."

There was a tenderness in his voice then, a softness that cut her more than anything else. "We loved each other," he said. It was a simple fact, old and large, and it landed between them like debris.

"Yes," she said. "We did."

They did not say what they meant to each other—flawed as their past was, it did not unspool in tidy endings. That night, they slept apart. The town had changed; their relationship had changed into something that could be visited with quiet proofs but could not be stitched back together into a life. Morten came to her door once weeks later with a bag of cod he had fished himself. They ate and talked about small things; they did not speak of the council meeting or the inspector or the plant's fines. In the evenings Sigrid would find herself walking the shore, where the sea licked at rocks with a patient insistence that seemed at once to accept and to demand.

The plant closed for three months while remediation plans were drafted and later partially executed. There were layoffs and hardship, but there were also grants and volunteers. A small committee formed to create sustainable employment projects; fishermen were offered retraining in aquaculture, in seaweed cultivation, in tourism. It was not enough, and it was enough in a way that is always provisional.

Sigrid became, unwillingly and then unwillingly still more, both a pariah and a quiet hero. People accused her at the bakery and later, more softly, asked if the samples were correct. Her name signified, to some, a betrayal of kin; to others, the preservation of things that mattered beyond wages and immediate comfort. It was an ambivalence she had seen in other places. When the short fish run came back a year later—numbers climbing slowly, the water clearing—some people saw in it vindication. Others said it had nothing to do with her name. The town breathed a collective sigh in different measures.

Morten found work not as mayor but running a small harbor service. He came to the committee meetings now and then, offering practical suggestions that sometimes hit the mark. He did not talk to Sigrid much, though his presence in the room was like a constant chord. When they did speak it was in the gentle economy of people who had known each other's bones. He called once on a raw evening and asked her to speak to a group of schoolchildren about the water. "They want to know what happens when things go wrong," the teacher said. "They want to understand."

Sigrid put together a talk that was less an instruction than a story. She told them about small chemistry and about fish, about patience and about listening to the signs the sea sends. A child asked if she was sad about what happened. "Sometimes," she answered. "But I think it was the only way for things to be fixed."

"Was it worth it?" the child pressed, blue scarf knotted under his chin.

Sigrid looked at the flock of children gathered, their knees muddy and their eyes bright with curiosity. She thought of Morten in the municipal office, of Rune wiping his face in a back room, of her mother knitting by the window. She thought of the fish that had returned like the cautious steps of something healing itself.

"It was the honest thing," she said, and the words felt like a small coin she wasn't sure she wanted to spend in that place. The child nodded as if this answered everything and nothing.

Months later, Sigrid sat with her mother and watched the tide come in until it covered the stones. Solveig's hands lay folded on her lap, the shawl warm on her shoulders. "You were always difficult," her mother said gently, then laughed. "Not rude. Difficult. It was a quality in our family."

Sigrid smiled. "Your mother—my grandmother—used to say that truth is like the winter road. It can be cleared, but sometimes you have to accept that it's dangerous to go out at night."

Solveig's mouth made a face. "We all have to decide which roads we take. Your road has a cliff and a light on it." She tapped the table. "Don't think I do not see the cost."

"I don't want the cost unseen," Sigrid said. "I don't enjoy a town looking at me the way some do now."

Solveig put her hand over Sigrid's. "Neither did I expect eternal gratitude," she said. "But you are still my daughter."

In the spring after the plant's remediation, Sigrid took a contract to run a small research station in Eidsund. It was not the plum post she still imagined herself deserving of—the institute in Oslo had greater pull—but it gave her space to monitor the fjord through its slow return. She rented a little room above the harbor, where the floorboards sighed in the night and her kettle took on the smell of fish smoke. The position put her in the town's center in a way that was intimate and lonely: she was both one who had caused harm and one who might help remediate it. It was, in other words, an accurate fit.

She and Morten exchanged the shorthand of practicalities. He stopped by once in the autumn with a crate of seaweed they'd been tending—he had adapted to the new rhythms, finding a patience he had not before needed. They did not return to the idea of a life together. They met in the middle passages of a town that had chosen to be practical with its affection.

Once, at the fish market, an old man spat bile in her direction for breaking the plant. She nodded. "You ought to spit in the river," she told him, because she did not want to have the last word about what she had done. He did not.

When the first smelt came back in decent numbers, they celebrated with a small festival. There was music and the scent of frying oil and the town square filled with people who had walked different paths to that night. Morten set up a small stand for harbor services and watched the children dance. Sigrid watched from a bench, half-hidden, her hands warm in her coat pockets.

Toward midnight he walked over. "You did not have to be the martyr," he said softly.

"I didn't want to be a martyr. I wanted to be someone who didn't let the sea die because it was inconvenient," she said.

They stood in the mild press of the crowd, two figures bound to the same history. "I don't know if this is forgiven," he said.

"Forgiveness is not a thing you can order in the town library," she answered. "But we can act differently. We can sew. We can learn where the seams are."

He smiled in a way that suggested both sadness and gratitude. It was not a reconciliation; it didn't attempt to be. It was a moment where two lives touched with the memory of what had been.

Years later, when Sigrid walked the shore in winter, the water had an edge to it that was not quite indifference and not quite malice. It was like a living thing that had been observed and tended and could therefore be more hospitable for the future than it had been in the past. Fishermen spoke with a quiet respect about how the juvenile fish had the time and space to grow; mothers found their children's skin clearing up from sores that had once been common. There was work to do. There would always be work to do.

She sometimes thought of the simpler path she had not taken: what her life might have been if she had let the report die in her drawer, if she had let Morten sleep with the comfortable illusion of being unblamed. She thought of the faces in the town—the ones who had lost jobs and the ones who had lost the fear of the water. There was no neat account to be made. Only a ledger of intimate details that meant something different to everyone involved.

On a spring morning almost three years after her return, Sigrid stood at the camera in the institute's small conference room and spoke about the long habits of sea and human error. Her voice had the same steady accuracy it had always had; the people who had come to listen were a mix of researchers and townsfolk. After, a man approached her, shy and young and with red fingers from working a net.

"You did the right thing," the man said, and what he meant was, in that small way, a closing.

Sigrid folded her hands. "The right thing is not always the kind one," she said.

He blinked, puzzled, and then nodded as if he had been given permission to accept complexity.

That evening she walked the jetty where the town's lights blinked against the dark water, and Morten came to stand beside her without ceremony. They were no longer young lovers on the brink of choice. They were people who had been made larger and smaller by what they had done and not done. He rested his hand on the rail and watched the sea with the gravity of a man who had had his hand in the coffin and in the cradle.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked. It was a quiet question with no accusation.

Sigrid thought of the child who had asked if it had been worth it, of her mother's hands, of Rune's weary face. She thought of the chemical reading that had stared at her from a laboratory printout and the way water had always told its truths if only someone would listen.

"I regret some things," she said finally. "I regret that people had to lose their work. I regret that it broke us in places even love couldn't mend. But I don't regret knowing what I knew and doing something about it."

He sighed, a small sound that carried the weight of acceptance. "We will always live with the consequences," he said.

"We will," she agreed. They stood in the cold wind until the stars were pale and the gulls had gone to roost, and there was no spectacle of forgiveness or absolution. There was simply the sea, listening to them both, a long-bodied presence that had no need for stories except as they fed its tides.

Sigrid watched the water. Sometimes a truth is a wound, sometimes it is a cure. Often, it is both. The town breathed around them—some following, some turned away—and the shore kept its gentle unhurried work of erosion and of return. The choice she had made sat with her like an old companion: rough and honest and sometimes weary. It had cost her something, and it had given something back. The balance struck itself not in the language of victory, but in the small acts of tending the damaged and the damaged thing that had been tended. She had told the truth. The relationship she had cared for had altered, as all things do under pressure. Both things existed now, neither wholly destroyed nor wholly unscathed, and that, perhaps, was the only kind of end that could be true.
