When the summons came, Avani Khatri was waist-deep in a well shaft, arguing with a mason about lime.

“The stone has shifted,” the mason called down. “No mortar will hold it.”

“Then stop feeding it mortar,” Avani said. She pressed her palm against the cool wall. The well had been dry for seven years, but the lower stones sweated faintly. “Pull it apart to the third ring. Replace the cracked blocks. And put the drain holes back.”

“The drain holes are what let the water out.”

“The drain holes are what keep the wall from bursting when it returns.”

The mason looked at her as one might look at a person who had begun speaking to goats.

Then a royal horse stamped beside the well, and a courier in blue-and-gold livery leaned over the lip.

“Avani Khatri?”

She shaded her eyes.

“The Maharaja commands your attendance at Aftabpur.”

The word commands made the mason straighten. Avani did not.

“For what?”

The courier unrolled a scroll. “For service to the crown in the matter of the last dragon.”

The mason crossed himself in the old hill manner, touching forehead, throat, and heart.

Avani looked again at the sweating stones around her.

“Tell the Maharaja,” she said, “that I am repairing a well.”

The courier’s face tightened. “Your mother served the crown in the same matter.”

That was enough to make Avani climb out.

Her mother had vanished fifteen years earlier in the northern mountains. The official account said Nirmala Khatri had died while surveying the roads to Kesar Kot, the ruined city beneath Mount Raktashila. In the village version, she had been carried away by the dragon.

Avani had never believed either account completely.

Still, she took the courier’s horse.

Aftabpur shimmered in late summer heat. The palace pools had been allowed to run dry, though the royal gardens remained green behind their carved sandstone walls. Water came first to the palace, then to the army barracks, then to the dye markets, then to everyone else. That was the order of things now.

Maharaja Harivardhan received her in a chamber cooled by wet khus screens. He was not an old man, though his beard had been dyed so black that it made his skin look pale. Beside him stood Captain Tej Bahadur, commander of the royal guard, broad-shouldered and polished as a new sword. At a low table sat Pundit Somesh, keeper of the royal chronicles, with three bound volumes spread before him.

On the carpet lay a long iron tube wrapped in oilcloth.

Avani knew it at once. A fire-lance. Not a soldier’s small hand cannon, but a mining piece designed to drive a charge deep into rock.

“You know what happened at Kesar Kot,” said the Maharaja.

“I know what I was taught.”

“Then repeat it.”

Avani kept her eyes on him. “One hundred and seventy-three years ago, the dragon of Raktashila descended on Kesar Kot. The king refused it tribute. It burned the city, consumed the royal household, and drove the surviving people into the plains. Since then it has slept beneath the mountain.”

Pundit Somesh nodded. His lips barely moved when he did so, as if he were conserving truth.

“The beast has woken,” said Harivardhan. “Shepherds report smoke from the upper ravines. Caravans have disappeared. Two forts on the northern road have lost their wells. The dragon has begun to move beneath the mountain.”

“Why send me?” Avani asked.

The Maharaja glanced at the covered fire-lance.

“Because Captain Tej Bahadur can lead men into a cave,” he said, “but only a Khatri can read old waterworks and stone pressure. The dragon’s lair lies under the ruined city. Your mother mapped its outer tunnels before she died. You will take her notes. You will find the chamber. You will place the charge where it will bring the mountain down.”

“And if the dragon is elsewhere?”

“Then Captain Tej Bahadur will persuade it to return.”

The captain smiled. There was a pale seam across his chin, perhaps an old sword wound. “I have brought thirty men, iron nets, oil jars, and enough powder to wake the ancestors.”

Avani looked at the fire-lance.

“Why not send an army?”

“Because armies eat,” said the Maharaja. “And because I would prefer the northern road open before the monsoon.”

There had been no proper monsoon in three years.

Pundit Somesh turned one of the chronicles toward her. On the page was a painting of the dragon. It had red scales, a mane of flame, and a woman’s jeweled hand dangling from its mouth.

“The beast is called Vahni,” he said. “Fire. She has worn many shapes in our histories, but this is the truest.”

Avani studied the painting. The artist had never seen a dragon. The hind legs were too small, the wings too neat. But the ruined city below it was carefully rendered: domes split open, minarets fallen, people running beneath a red sky.

At the bottom of the page, in faded ink, was a line from the old king’s proclamation.

Let no tongue soften the cruelty of the serpent.

Avani touched the margin.

“This is not the same hand as the rest.”

Pundit Somesh shut the book.

“You have sharp eyes.”

“My mother taught me to notice repairs.”

The Maharaja’s expression cooled.

“Then notice this, Avani Khatri: your village well will be repaired at royal expense when you return. The tax arrears on your family’s land will be forgiven. Your younger brother will be given a post in the river office.”

“And if I do not return?”

“Then your brother will receive the post in your memory.”

Captain Tej Bahadur laughed softly.

Avani bowed because there was nothing else to do.

They left Aftabpur at dawn.

The expedition climbed north through fields of cracked millet and villages where women stood in dry ponds, scraping mud from the bottom with brass bowls. At every stop people came to look at the soldiers and the powder mules. Some offered sweets and flowers. Others spat after the party had passed.

Captain Tej Bahadur rode at the front. Pundit Somesh traveled in a covered litter with his books. Avani walked beside the pack animals, carrying her mother’s old survey case: measuring chains, plumb bobs, a brass compass, wax tablets, and a roll of maps copied from the palace archives.

On the third day, at a roadside shrine, an old woman caught Avani by the wrist.

Her fingers were thin but strong.

“You are Nirmala’s girl,” she said.

Avani stopped.

The old woman wore a patched indigo shawl and had the clouded eyes of a person who had spent years staring into bright snow.

“Who are you?”

“Lata. I cooked in the survey camp at Raktashila.”

“My mother’s camp?”

Lata nodded. “She had hair like yours. Always falling out of its braid. Always telling men with swords that they could not pitch tents on a slope.”

Captain Tej Bahadur called from ahead, “Khatri.”

Avani ignored him for a moment.

“What happened to her?” she asked.

The old woman’s grip tightened.

“She found something in the old city. Not gold. Not a tomb. A water map, she said. A whole kingdom under the mountain.” Lata swallowed. “Then the captain before this one told us the survey was finished. He sent us down. Your mother did not come.”

“Was there a dragon?”

Lata stared toward the white teeth of Raktashila far beyond the road.

“There was a sound,” she said. “Like a mountain breathing through its teeth.”

Then Captain Tej Bahadur turned his horse around.

Avani pulled free and rejoined the column.

That night she unrolled her mother’s maps by the fire. The palace copy showed a scattering of tunnels beneath Kesar Kot, marked with old warnings: collapsed passages, poisonous fumes, dragon vents. Yet the measurements were peculiar. Straight lines joined chambers that should have had no connection. Small symbols repeated beside every spring: circles with three downward strokes.

Water gates.

Avani traced one line with her finger. It ran from the mountain’s eastern face to a point far below the ruins. Another stretched westward, toward the dry plains where Aftabpur now stood.

Someone had drawn a river under the mountain.

By the sixth day, the land changed. Dry grass gave way to slate and thorn. Streams appeared between boulders, icy and quick, then vanished under stone. The ruined walls of Kesar Kot emerged at sunset: black arches half-swallowed by scree, domes tilted like broken bowls, watchtowers with trees growing from their windows.

No birds sang there.

The soldiers made camp in a former market square. Wind moved through the empty streets with a low whistling sound.

Pundit Somesh stood before a fallen gateway, reading the old inscriptions.

“Here,” he said, with the reverence of a priest at an altar. “The final testimony.”

The gate’s carved panels showed a crowned king raising a sword toward a winged creature. Beneath them, half obscured by moss, were lines of writing in the formal court script.

Pundit Somesh began translating aloud.

“Let it be remembered that King Varaditya, Defender of the Sun Throne, met the ravenous beast in battle—”

“Stop,” Avani said.

The pundit frowned.

“The first line,” she said. “You skipped it.”

He looked down.

“It is damaged.”

“It says something about the gates.”

Captain Tej Bahadur came over. “Does it matter?”

“It might.”

Pundit Somesh wiped moss from the stone with his sleeve. His mouth tightened.

The first line was clear enough once cleaned.

When the upper gates broke, the Queen commanded the river be turned.

Avani looked at the relief again. The crowned figure was not holding a sword. It was holding a long-handled lever. Behind it, men strained against what looked like a wheel. The winged creature did not descend upon them. Its body curved around the city, its wings spread over small carved figures fleeing through an open arch.

“An allegory,” said Pundit Somesh sharply. “The dragon is a flood. The king conquered it.”

“Then why does the inscription call the crowned figure Queen?” Avani asked.

No one answered.

That night, the mountain spoke.

The sound began after midnight: a deep, hollow pulse that passed through the stone beneath Avani’s bedroll and into her bones. Not a roar. Not thunder. It came again, slower, with a shuddering exhalation.

The horses screamed.

By dawn, one of the powder mules had gone missing. Its tether was found cut cleanly in two, not burned. The soldiers searched the streets and found it standing in a dry cistern, trembling but unharmed.

At the bottom of the cistern was an opening no wider than a doorway.

Captain Tej Bahadur ordered ropes lowered.

Avani went first.

The air beneath Kesar Kot was wet and warm. Her lantern revealed walls of fitted stone descending in a spiral. They were not natural caves. Brass pipes ran along the ceiling, green with age. Every few yards, a stone basin received a steady trickle of clear water.

The passage opened into a vaulted chamber large enough to hold a palace courtyard. At its center stood a wheel taller than a house. The wheel’s paddles turned slowly in a black channel, driven by water rushing below.

On the far wall was a mosaic.

Avani walked toward it, forgetting the soldiers behind her.

It showed a mountain split open, a city beneath it, and a river divided into many shining branches. At one side stood a woman in a crown, her hands on the same great wheel. Beside her crouched a dragon.

This dragon was blue-black, not red. It had a long narrow head, ridged horns, and gold eyes. Chains looped around neither its neck nor its claws. The woman’s hand rested against its snout.

Below them, in old script, ran a sentence:

Let Vahni keep the high waters, and let the high waters keep the people.

Pundit Somesh made a sound like a cough.

Captain Tej Bahadur said, “Old kings worshiped strange things.”

“No,” Avani said. “They built with them.”

Behind the mosaic, one of the brass pipes groaned. A seam opened in the wall, spilling a breath of air that smelled of rain on hot dust.

Then a voice said, from somewhere in the dark:

“Nirmala’s daughter.”

Every soldier raised a weapon.

Avani’s lantern shook in her hand.

The voice came again, not loud, but it filled the chamber. Its words had a strange cadence, each syllable carrying a faint rasp beneath it.

“She had a better sense of direction.”

From the darkness beyond the waterwheel, something moved.

At first Avani saw only an eye, gold and immense, opening between pillars. Then the head followed: long as a riverboat, scaled in dark blue plates that flashed green where lantern light struck them. One horn had broken near the base. The dragon’s muzzle was scarred with old iron burns.

Chains ran from a bronze collar around her neck into the stone floor.

Vahni was thinner than any legend had shown. Her wings were folded tightly around her body, their membranes torn in places. Steam rose from the gaps between her scales.

The soldiers backed away.

Captain Tej Bahadur did not. He lifted his sword.

“Behold,” he said. “The devourer.”

Vahni’s gold eye settled on him.

“You smell of the same house as Varaditya,” she said.

“Do you remember him?”

“I remember all the people he left screaming under the gates.”

Captain Tej Bahadur’s face changed. Only slightly. “Lies.”

“Of course,” said Vahni. “That is why your king brought books.”

Pundit Somesh clutched one of the chronicles against his chest.

Avani stepped forward before she decided to.

“What happened here?” she asked.

The dragon turned her head. The chain at her neck clinked softly.

“Your mother asked better questions.”

“Where is she?”

Vahni’s gaze did not move.

“Dead.”

The word struck Avani more cleanly than she expected.

“Did you kill her?”

“No.”

The dragon raised one foreclaw. Between two talons lay a small tarnished object.

Avani recognized the brass compass before it touched the ground.

It had been her mother’s. The lid bore a dent shaped like a crescent moon.

“She came through the eastern tunnels,” said Vahni. “She found the old gates. She understood them. Then men from Aftabpur came with powder.”

Captain Tej Bahadur spoke quickly. “Do not listen. It imitates speech. It has had centuries to poison this place.”

Vahni looked at him.

“Your grandfather was among those men. He wore silver rings on each thumb. He wept after he cut her throat.”

For the first time, the captain’s sword lowered.

Avani picked up the compass. There was something folded inside it, behind the needle. A scrap of oiled cloth, blackened at the edges.

Her mother’s handwriting covered it.

Avani—

The king’s histories are backwards. The dragon did not destroy Kesar Kot. Varaditya broke the eastern gate to drown the hill clans who had rebelled against him. Queen Samira opened the lower channels to carry the flood away. Vahni held the breach with her body until the water passed.

Varaditya took the throne afterward. He blamed Vahni for the dead. He chained her into the gate system because only her heat keeps the upper reservoir from freezing and bursting the mountain.

The channels still reach the plains. They could water half the kingdom, if the crown had not sealed them.

Do not let them use powder in the heart chamber.

The note ended there.

Avani read it twice.

Above her, through layers of stone, came a dull boom.

Captain Tej Bahadur’s men had begun moving the powder barrels.

“You knew,” Avani said.

The captain’s face hardened into something almost weary.

“I knew enough. The mountain is full of water. The king wants it released.”

“Into the plains?”

“Into the royal canals. The old channels are cracked. The mountain must be opened. The dragon is an obstacle.”

“She is holding it together.”

“For now.” He gestured toward Vahni. “Look at her. One storm, one hard winter, and those chains will not matter. The reservoir will break anyway. Better that we choose where the water goes.”

“To Aftabpur first,” Avani said.

“And then to the kingdom.”

“The villages on the northern slope will be washed away.”

“They are hill clans. They know how to move uphill.”

Avani heard the old woman Lata’s voice: There was a sound like a mountain breathing through its teeth.

Captain Tej Bahadur took the fire-lance from one of his men.

“Step aside, Khatri.”

Vahni shifted. Her chains pulled taut. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, a tremendous pressure groaned.

“You cannot kill me with that,” the dragon said.

“We do not need to,” said the captain. “We only need to bury you.”

Avani looked at the waterwheel, the brass pipes, the lines in her mother’s map. The heart chamber lay beyond Vahni’s chains, where the old river split east and west. The powder was meant for the central rock wall. If that wall fell, the whole reservoir would pour through the eastern breach toward the slope villages before the western channels had time to open.

Unless the channels were opened first.

“Captain,” Avani said, “the charge needs to go deeper. The stone here will only shear sideways.”

He looked at her.

“Show me.”

She picked up her survey case.

“The heart chamber has two old spillways. If we break the western seals, the pressure will divide. You want water for Aftabpur. This is the way.”

Captain Tej Bahadur stared at her for a long moment.

Then he smiled.

“I knew the Maharaja chose well.”

He ordered six men to carry powder after her.

Avani led them past Vahni. As she passed the dragon’s great head, Vahni spoke so quietly that only Avani heard.

“The west gate is dead.”

“I know,” Avani murmured.

“You cannot open it.”

“I can make a hole.”

The heart chamber was not a chamber at all, but an abyss. A stone bridge crossed it, built over roaring darkness. On either side, brass gates filled enormous arches. The eastern gate was cracked and bowed inward. Water sprayed from its seams in glittering threads.

The western gate was sealed behind a wall of newer stone, mortar pale against the ancient blocks.

Avani’s mother had marked it on the map.

Captain Tej Bahadur walked to the edge of the bridge and looked down.

“Put the charges there,” he said, pointing at the eastern gate supports.

“No,” Avani said. “There. The west seal.”

His hand went to his sword.

“You are trying my patience.”

“If you take the eastern supports first, the bridge goes with them. We lose the route out. The men will drown before the water reaches the plains.”

The captain studied the stone.

Avani had learned long ago that men who wished to appear fearless often feared looking foolish more than death.

At last he said, “Set three barrels against the west seal. Two at the east gate. I will fire them myself.”

While the soldiers arranged the powder, Avani crouched beside the ancient wall and ran her fingers over the mortar. It was not limestone. It was packed clay, gravel, and powdered brick—made quickly, meant to hold only long enough for a king’s engineers to forget it existed.

She placed the fuses herself.

Then she cut one almost through.

Captain Tej Bahadur saw her hands.

“What did you do?”

“Made the burn equal,” she said.

He glanced toward the eastern gate, then back to her. The mountain trembled. Dust sifted from the ceiling.

“Bring the fire-lance,” he ordered.

One of the soldiers handed it to him.

Avani stepped away from the wall.

Captain Tej Bahadur leaned the lance against his shoulder and touched the match cord to its vent.

Vahni roared.

The sound came through the stone like the breaking of a sky.

The captain fired.

The first shot struck the west seal. The powder barrels exploded with a flat white flash. Stone tore outward. Behind it, black water surged against a second, older gate.

The captain cursed.

“Again!”

Before he could fire, Avani ran to the lever embedded in the wall beside the gate. It had rusted almost solid. She put both hands on it and pulled.

Nothing happened.

Behind her, Captain Tej Bahadur raised the fire-lance.

“Move.”

Avani seized a fallen iron bar, jammed it through the lever’s spokes, and heaved until the muscles in her back screamed.

The mechanism shrieked.

Far beyond the bridge, Vahni gave another roar. Steam flooded the heart chamber. The dragon had torn one chain free. Her dark body appeared through the mist, dragging bronze links behind her.

Captain Tej Bahadur fired at her.

The shot burst against her shoulder. Scales flew like black coins. Vahni recoiled, then swept one wing forward.

The wind of it knocked three soldiers into the abyss.

The captain staggered but kept his feet. He reached for another powder match.

Avani pulled again.

The western gate moved a finger’s width.

Then the weakened eastern supports gave way.

Stone cracked beneath the captain. He turned too late. The bridge dropped under him, breaking into slabs. He caught the edge with one hand, the fire-lance falling into the dark.

For a moment, he hung over the roar.

“Help me,” he said.

Avani lay flat on the shaking stone, one hand still gripping the lever bar. She could reach him if she let go.

Behind the western gate, water pressed like a living thing. The gate opened another inch.

Captain Tej Bahadur’s face had lost all its polish. He was simply a man now, sweating and terrified.

“Avani.”

She looked at his hand. A silver ring shone on his thumb.

Not his grandfather’s, perhaps. But the same shape.

The eastern breach split wider.

Vahni lunged across the gap. One claw closed around the captain’s wrist.

For an instant Avani thought she meant to save him.

Then the dragon’s claws opened.

Captain Tej Bahadur fell without a cry.

Vahni turned her gold eyes on Avani.

“Pull.”

Together they did.

Avani hauled the lever; Vahni hooked her claws into the gate’s iron ribs and dragged. The old mechanism fought them with centuries of rust. Water burst through the widening seam, first in a hard white jet, then in a roaring sheet.

It struck the western channel and vanished toward the plains.

The pressure against the eastern gate eased.

For one breath, everything became still.

Then the remaining powder at the eastern supports ignited.

Avani had cut the fuse, but not enough.

The blast threw her against the wall. Her left arm went numb. Stone rained down. She saw Vahni wrap one wing over the broken arch as the eastern gate ruptured.

Water struck the dragon full on.

It should have swept her away. Instead Vahni braced herself against the shattered stone, wings spread, body wedged across the breach. Steam rose from her scales in blinding clouds.

“Go,” she said.

Avani could not hear the word so much as feel it in the bones of her jaw.

She stumbled across the remaining bridge. Behind her, the dragon held.

The tunnels filled with mist and the thunder of water. Soldiers fled past her, some without weapons, some bleeding, none looking back. Avani followed the brass pipes toward the upper city, climbing through darkness until her lungs burned.

At the last stair, she found Pundit Somesh crouched beside the mosaic chamber, clutching his books.

“The chronicles,” he gasped. “They must be saved.”

Avani took one from his arms and tore out the painted page of the red dragon with the jeweled hand.

The pundit stared at her in horror.

She shoved her mother’s note into the book in its place.

“Save that,” she said.

They emerged from the cistern as rain began.

Not a drizzle. Not the dusty spitting rain of failed seasons. The sky had opened over Raktashila, and water poured through the ruined city in silver curtains. From cracks in the streets, old channels surged awake. Dry fountains filled. Stone troughs overflowed down the mountain in branching streams.

Across the valley, people were shouting.

The hill villages would have heard the warning in the ground, Avani prayed. They would have seen the water before it came. They would have run upward.

Above Kesar Kot, lightning split the clouds.

For one impossible moment, a vast blue-black shape rose through the rain.

Vahni’s wings beat once, twice. Water streamed from them. One wing hung ragged, and blood darkened the scales of her shoulder. She did not fly high. She circled the mountain, following the new river as it raced west through channels buried for generations.

Then she disappeared into the storm.

In Aftabpur, three weeks later, the palace pools overflowed.

The Maharaja stood on a balcony above the flooded gardens while officials shouted below. Water had reached the city by old stone canals no map in the river office recorded. It filled public wells first, then market tanks, then the dry basin beneath the eastern fort. Beyond the walls, the plain had turned bright green in strips and patches.

There were dead in the hill country. There were houses gone, terraces broken, cattle carried away. There were also villages whose wells had filled for the first time in years.

Harivardhan received Avani in the Hall of Mirrors.

Her arm was bound from wrist to shoulder. Pundit Somesh stood beside her, thinner than before. He had brought the old gate inscription, copied carefully on fresh paper, and the chronicle with Nirmala Khatri’s note sewn into its binding.

“The dragon escaped,” said the Maharaja.

“Yes.”

“You failed.”

“The western channels are open,” Avani said.

“They were not the object of your mission.”

“No.”

The Maharaja’s gaze moved to Pundit Somesh.

“And you support this account?”

The old scholar looked at the polished floor. For several seconds Avani thought he would deny everything.

Then he said, “The older inscriptions support Engineer Khatri’s findings.”

Harivardhan’s expression did not change.

“The people will call it a miracle,” he said.

“People call many things miracles,” Avani replied.

He looked at her then, and she understood that he had not forgiven her, would not forgive her, and might still find some quiet way to ruin her family.

But outside the hall, water rushed through the palace gardens, louder than the voices of kings.

That evening Avani went to the river office. Her brother, Imran, had been given his promised post despite everything. He stood ankle-deep in the archive room, trying to rescue ledgers from the flood.

“You are late,” he said when she entered.

“I was in the palace.”

“Did they give you gold?”

“No.”

“Land?”

“No.”

“A title?”

“No.”

He looked at her bandaged arm. “Then what did you bring me?”

Avani set her mother’s brass compass on the desk.

The needle had always pointed north. Now, after the mountain had shifted and the channels had opened, it spun slowly before settling west.

Imran watched it.

“Is it broken?” he asked.

Avani listened to the river knocking beneath the floorstones.

“Not anymore,” she said.
