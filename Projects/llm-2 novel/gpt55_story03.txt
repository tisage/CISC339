At five in the morning, when the first tray of poppy-seed biscuits slid into the oven with the neat obedience of soldiers, Zuzanna Łabędź believed the day could still be saved.

This was an optimistic belief, because the day had not yet gone wrong.

Her grandmother would have said that optimism before sunrise was just a way of inviting Saint Peter to hide your shoes. But Babcia Halina had also believed in stirring mushroom sauce clockwise, scolding dough as if it had moral failings, and keeping a bottle of plum vodka in the flour cupboard “for emergencies,” so Zuza did not always take her grandmother’s sayings literally.

She stood in the kitchen of Łabędź & Spoon, the catering company she ran from a converted former tailor’s shop in Kazimierz, and watched her assistant Paweł pipe horseradish cream onto rye toasts in small white rosettes.

“Not so high,” she said. “They have to fit in mouths, not win a hat competition.”

Paweł lowered the piping bag. He was nineteen, pale, and so terrified of doing anything wrong that he sometimes forgot to breathe. At the sink, Lidia chopped dill with the calm violence of a woman who had raised three sons and could reduce a bunch of herbs to green dust in nine seconds.

On the stainless-steel prep table lay the timeline, laminated, annotated, and weighted down with a jar of pickled cucumbers. Zuza had written it in four colors.

7:00 — Load van.  
7:45 — Depart Kraków.  
9:15 — Arrive Pałac Brzezina.  
10:00 — Begin kitchen setup.  
13:00 — Canapés.  
15:30 — Dinner.  
17:00 — Cake.  
18:00 — Hot bar.  
22:00 — Late pierogi service.  
24:00 — Collect dignity, equipment, leftover sanity.

Today’s wedding was the wedding: the one that would either elevate Łabędź & Spoon beyond birthday luncheons and embassy canapé trays, or send Zuza crawling back to the Radisson kitchen where Chef Marcin would greet her with the smile of a saint painted on a crocodile.

Three hundred guests. A renovated manor outside Niepołomice. The bride, Alicja Morawiecka, daughter of a developer with a voice like gravel being poured into a bathtub. The groom, Mateusz Wrona, whose mother had spoken to Zuza for forty minutes about the spiritual importance of a clear consommé.

The menu had been negotiated over three tastings, two family arguments, and one small crying episode involving an aunt who insisted that a wedding without cold pork jelly was a legal impossibility.

Now there were buckwheat blini with smoked trout, beetroot tartare with goat cheese, mushroom żurek in porcelain cups, roast duck with sour cherry sauce, vegetarian stuffed cabbage for the groom’s cousins from Wrocław, twelve trays of pierogi ruskie for midnight, and a five-tier poppy-seed and plum wedding cake, currently sleeping in the walk-in refrigerator like a white, sugared monument to Zuza’s ambition.

Everything was labeled. Everything was timed. Everything was under control.

Then the lights went out.

The ovens died first, with a little click of betrayal. The refrigerator’s hum softened to silence. The kitchen fell into a gray dawn stillness broken only by Lidia saying, “No.”

Zuza stood very straight.

Paweł whispered, “Maybe it’s romantic?”

“It is not romantic,” said Lidia. “It is electricity.”

Zuza checked the breakers. Nothing. She ran into the courtyard behind the shop, where a man from the bakery next door was standing in slippers and a coat, staring at the dark windows.

“Pan Mirek!” she called. “Do you have power?”

“I have old age and knees like wet cardboard,” he said. “Power, no.”

The entire block was out.

Zuza returned to the kitchen, where the timeline on the table seemed to have changed from a plan into a threat.

“How long until it comes back?” Paweł asked.

“In Kraków?” said Lidia. “Between five minutes and the Second Coming.”

Zuza looked at the half-baked biscuits in the cooling oven, at the trays of duck waiting to be finished, at the cake in the refrigerator whose buttercream was entering a negotiation with physics.

Then she clapped her hands.

“We move.”

“To where?” Paweł said.

“To anywhere with fire.”

Within twenty minutes, the tidy prep kitchen became an evacuation zone. Lidia commandeered the gas burners from the old soup kitchen at Saint Stanisław parish by calling the priest and saying, “Father, do you want to explain to three hundred wedding guests why God hates duck?” Pan Mirek opened his bakery’s wood-fired oven, which he claimed was “temperamental” but which turned out to be less temperamental than several living chefs Zuza had known. Zuza’s cousin Olek, who sold kiełbasa at the Sunday market, arrived with two portable grills, a bag of charcoal, and the expression of a man delighted to be needed before breakfast.

They finished the biscuits in the bakery, seared duck breasts behind the parish hall, and kept the cake cold in the cellar beneath a grocery shop whose owner stored cabbages there and asked only for “one little plate of the trout things later.”

At six fifty-five, so close to the timeline that Zuza dared to feel proud, they began loading the van.

The van was not there.

Zuza stood in the alley staring at the empty rectangle of oil-darkened cobblestone where the white refrigerated Mercedes should have been. For three seconds her mind rejected this sight as a printing error in reality.

“Paweł,” she said carefully. “Where is the van?”

Paweł turned around while holding a tray of beet tartare. “Here?”

“No.”

“Maybe around the corner?”

“No.”

“Maybe invisible?”

Lidia took the keys from the hook by the back door and jingled them. “It did not drive itself.”

Zuza called the rental company.

The man who answered sounded as if he had been born under a desk and had never seen daylight. “Yes, pani Łabędź. Our driver came to collect the van for maintenance at six thirty.”

“Maintenance?”

“Yes.”

“I rented it until tomorrow.”

“Yes, but there was a note.”

“What note?”

“A note that it needed maintenance.”

“Who wrote the note?”

“I am checking.”

There was a pause. In the alley, Olek leaned against his grill and ate one of the biscuits.

The man returned. “It says, ‘left wheel making sound.’”

“All wheels make sounds! They are wheels!”

“Not like this, apparently.”

Zuza pressed the phone so hard to her ear that her fingers hurt. “Bring it back.”

“It is already at Wieliczka.”

“Then bring me another.”

“We have a florist van. It smells a little of lilies.”

“Does it refrigerate?”

“No, but it has shelves.”

Zuza looked at three hundred smoked trout blini and a five-tier buttercream cake. “I cannot transport a wedding in a flower coffin.”

Silence.

Then Lidia said, “My brother.”

“No,” said Zuza.

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Yes, and you know it.”

Lidia’s brother, Wiesław, owned a vehicle that had begun life in the 1980s as a delivery truck, spent some time as a mobile honey stall, and currently served as a general-purpose village machine for carrying potatoes, furniture, and once, according to legend, a calf with opinions. It was orange, shaped like a loaf of bread, and bore on its side a peeling sign that read: MIÓD — NATURALNY, UCZCIWY, NIE MA TAKIEGO DRUGIEGO. Honey — natural, honest, there is no other like it.

“It smells of bees,” Zuza said.

“So do flowers,” Lidia said. “And you were considering flowers.”

By seven thirty, Wiesław’s orange truck backed into the alley in a cloud of diesel, with Wiesław at the wheel wearing a suit jacket over pajamas.

“Beautiful morning for a wedding,” he said, climbing down. “Who died?”

“My patience,” said Zuza.

They lined the interior with thermal blankets, dry ice, and stern prayers. The cake, carried by four people and watched by six, was loaded last. Its white tiers gleamed in the dim truck like a duchess kidnapped by peasants.

At eight ten, they left Kraków, forty minutes behind schedule but moving.

Zuza sat in the passenger seat of the honey truck with the timeline on her lap. Lidia and Paweł followed in Olek’s car with the flowers of horseradish cream, which seemed safer there than beside the grill equipment and Wiesław’s emergency bag of beekeeping supplies.

“We will still arrive before ten,” Zuza said.

Wiesław nodded. “Unless there is a procession.”

“Why would there be a procession?”

He shrugged. “In Poland? For God, for veterans, for mushrooms, for a new pavement. There is always a procession.”

There was a procession.

At Zabierzów Bocheński, the road narrowed between a church and a row of linden trees. Ahead, the entire village moved at the pace of solemn incense. Girls in white dresses scattered petals. Men carried banners. A brass band played something that might once have been a hymn but had been wounded in battle. Behind them came two hundred parishioners, three dogs, and an elderly woman pushing a bicycle with cabbages tied to the rack.

Wiesław braked. In the back, something slid.

Zuza’s head snapped around. “What was that?”

“Honey memory,” he said.

She got out and ran to the rear doors. The cake stood intact. A tray of rye toasts had shifted gently against a crate of pickles. She exhaled.

Then she noticed the bees.

Not many at first. Perhaps seven. Then twelve. Then a suggestion of more.

“Wiesław,” she said.

He appeared beside her. “Ah.”

“Why are there bees?”

“They must have remained from Tuesday.”

“What happened Tuesday?”

“Bees.”

One landed on the white buttercream of the wedding cake and began walking across it with purpose.

Zuza stared at it. The bee stared back, perhaps. It was difficult to tell with bees.

“Remove them,” she said.

“They do not respond to commands,” Wiesław said. “They are not soldiers.”

“You promised me this truck was empty.”

“It is empty of large animals.”

The procession continued. Petals fell. Trumpets blared. Inside the truck, bees discovered that smoked trout and plum buttercream were more interesting than whatever bees usually discussed.

Olek arrived from the car behind, looked into the truck, and said, “Ah, catering with pollination.”

“I will kill you,” Zuza told no one in particular.

“You should not kill bees,” Paweł said earnestly from behind her. “They are important.”

“So is my cake!”

A priest approached, swinging incense. “Is everything all right?”

“No,” Zuza said.

“We have bees,” Paweł explained.

The priest peered inside. “Saint Ambrose is patron of beekeepers.”

“Can he drive faster?” Zuza asked.

The elderly cabbage woman stopped. “Smoke,” she said.

“What?”

“You need smoke. For bees.”

“We have a smoker!” Wiesław cried, suddenly happy, and rummaged under the front seat.

Of course he had a smoker. He also had a veil, gloves, a hive tool, two jars of honey, and a cracked cassette of disco polo.

There, beside the procession, while the brass band wheezed through the final verse, Zuza stood in her black catering trousers and chef’s jacket directing Wiesław as he puffed smoke into the back of the truck. Bees lifted lazily from canapés and cake and drifted toward the linden trees. Several guests in the procession applauded, either because they thought it was part of the religious observance or because people like applauding unusual competence.

The cake survived with only three tiny footprints in the buttercream, which Zuza smoothed with a heated palette knife and a shaking hand.

They arrived at Pałac Brzezina at ten thirty-two.

The manor stood beyond iron gates, pale yellow, with ivy climbing one side and a stork nest balanced on a chimney. White chairs had been arranged in the garden. A tent spread over the lawn like a rich man’s handkerchief. Staff from the rental company were unloading glassware. The photographer was taking pictures of shoes hanging from a chandelier.

Zuza jumped from the truck and went looking for the kitchen entrance.

She found it locked.

She found the side entrance locked.

She found the rear entrance locked, chained, and blocked by three decorative barrels.

She found the venue manager, a narrow woman named Pani Renata, on the terrace speaking into a headset and holding a clipboard with the feverish dignity of someone whose clipboard was not helping.

“The kitchen is locked,” Zuza said.

“Yes,” said Pani Renata.

“I need it unlocked.”

“Yes.”

“Now.”

“Unfortunately, the key is with Pan Andrzej.”

“Who is Pan Andrzej?”

“Our maintenance man.”

“Where is he?”

“At another wedding.”

Zuza waited for this to transform into a joke.

It did not.

“Why does the maintenance man have the only key to the kitchen during a wedding?”

Pani Renata looked offended. “He is very responsible.”

“He is not here.”

“He was called away because at the other venue, the toilet is producing foam.”

Zuza closed her eyes.

Behind her, Wiesław lowered the truck’s liftgate with a squeal. Lidia emerged from Olek’s car holding a box of dill as if it were a weapon.

“We have a locked kitchen,” Zuza said.

Lidia nodded once. “Window?”

“That is illegal.”

“Only if we are bad at it.”

Paweł raised a finger. “There is a small window open above the sink.”

Everyone looked at him.

“How do you know?”

“I was checking if we could smell the kitchen.”

Zuza did not ask why. She went to see.

The window was high, narrow, and tilted inward. It was just large enough for a thin, frightened nineteen-year-old to pass through if his employer was desperate and his mother never found out.

“Paweł,” Zuza said gently.

“No.”

“You are the future of Polish gastronomy.”

“No.”

“You are also the only one who fits.”

He looked at the window. “If I die, please tell my mother I never smoked behind the tram stop.”

“You smoked behind the tram stop?”

“No.”

With Lidia boosting his feet and Olek providing completely useless encouragement, Paweł wriggled through the window and disappeared into the kitchen with a yelp, a crash, and the unmistakable sound of pots meeting tile.

A moment later the door opened. Paweł stood there wearing a colander around one arm.

“I am alive,” he announced.

They had lost another twenty minutes, but now they had a kitchen.

Or rather, they had a room that had once been a kitchen before someone decided that weddings looked better when essential work happened elsewhere. There were two ovens, one of which was older than democracy. The refrigerator smelled faintly of lemon and despair. There was a decorative copper pot too large to move and too small to be useful. The sinks worked only when the tap was held at a particular angle, like coaxing a confession.

“No matter,” Zuza said. “We have done more with less.”

“When?” Paweł asked.

“Never. Begin.”

Canapés went out at one fifteen, only fifteen minutes late. Guests circulated in the garden, sipping prosecco and praising the beet tartare. The bride had not yet emerged. The groom, Mateusz, a tall man with nervous ears, came into the kitchen twice to ask if anyone had seen his grandfather and once to ask if the cake was “emotionally stable.”

“It is more stable than I am,” Zuza told him.

At two o’clock, as servers carried trays beneath the tent, Zuza allowed herself half a cup of coffee, cold.

That was when the peacock arrived.

It came from nowhere, stepping onto the lawn with imperial stupidity, blue neck shining, tail dragging like a ceremonial mistake. For a moment everyone admired it. Phones appeared. The photographer crouched. Someone said, “How elegant.”

Then the peacock screamed like a murdered hinge and attacked a platter of smoked trout blini.

“Is that part of the décor?” Paweł asked.

“No,” said Pani Renata, appearing beside Zuza. “That is Zygmunt.”

“You know this bird?”

“He belongs to the neighboring property. He is not allowed here.”

“Does he know?”

Zygmunt leapt onto the canapé station, scattering dill and trout. A bridesmaid shrieked. The peacock spread his tail in triumph and knocked over two glasses of elderflower lemonade.

Zuza ran.

She had never been trained in peacock removal. The Radisson had taught her knife skills, costing, pastry lamination, and how to tell a French tourist that no, ketchup was not a traditional accompaniment to duck confit. It had not covered large ornamental birds with criminal personalities.

“Shoo!” she shouted.

Zygmunt pecked a blini.

Lidia appeared with a tablecloth. “We surround.”

“With what army?”

“Paweł!”

Paweł, who had just come out holding a tray of mushroom cups, froze. “I respect animals.”

“Respect him from the left!”

The three of them advanced with tablecloths spread like nets. Zygmunt eyed them, screamed again, and fled across the lawn, past the string quartet, between two uncles, and directly toward the bride.

Alicja Morawiecka had chosen that exact moment to make her entrance down the terrace steps in lace and satin, her hair crowned with tiny pearls. She looked radiant, composed, and entirely unprepared to meet a peacock at speed.

Zuza lunged.

She missed the peacock but caught the end of Alicja’s veil. The bride stopped; the veil did not. For one terrible second, Zuza was attached to the bride by three meters of tulle and guilt.

The peacock veered. The bride gasped. Zuza, trying to release the veil, stepped backward into a decorative fountain.

Cold water closed over her shoes.

The guests went silent.

Zygmunt escaped through a hedge with two blini and the dignity of a conquering prince.

Zuza stood calf-deep in the fountain, holding the veil in both hands.

Alicja turned slowly.

The bride had the sort of face that could have gone either way: murder or laughter. Her eyes moved from Zuza’s wet trousers to the veil, then to the hedge where the peacock had vanished.

Then she laughed.

It began as a snort, which she tried to suppress, which made it worse. Her shoulders shook. One of the bridesmaids started laughing too. Mateusz, pale with horror at first, saw his bride laugh and laughed in relief so strongly he had to sit on a chair.

The guests applauded, because again people like applauding unusual competence, and also because they were drunk enough on prosecco to believe they had witnessed entertainment.

Zuza stepped out of the fountain, bowed with what dignity she could assemble, and returned to the kitchen leaving wet footprints behind her.

“Your trousers,” Paweł said.

“If you mention my trousers, I will serve you as pâté.”

Dinner had to be perfect. Dinner would restore order. Dinner was where Zuza lived.

The first course, mushroom żurek in porcelain cups, went smoothly. The second course, roast duck with sour cherry sauce, left the kitchen on time and glistening. Servers moved in synchronized lines. Guests murmured approval. Zuza watched plates return scraped nearly clean, and the tight knot between her ribs loosened.

Then Pan Morawiecki, the bride’s father, stormed in holding a plate.

“This duck is excellent,” he said angrily.

Zuza blinked. “Thank you?”

“But where is the wild boar?”

“There is no wild boar on the menu.”

“There was to be wild boar.”

“There was never wild boar.”

“My brother remembers wild boar.”

“Your brother arrived at the tasting with his own flask and asked if quinoa was a fish.”

Pan Morawiecki frowned, perhaps because this was true. “Guests are asking.”

“For wild boar?”

“For meat with authority.”

Before Zuza could answer, Pani Wrona, the groom’s mother, entered from the other side, eyes bright with panic.

“Please,” she said. “Tell me the broth is not made with chicken.”

“It is mushroom żurek.”

“My sister says she tasted chicken.”

“Your sister is eating duck.”

“She has sensitivities.”

“To duck?”

“To being ignored.”

Pan Morawiecki pointed at Pani Wrona. “Your side complained about the venison too.”

“There is no venison,” said Zuza.

“That is why we complained,” he said.

In the doorway, Lidia caught Zuza’s eye and made a face that meant: Families. Worse than shellfish.

Zuza inhaled.

“Pan Morawiecki, Pani Wrona, I am happy to discuss additional game meats after service. At this moment, I am feeding your guests the menu you approved and signed in triplicate.”

“My cousin from Tarnów says weddings require abundance,” Pan Morawiecki said.

“Your cousin from Tarnów has eaten seven duck legs,” said Paweł, passing with a stack of plates, then immediately looked as if he wished to climb back through the window and never return.

Pan Morawiecki stared at him.

Zuza stepped in front of Paweł. “What my assistant means is that abundance is being achieved.”

A crash came from the tent.

Not a small crash. Not a dropped fork or tipsy aunt. A structural crash.

Zuza ran.

The dessert table had collapsed.

Or rather, one leg of it had sunk into the lawn where the earlier fountain water, combined with afternoon rain no one had ordered, had softened the ground. The table tilted at a tragic angle. Miniature serniki slid toward disaster. Plum tartlets piled against bowls of whipped cream. Macarons rolled like pastel coins into the grass.

At the far end of the table, the wedding cake leaned.

People screamed in six different registers.

Zuza moved faster than thought. She grabbed the cake stand. Lidia grabbed her waist. Paweł grabbed Lidia. Olek, appearing from nowhere, shoved a crate under the failing table leg. The cake wobbled, sighed, and settled.

For a moment, it was saved.

Then one of the storks on the chimney above the manor took flight.

It dropped a twig.

The twig landed on the tent canopy, rolled down, and struck the chandelier hanging over the dessert table. The chandelier swung. Its lowest crystal pendants kissed the top tier of the cake with a delicate, fatal tap.

The top tier slid.

Zuza caught it against her chest.

White buttercream smeared across her chef’s jacket. Plum filling oozed down her sleeve. In her arms, the top tier of the wedding cake rested like an injured animal.

Every guest in the tent was watching.

Alicja, still in her gown, approached carefully. “Is that… our cake?”

“Part of it,” Zuza said.

“Is it supposed to be there?”

“No.”

Mateusz came beside his bride. “Can we still cut it?”

Zuza looked at the remaining four tiers, slightly dented, still upright. She looked at the top tier in her arms. She looked at the grass-stained macarons, the collapsed serniki, the guests holding phones, the fathers arguing about imaginary boar, the groom’s mother fanning herself with the seating chart, Paweł silently mouthing what might have been a prayer.

Then she began to laugh.

It was not a graceful laugh. It came out cracked and helpless, and at first she tried to stop it, because professionals did not laugh while wearing a cake. But the day had climbed past disaster into some bright region beyond shame. Bees. Procession. Locked kitchen. Peacock. Fountain. Wild boar that had never existed. Stork twig. Cake against her heart.

Alicja laughed too, again. Mateusz covered his mouth. Then Paweł. Then Lidia, who almost never laughed during paid hours, bent double.

“What do we do?” Alicja asked.

Zuza wiped her eyes with the only clean part of her sleeve and said, “We change the plan.”

The manor had an old bread oven in the courtyard, decorative according to Pani Renata, which to Zuza meant neglected but possibly alive. Olek and Wiesław inspected it and declared it “mostly not dangerous.” Lidia found crates of apples in a storage room. Pan Mirek’s unfinished biscuits, saved from the morning power outage, became crumble. The broken sernik was scooped into glasses with sour cherry sauce and called layered cheesecake. Grass-free macarons were stacked around bowls of whipped cream. The top tier of cake, after Zuza sacrificed the parts that had touched her jacket, became cubes on skewers with plums and honey.

“From your truck?” she asked Wiesław.

He held up a jar. “Natural, honest, there is no other like it.”

“Fine. If anyone finds a bee, it is garnish.”

They moved dessert outside. Guests followed, curious and cheerful now that perfection had been officially killed. The string quartet gave up on Schubert and played folk tunes. Someone brought out an accordion. Alicja, barefoot beneath her gown, helped arrange cake skewers on a platter while Mateusz carried firewood.

“You do not have to do that,” Zuza told them.

“It’s my wedding,” Alicja said. “I can carry my own disaster.”

The fathers stopped arguing when Olek put them both in charge of turning apples over the fire. Pan Morawiecki proved surprisingly delicate with tongs. Pani Wrona discovered that she loved the hot plum sauce and told three people it was exactly what she had suggested at the tasting, which was not true but no longer mattered.

By dusk, the courtyard smelled of smoke, butter, apples, sour cherries, coffee, and wet grass. Guests stood in clusters, plates in hand, eating with the concentration of people who had stopped waiting for things to happen correctly and begun enjoying them as they happened. Children chased one another around the fountain, which Zuza avoided on principle. Zygmunt the peacock watched from beyond the hedge, denied reentry by Wiesław, who had taken to his role as bird security with unsettling passion.

At nine, the late pierogi service began two hours early because everyone was hungry again.

This, at least, was simple.

There were few problems in life that did not improve when one placed hot pierogi in front of them. Paweł boiled, Lidia fried onions, Zuza browned butter until it smelled nutty and deep. Plates went out in waves. Ruskie, mushroom, cabbage, cheese with mint for the old aunt who claimed mint kept the hips young. Guests lined up with the reverence usually reserved for relics.

Pan Morawiecki came through twice. The second time he leaned toward Zuza and said, “You know, wild boar would have been too heavy.”

“Indeed,” said Zuza.

He nodded as if they had agreed all along.

At ten thirty, Alicja and Mateusz finally cut what remained of the cake. The four tiers stood patched with flowers, slightly crooked, and radiant in candlelight. The photographer found an angle that made it look intentional. Alicja fed Mateusz a bite; he fed her one; then she dabbed buttercream on his nose, and he kissed her before she could apologize.

The guests cheered. The storks clattered from the chimney. No twig fell.

Near midnight, after the last pierogi had vanished and the dance floor had become a rotating machine of cousins, Zuza sat on the back step of the kitchen with her shoes off. Her feet were swollen. Her trousers were stiff from fountain water. Her chef’s jacket was ruined beyond any detergent known to Europe. There was buttercream in her hair.

Paweł sat beside her holding a cup of tea. “I think I lost three years of life.”

“You are nineteen. You can spare them.”

Lidia emerged and lowered herself onto the step with a groan. “The groom’s grandmother wants our card.”

“For what?” Zuza asked.

“Her eightieth birthday. She says she wants the same menu, but with less peacock.”

Zuza laughed, too tired to stop herself.

Pani Renata came around the corner then, headset crooked, clipboard gone. Without it, she looked younger and much less narrow.

“The families are happy,” she said.

“That seems medically unlikely,” Zuza replied.

“They are. The bride’s father asked if you could cater their company Christmas party.”

“No.”

Lidia elbowed her.

Zuza sighed. “Maybe.”

Pani Renata handed her an envelope. “Payment. Full amount. Plus something extra from the bride and groom.”

Zuza took it. “For damages?”

“For memories, I think.”

After she left, Zuza opened the envelope. Inside was the final payment, a generous tip, and a note on manor stationery.

Pani Zuzanna,

We wanted a perfect wedding. Thank you for giving us a better one.

Alicja & Mateusz

P.S. Please send recipe for the broken cheesecake.

Zuza read it twice, then folded it carefully and put it in her pocket.

From the hedge came a scream like a rusty gate being murdered.

Paweł flinched. “Peacock.”

Wiesław shouted from the darkness, “Not today, Zygmunt!”

Lidia sipped her tea. “Next time, we charge extra for wildlife.”

“Next time,” Zuza said, leaning back against the warm kitchen wall, “we rent two vans.”

“And bring our own key.”

“And no bees.”

Wiesław appeared carrying a stolen blini retrieved from the hedge. “Bees are never the problem,” he said.

Everyone looked at him.

He considered this. “Fine. Bees are sometimes the problem.”

The band inside began playing “Sto lat,” though whose hundred years they were celebrating was unclear. Guests sang too loudly, glasses clinked, and someone laughed with a force that carried over the lawn and into the night fields beyond the manor.

Zuza closed her eyes for one second.

In the morning there would be invoices, laundry, apologies to make or refuse, and a rental company employee who would learn several new uses for the word maintenance. There would be trays to count, burns to bandage, and the suspicious task of discovering whether honey could be cleaned from a cake stand hinge.

But for now, the guests were fed, the bride was dancing, the groom was alive, and somewhere in the courtyard two rival families were eating apples from the same dented pan.

Zuza lifted her tea in the direction of the music.

“To dignity,” Paweł said.

She looked down at her stained jacket, bare feet, and plum-colored sleeve.

“No,” she said. “To surviving without it.”

Lidia touched her cup to Zuza’s. Wiesław did the same with a jar of honey. Paweł hesitated, then joined them.

Beyond the hedge, Zygmunt screamed once more into the dark, unanswered and unfed.
