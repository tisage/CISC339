Theodoros heard the rooster before dawn. It belonged to the neighbor two houses down, a bird with no sense of territory or time, and it crowed at all hours as if it had something to prove. The sound came through the kitchen wall, faint and stupid, while he sat with a glass of cold water and watched the baseboard.

The baseboard had not changed overnight. It was still warped, still dark along the bottom edge where the water had seeped in for two weeks. He had learned to measure the damage by how far his mother moved her photograph albums. First the shelf by the window, then the top of the wardrobe, then the bedroom beside the icon of Saint Marina. Each move was a declaration. She did not speak of it. She just carried the albums and set them down with the finality of a woman who had lived through occupations and droughts and knew a flood when she saw one.

The water had come from the pit outside. The pit was supposed to be a foundation for the new extension, a small bright room where his mother could sit in the afternoon sun and look at her lemon tree. Theodoros had paid a man named Cristos Kolios six thousand euros to dig it and pour the concrete. Cristos had come recommended by a cousin, which in Larnaca meant the recommendation was a social contract, not a credential. He had arrived in a clean white shirt with a clipboard and three men who spoke Bulgarian, or perhaps Romanian—Theodoros never learned which. They had dug the hole in four days. Then the rains came. Then the pump ran out of fuel. Then Cristos stopped answering his phone, except once, when he sent a text: *We will fix tomorrow, don’t worry.*

That was eleven days ago.

Theodoros went to work every morning at the customs office, stamping forms for imported air-conditioning units and construction materials. He came home every evening to the smell of damp plaster and his mother’s silence. The hole sat behind the house like an unfinished sentence.

He did not consider himself a passive man. He was forty-three, solidly built, with a mustache he trimmed every Sunday and a balding crown he accepted as the price of avoiding his father’s vanity. He had been married once, to a woman from Nicosia who now lived in Athens and sent him a Christmas card every three years. He had no children. He owned the house outright, left to him by his father, and he had saved for the extension for nine years. The money had not come easily. The house was all he had.

The morning after he decided to stop waiting, he called Cristos again. The phone rang eight times, then a woman’s voice said, “The number you are calling is unavailable.” He hung up and opened the kitchen cabinet where he kept the paperwork. The contract was handwritten, two pages, signed with a flourish Theodoros now recognized as theatrical. It promised a foundation, a shell, a roof, a tiled floor, and two windows. It promised completion in eight weeks. It promised nothing about water.

He drove to the house of the cousin who had recommended Cristos, a man named Iordanis Petrides, but the cousin was away, his neighbor said, gone to Limassol for his daughter’s surgery and not expected back. Theodoros stood on the doorstep with his hands in his pockets and felt the whole city contracting around him, everyone suddenly absent, as if a signal had gone out.

So he did what he had not done in years: he went to the café.

The café was called To Perivoli, the orchard, though no trees grew nearby and the only vegetation was the potted basil that the owner, a widower named Kyriakos, watered with the dregs of the Turkish coffee. It was late morning. Old men from the neighborhood sat at the metal tables, playing backgammon and arguing about the water shortages, the priests, the football. Theodoros took a seat at the edge and ordered a bitter coffee and a glass of water. He drank the water slowly, letting the ice rest against his teeth.

“You have a face,” Kyriakos said, wiping a table, “like a man whose roof fell in.”

“The roof is fine,” Theodoros said. “The foundation is the problem.”

He did not explain. He had learned long ago that words in a café were currency: spend them and they would be spent again. But by the second afternoon, the story was already out. A man at the next table leaned over and said, “You are the one with the hole? The one who hired Kolios?”

Theodoros set down his glass. “You know him.”

“Everyone knows him.” The man had a thin face and wore a gray cap pulled low. “He does the job halfway, then the rains come, then he says the ground was bad and that God made the weather, not him. Then he disappears.” He made a spitting gesture into the street. “My brother-in-law hired him for a tile roof in Kiti. The tiles slid off in March. He said the birds loosened them.”

Theodoros felt a kind of cold relief, the confirmation that he was not uniquely foolish. “Where does he go when he disappears?”

The man in the cap shrugged. “They say he has a place in Pyla. Or he stays with his mother in Dromolaxia. But if you want to find him, go on Thursday to the coffee shop by the salt lake, the one with the green windows. He plays backgammon there with the man who sells septic tanks.”

“What time?”

“After four. He will be there. He always has a story.”

On Thursday, Theodoros left the office at three-thirty, telling his supervisor he had a pain in his back. The drive to the salt lake took fifteen minutes. In the summer the lake was a pale crust of salt and pink algae, the flamingos gone north, the air thick with iodine. He found the coffee shop with the green windows and parked across the street. Through the glass he saw four men at a table, slapping tiles and moving their pieces. One of them was Cristos Kolios: broad, white-haired, wearing a polo shirt the color of wet sand, his sleeves stretched tight around his upper arms. He was laughing.

Theodoros got out of the car. He did not cross at the crosswalk. He walked directly, through the thin shade of the carob trees, and pushed open the door. The bell rang, a small copper note. Cristos looked up. The laugh stopped, but the smile remained, caught in place like a wave frozen before it breaks.

“Theo,” Cristos said. “Sit. Have a coffee.”

“I don’t want a coffee.”

“A lemon juice, then. It’s hot.”

The other men looked down at the board, suddenly absorbed. One of them, a fat man in a canvas hat, moved a piece and said, “Your move, Nikos.”

Theodoros stood at the table, looking down at Cristos. He could feel his own pulse in his forearms, the skin of his face tight. He had thought he would shout. He had imagined it in the car, the words building like steam. But now the man was before him, and the only thing that came was a tired, heavy sentence:

“The hole is full of water, Cristos. It’s killing the foundation. My mother’s moved her albums twice.”

Cristos waved a hand, as if shooing a fly. “These things happen. The water table in this area is high. No one can control the water table. We hit a vein, maybe. An underground spring. That’s not a mistake, that’s geology.”

“An underground spring,” Theodoros repeated. “We are four hundred meters from the sea and two meters above the salt lake. There’s no spring. There’s a hole you dug too deep and left open in the rainy season.”

“Now you are an engineer?” Cristos looked around at the other men, performing. “He works in customs. He stamps papers. But now he is an engineer.”

The fat man laughed, a short, nervous bark. The others did not.

Theodoros pulled a copy of the contract from his jacket pocket, folded once, creased down the middle. He opened it and held it in front of Cristos’s face. “You signed this. You promised a foundation. There is no foundation. There’s a pond. I gave you six thousand euros.”

“And I bought materials. I paid the workers. The digger was four hundred a day.”

“The digger was here for three days. The workers were here for four. The materials are in a pile in my yard, getting rained on. The wood is warping. The cement bags are solid now.” Theodoros’s voice had not risen, but it had gained a hard edge, like a file. “Give me back the money.”

Cristos leaned back in his chair. He picked up his backgammon dice and shook them idly in his hand, a habit, as if the conversation were a turn he was waiting to take. “I cannot give you back money that is spent. That’s not how business works. But I can fix the hole. I told you. I will come next week with a pump and a proper crew, no more Bulgarians, and we will pump it dry and pour the foundation. You will see. It will be a beautiful foundation. You can build a church on it.”

“When next week?”

“Monday. Or Tuesday, at the latest.”

“You have said Monday before.”

“This time is different. I have a pump from a man in Aradippou. A diesel pump, big as a donkey.” He grinned, showing a gold tooth far back in his mouth. “Trust me.”

Theodoros looked at him for a long moment. The café was very still. Outside, the salt lake shimmered. A dog crossed the road and lay down in the shade of a car.

“I don’t trust you,” Theodoros said.

Cristos set the dice down on the table. Six and one. “Then take me to court,” he said, smiling still. “See how long that takes. Three years, maybe four. And in the meantime, the water will rise and your house will sink. Or you can let me do what I do best.” He spread his hands. “Fix. Build. Finish.”

For a moment, Theodoros considered taking the wooden backgammon board and breaking it over the man’s head. He could feel the decision in his hands, the animal willingness to choose violence. Then he thought of his mother, waiting in the kitchen with her dry silence. A noise complaint, a police report, a lawyer—none of it would dry the floor. He folded the contract slowly and put it back in his pocket.

“Monday,” he said. “If you are not at my house on Monday morning with a pump, I will find you again. And I will not be asking for a refund.”

He turned and walked out. The bell rang behind him. He did not look back.

On Monday, Cristos did not come.

Theodoros took the day off work. He sat in the kitchen from seven in the morning until noon, watching the drive. His mother brought him a piece of cheese pie and a small dish of olives, then retreated to the front room. At one, he called. The phone rang and rang. At one-thirty, he called from his mother’s mobile, a different number. On the fourth ring, Cristos answered.

“Kolios.”

“It’s Theodoros Ioannou. You were supposed to be here at eight.”

“Ah, Theo.” The voice was thick, oily, as if the man had just eaten. “I had a delay. The truck with the pump broke down on the highway. I am arranging another one.”

“Where are you?”

“In Larnaca. Near the port. I can come tomorr—"

“You will come today,” Theodoros said.

“Today is difficult. The pump is the problem. This is not washing a car, it’s heavy equipment.”

“Then I will come to you. Tell me where you are.”

There was a pause, long enough that Theodoros could hear the man breathing, the faint rattle of a television in the background. “I will come tomorrow morning. I promise.”

The line went dead.

Theodoros stood in the kitchen with the phone in his hand. The rooster crowed, though it was afternoon. His mother appeared in the doorway, wiping her hands on a cloth. She was a small woman, seventy-eight, with hair gone white and knuckles permanently swollen from a lifetime of sewing and olive picking. She looked at him with an expression he knew well: not anger, not fear, but a kind of practical grief.

“He’s not coming,” she said.

“I know.”

“What will you do?”

He did not answer, because he did not know. Then he said, “I’ll find someone else. A real builder. And I’ll make Cristos pay for it, somehow. The court, maybe. A complaint.”

His mother made a small sound, half sigh, half laugh. “The courts here are deep water too.” She turned away, then paused. “Go see Sofronia,” she said.

“The woman on Nikodimou Street?”

“She has a son who works with concrete. Or a nephew. I don’t remember. But she knows people. You can’t sit in the kitchen and hate a man until the water rises.”

He went that evening, after the heat broke. Sofronia lived in a two-story house with a broken bell, so he knocked. A young woman opened, barefoot, with long black hair and a tattoo of a salamander on her ankle. Behind her the house smelled of ginger and kerosene.

“I’m looking for Sofronia,” he said. “About her son? Or nephew. A concrete worker.”

The young woman leaned against the doorframe. “Sofronia is my aunt. She’s in the hospital, since Sunday. Heart.” She touched her chest. “Her son is in Thessaloniki. But I’m here.” She raised an eyebrow. “What do you need, concrete?”

He almost left. She looked too young, too sharp-edged, not at all like a person who could stop water from coming into a house. But something—desperation, perhaps, or the memory of Cristos’s white-shirted confidence—made him stay. “A foundation,” he said. “A hole, already dug, full of water. The builder took my money and disappeared.”

The woman’s eyebrow went higher. “Which builder?”

“Cristos Kolios.”

She made a sound, a soft exhalation through her teeth, and then she laughed. It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of recognition. “Of course,” she said. “Cristos. The man has dug more holes than the war.” She stepped back from the door. “Come inside. Let’s talk. I’m Eleni. Eleni Stavrou. And before you ask, yes, I know concrete. I know drainage. I know how to fix a hole that Cristos dug. I’ve fixed two already, one in Pervolia, one near the airport.”

Theodoros followed her into the house. The hallway was lined with framed photographs, faces going gray under the glass. In the kitchen, she gestured to a chair and put a kettle on. He noticed the tools by the back door: a spirit level, a trowel, work boots with cement dust in the creases.

“You’re a builder?” he asked.

“I’m a civil engineer,” she said. “I worked in Athens for eight years, mostly on shore protection and drainage. Then I came back to take care of my father, who died. Now I do small jobs, renovations, repairs.” She sat across from him, her eyes steady. “Tell me about the hole.”

He told her. She listened without interrupting, her hands wrapped around a glass of cold water. When he finished, she nodded slowly.

“Six thousand,” she said. “Cristos is a thief, but he’s not stupid. He knew the water table there. He knew the site drains toward the house. He put the hole in the wrong place, wrong depth, wrong slope, and then he walked away with half your money and the job.” She stood and took a notebook from a drawer, wrote something. “I can come look at it tomorrow morning. If I think it’s fixable, I’ll give you a price. If it’s not fixable, I’ll tell you, and I won’t take your money.”

Something in him eased, a taut rope loosening. “Tomorrow is good.”

She came at eight, riding a blue scooter that leaked a small plume of white smoke. She wore work trousers and a tank top and carried a bag of instruments he could not name. She walked around the hole for ten minutes, saying nothing. Then she went into the house, knelt by the baseboard, pulled back the warped wood, and ran a thin metal probe into the gap between the foundation and the floor. She sniffed the plaster, touched the damp with her finger, and said, “Salt.”

He stood behind her, too wired to sit. “Salt?”

“Seawater intrusion. The water isn’t just rain. The groundwater here is brackish, and it’s been rising. The salt will break down the mortar and corrode the rebar over time. That’s why the wall is dark.” She stood, wiping her hands on a cloth. “You need to drain the hole properly and move the water away from the house. A French drain along the foundation, new gravel sump, a pump that actually works, and then a proper slab on top of compacted fill. And you need to seal the outside of the foundation wall to stop the moisture coming through the old stone.” She looked at him. “It’s a big job. Maybe ten, twelve thousand euros.”

He felt the number drop through him like a stone. “I have four thousand left. Maybe five.”

She put the cloth away. “Then we start with the water. We drain the hole, fix the drainage, dry out the house. The slab can wait. The septic of a house is not a luxury, but a wet foundation is an emergency.” She looked around the kitchen, at his mother’s photograph albums stacked now on the floor beside the table. “You have somewhere else to live while the work happens?”

“No.”

“Then the work happens around you. It will be dusty and loud. Your mother will be unhappy. But the water will stop.”

Theodoros thought about it. Five thousand was everything he had left, the bottom of the savings account, the money he had been saving for the roof, for the old car, for the uncertain future. But the water had already taken six. The house was the future. He said, “Do it.”

Eleni hired a pump from a man in Livadia, a foul-mouthed but efficient fellow who arrived with a flatbed truck and a diesel pump that looked as if it had served in two wars. They spent the first day draining the hole, the brown water gushing through a pipe laid across the yard and into the street drain, which the municipality had not cleaned in years and which flooded briefly, bringing a neighbor out to wave his arms. Theodoros waved back. The water level dropped, then dropped again, and by evening the hole was a pit of clotted mud and exposed rubble. The smell was terrible, like a marsh turned upside down.

His mother stayed inside with the windows closed.

The next day, Eleni and a young man named Nikos, whom she paid in cash from the budget, dug a trench along the outside wall of the house. Theodoros worked beside them. His back remembered manual labor from his youth, but the memory was far away, and by noon he was moving in a haze of sweat and salt. The trench was a meter deep, then a meter and a half. They laid perforated pipe along the base of the foundation, wrapped it in a geotextile sock, and covered it with gravel. At the far corner of the house, they dug a sump, a deep square hole lined with a perforated barrel, and set the pump hose into it. The system was simple, Eleni explained: water would collect in the drain, flow to the sump, and the pump would send it away from the house, down a gradient they created by regrading the yard.

For three days, the work held. Then the rain came again, a heavy October downpour that lasted eighteen hours. Theodoros woke at three in the morning to the pump alarm, a shrill electric shriek. He ran outside in his bare feet and found Eleni already there, a headlamp strapped over her wet hair, kneeling beside the sump. The power had failed, and the pump was dead. The water was rising.

“The breaker tripped,” she shouted over the rain. “The house is on the old wire. Too much draw.”

They stood in the downpour, water streaming into the sump, the hole filling again, and Theodoros felt something crack in his chest, a despair too vast for words. But Eleni did not stop. She reached into the sump, into the dark water, and checked the float. Then she looked at him. “I need a generator. Or we need to run a new line from the street box. Tonight, the generator. Do you know who has one?”

He didn’t. Then he remembered Sofronia’s house, the same street, a generator in the garden shed from the old days of the power cuts. He ran, through the rain, down the block and around the corner, banging on doors until the young woman from before—the one with the salamander tattoo—opened in a robe. She listened, said nothing, and went out back. Ten minutes later he was pushing a rusted red generator on a cart through the flooded street, and Elena helped him wire it. The pump groaned, sputtered, and came back to life. Water flowed away from the house, out through the pipe, into the storm drain.

At dawn, the rain stopped. The house was dry. The hole was full, but the water was being moved, not trapped. Eleni sat on the back step, her hands black with mud, her face gray. She looked at him and said, “You need to rewire the pump output. Or this will happen every time it rains.” She stood, slowly. “We do it tomorrow. I’ll bring an electrician I trust. It will cost another six hundred.”

He stood in the muddy yard, watching the pump trickle its clear stream away from the foundation. The rooster crowed. Somewhere in the house, his mother was making coffee, the smell drifting through the open kitchen window. He thought of the money, the dwindling number in the bank. Then he thought of the water, the salt, the boards of the kitchen floor. He nodded. “Do it.”

The electrician came, a thin, precise man named Varnavas, who called Eleni “the engineer” with no irony. They ran a new dedicated line from the breaker panel to the pump, installed a ground-fault interrupter, and tied the generator inlet into a switch so that the pump could run from either the mains or the portable unit. The work took two days. On the third day, they poured the concrete sump collar and set the pump properly, a submersible unit with a float switch and an alarm connected to an app on Eleni’s phone. She showed him: if the water rose while he was at work, the pump would text him. He almost laughed. The hole in the ground now texted.

But the hole itself remained. They had drained the water away from the house, but the excavation that Cristos had dug still sat open, a clay crater collapsing at the edges. Eleni said it would need to be filled and graded, regardless of whether he ever built the extension. Leaving it open would drain water into the ground under the house, soaking the earth, causing the foundation to shift. She gave him a choice: for the money he had left, she could fill it properly, compact it, and make the yard stable, or she could leave it, and they would be back in the same place by spring. He said to fill it.

They spent a week bringing in fill: soil, crushed stone, gravel, sand. Eleni drove the excavator herself, a rented orange Kubota that looked like a child’s toy until it moved. Nikos shoveled, Theodoros hauled buckets, and his mother, unexpectedly, made lunch every day, big spreads of beans and fried halloumi and tomato and cucumber, setting them out on the table in the yard under the lemon tree. Even Eleni, who usually ate standing, sat down.

On the last day of the fill work, a car pulled up outside the house. A white Mercedes, old but polished. Theodoros looked up from the wheelbarrow and saw Cristos Kolios step out, as if from a dream, with his hands in his pockets and his white shirt bright against the gray sky. He walked toward the fence, looked at the hole—now nearly level with the ground, smooth and graded—and said, “You filled it?”

Theodoros set down the wheelbarrow. His hands were raw. The shovel was in his right hand, and he did not put it down. “What do you want?”

Cristos smiled. That same smile. “I came to fix the foundation. You couldn’t wait a few weeks? You filled in a beautiful shaft. What a waste.” He shook his head, as if genuinely moved. “I heard from Sofronia’s niece that you hired an engineer. You spent more money. You see, that’s how they get you. They come with their instruments and their words. They don’t know the ground. I know this ground. My father and his father–"

“Your father,” Theodoros said, “was a plasterer from Athienou. He never built a foundation in his life. You had two months and six thousand euros, and you left me with a pond under my windows. You’re a thief, Cristos, and everyone at the green-window café knows it. The only ones who don’t are the ones who owe you money.”

Cristos’s smile did not move, but something behind it tightened. “Thief is a strong word. I can go to the police, you know. Defamation.”

Eleni had come around the side of the house, still holding the level. She did not say anything. She just stood there, the level across her shoulder, her boots crusted with clay.

“Go to the police,” Theodoros said. “And I’ll show them the contract, the text messages, the photographs of the hole, the water in the kitchen, the ruined wood. I’ll show them the receipt for the diesel pump and the new electrical line and the fill. We can all sit in a room and explain what happened. But you won’t, because you know what will happen next. You’ll have to give back the money, or go to court, and then every man you ever cheated will come out of the woodwork. Isn’t that right, Eleni?”

Eleni did not answer. She looked at Cristos, and he looked at her, and something passed between them, a recognition, perhaps, of two species occupying the same trade. Then she said, “The fill is compacted. The drainage is working. The house is dry. Whatever you think you’re owed, Cristos, it’s less than what it cost him to fix your work.” She turned and walked back toward the pump.

Cristos stood at the fence, smelling of cologne and old tobacco. For the first time, the performance faltered. His hands came out of his pockets, and he touched the gate latch, then let it go. “You should have waited,” he said, quieter. “I was coming.”

“No,” Theodoros said. “You were never coming. You were just never leaving.”

The man stood for a while, looking at the graded yard, the line of the drainage trench now buried, the small black sump cover against the wall. Then he turned, got into the Mercedes, and drove away. He did not look back.

Theodoros stood in the yard for a long time after the car had disappeared. He did not feel triumph. He felt the ache in his shoulders, the slight shiver of exhaustion, and, underneath it, a kind of relief that the man was gone. Gone, but not finished. The money was gone too, all of it, and the extension had not been built. The yard was flat and stable. The house was dry. The pump hummed quietly against the wall, a low, constant pulse, like a second heart.

That evening, he sat with Eleni on the back step. She had come to collect the last of her tools and to settle the final bill. The sun was going down over the salt lake, turning the water a deep rose. His mother brought them two glasses of lemonade and a plate of small cakes she had made that morning, the flour still on her blouse.

“You don’t have to pay me all at once,” Eleni said, not looking at him. “I know the extra decisions cost more than you’d planned. The wiring, the fill, the generator. I can wait for the rest.”

He looked at the lemonade glass in his hands. The juice was cold, the pulp suspended. “I’ll pay by the end of the month. I have a salary. It’s not much, but it’s steady.” He paused. “And the extension… I don’t know. Maybe in a year. Maybe two.”

“The house is safe now,” she said. “That’s not nothing.”

The pump clicked on, a soft mechanical grunt, then clicked off. It ran in cycles now, triggered by the float. The water from the sump ran out through the side of the building, along a new swale planted with coarse grass, and seeped into the far corner of the garden, where the lemon tree was already greener than it had been in months.

“What will you do about Cristos?” Eleni asked.

Theodoros thought about it. The legal advice, the complaint to the police, the word around the neighborhood. He thought about the man’s face at the fence, the white Mercedes, the smile that never quite broke. “I don’t know,” he said. “Maybe nothing. Maybe tell the story to whoever asks. Some of them already know.” He took a sip of lemonade. “I used to think the worst thing would be to let him keep the money. But the worst thing was the water in the kitchen, and that’s over.”

Eleni was quiet. Then she said, “In Athens, I saw a man cheat a restaurant owner for 20,000 euros. The owner paid a lawyer, got a judgment, and a year later the man filed for bankruptcy. He got nothing. But the restaurant owner, he used to sit in the square with his friends and tell the story, and every time he told it, he’d laugh at the end. Not a happy laugh. A laugh like a scar. It reminded him he survived.” She lifted her glass to him. “So maybe you’ll tell it in the café.”

Theodoros looked at her. The rising moon was full and low, and the pump clicked off. The house stood behind them, dry and quiet. His mother was inside, moving the albums back to the shelves, one by one.

The next afternoon, he did tell it. He sat at To Perivoli with a bitter coffee and a glass of water, and when Kyriakos asked about the hole, Theodoros began at the beginning: the white shirt, the clipboard, six thousand euros, the rain. He told it plainly, without ornament. The old men at the next table stopped their game and listened. By the time he reached the part about the generator and the rain in October, a small crowd had gathered. They slapped their knees and shook their heads. One old man with a purple nose said, “Kolios, that son of a donkey,” and spat on the ground.

Afterwards, a man introduced himself as a developer from Kiti. He had a plot of land and needed someone to lay a foundation. “Your engineer,” he said. “The one who saved your house. Give me her number.”

Theodoros wrote Eleni’s number on the corner of a napkin. The developer folded it carefully and put it in his wallet. Then he ordered a round of coffees for the table, and the afternoon passed into evening, the smell of the sea and the basil and the old men’s cigarettes, and the sound of the dice clicking on the board.

By the end of the month, the ground around the house was firm. The grass had grown over the graded fill, and the small swale at the corner of the garden was a line of green that the neighbor’s rooster liked to dust itself in each morning. The kitchen floor was dry, though the boards still showed a faint watermark, a high-water mark, and the wall by the door had a patch of new plaster that no one had painted yet.

Theodoros’s mother moved her photographs back to the shelf by the window, where the light came in. She arranged them by year, earliest on the left, and stood back to look. The albums were dry. She touched the spines, one by one, as if greeting old friends.

At night, the pump clicked on and off, a small, steady metronome. Sometimes Theodoros woke and lay listening to it, the sound of water moving away from the place he lived. It was not a solution. It was not an ending, not exactly. But it was a kind of sense, a logic to the ground, and he slept better now, with the rooster’s first crow still hours away.
