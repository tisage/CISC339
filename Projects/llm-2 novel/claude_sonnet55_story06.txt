Rima Abu Salim came into the atelier on a Tuesday, three days before the opening, while Hala Sabbagh was on her knees in the back room pinning a hem on a mannequin that did not stand straight.

"There's a girl asking for you," said Dina, the receptionist. "She says you'll know the name."

"Whose name?"

"Hers. She wouldn't say it. She said you would know it when you saw her."

Hala took the pins out of her mouth and set them in the dish on the floor. It was a habit from her first years, when she had stitched on a rented table in Jabal Amman and everything she owned fit in dishes. She was forty-one. The atelier now occupied two floors of a restored stone house in Jabal al-Weibdeh, with a garden, a cat that belonged to no one, and a wall of framed press: *Vogue Arabia*, a column in *Le Monde*, a photograph of a Parisian actress in a coat of midnight wool with a cross-stitched cypress rising up one sleeve. Hala had designed that cypress and it was now the label's emblem. It was on the bags, the boxes, and the little woven tags that said *Sabbagh* in a script she had paid a calligrapher in Fez to draw.

She came out wiping her hands on her trousers.

The young woman by the front table was perhaps twenty-three, in a gray hijab and a denim jacket too thin for February. She was not looking at the garments. She was looking at the floor, where the sun made a long rectangle on the tiles, as if she had been told not to touch anything and was taking it seriously.

Hala would have known her anywhere, though she had never seen her. The set of the mouth was the old woman's. So was the way she stood with her weight on the outside of her feet, as people do who have spent their lives sitting on floors.

"You're Umm Salim's granddaughter," Hala said.

"Rima." She looked up. "She said you'd remember her."

"Of course I remember her." Her voice came out too bright, a hostess's voice. "Please, sit. Dina, coffee. Or tea? Rima, which do you want?"

"I don't need anything. I came on the bus. I only wanted to ask you something."

"Then sit and ask."

Rima sat on the edge of the velvet bench, the one Hala had had reupholstered in Beirut for four hundred dinars. She put her bag in her lap and kept both hands on it.

"My grandmother is not well," she said. "It's her mind, mostly. Some days she's herself and some days she's in Isdud, before. She asks for the same things. She asks for her mother, and she asks for you."

"For me."

"She asks about the book." Rima said it flatly, without accusation, as she might have said the name of a medicine. "The book you were making. With her patterns. She says the lady from Amman is making a book and her name will be on the first page, with the village. She wants to know if it's come yet. Every day. My aunt told her it was printing. That was two years ago. Now my aunt is tired of saying it."

The coffee arrived. Dina set it down and went away with the quick tact of someone who had heard the silence change. Hala looked at the little cup. The gold rim had a chip she had meant to replace.

"The book," she said.

"I looked for it. I asked at the library. I searched online." Rima's eyes were dry and steady. "I found your exhibition. They say you've revived the tatreez of the Palestinian coast. There's a photograph of a dress with a cypress, and I know that cypress. She has it on the chest of her wedding dress. It's been in the trunk since 1948, because she carried it."

"Rima—"

"I'm not here to make trouble." She said it quickly. "I'm studying pharmacy. I don't know about this world. I only want to ask whether there's a book. If there's no book, I'll tell her something. If there is one, I'll take it to her. That's all."

Hala heard in her own head the sound of a pen scratching, then a pause, then the soft press of a thumb on an ink pad. Fifteen years earlier, in a front room in Baqa'a camp where a gas heater ticked and a sparrow was trapped against the window screen, an old woman had pressed her thumb to a paper that Hala had said was a permission to photograph.

It had not said that. In clear, legal Arabic, with a clause Hala had copied from a lawyer's template, it had said that Khadra Abu Salim granted full and perpetual rights of reproduction, adaptation and commercial use to Hala Sabbagh in the patterns she had provided. Hala had known what it said. She had read it twice that morning in the car.

"There's no book yet," Hala said.

Rima nodded slowly, a pharmacist's nod, a small acknowledgment of a symptom.

"But there's going to be," Hala said. "I've been planning it. It's part of the exhibition, a catalogue. The printer is waiting on one last section."

She heard herself. She stopped, and the stopping hung there.

"When can I see it?" said Rima.

"Come Friday. To the opening. I'll have a copy for her. I'll put her name on the first page. I promise it."

"On Friday it'll exist?"

"Yes."

Rima looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she took a folded paper from her bag, a photocopy of the museum announcement, and set it on the table between them. "My aunt says I shouldn't come. She says rich people don't like a surprise. But I'll come. I'll bring my grandmother, if she's well enough."

"Bring her," Hala said. "Please."

After Rima left, Hala sat alone for a long time. Then she went upstairs and shut the door of the archive room, a cool windowless place lined with acid-free boxes. In the third drawer, in a folder labeled *Source, Baqa'a, 2009*, there were forty-one pieces of tracing paper. Each had a grid of tiny penciled crosses and a name, in Hala's own twenty-six-year-old handwriting: *cypress (large)*, *almond blossom*, *the water-carrier's jar*, *comb*, *moon over the threshing floor*.

She had told herself for fifteen years that the patterns belonged to everyone, that a village's art is a river, that no one owns a river. She had told herself that the three hundred dinars she paid Umm Salim, plus the sack of rice and the heater fuel, was fair. She had told herself that without her the patterns would have died with the old women, and now they were on the shoulders of women in Paris and Doha. She had a cooperative, too, nine women in Marka, though six now, and the work had dwindled since she'd moved production to a factory in Tunis. That was a business decision. Anyone would have made it.

She laid out the tracings on the steel table and looked at them under the light, and for the first time in years she looked at them without thinking about what they could become.

She had been so pleased with herself, back then, for cleaning them.

---

The drive to Baqa'a on Wednesday took forty minutes through ugly traffic. She had not told anyone. She left the car at the edge of the camp, which was not a camp anymore but a dense town of concrete and rebar, with cables looped overhead like black hair. She carried a bag with a thermos of coffee, a box of the good dates from Jericho, and in a folder under her arm the tracings. She had no plan beyond that.

She found the building by memory and by the pomegranate tree in the courtyard, which was bare now, with a few mummified fruits on the high branches. The door was opened by Rima's aunt, a heavyset woman who looked at Hala and then, with great slowness, at the folder.

"She said you'd come," said the aunt. "Not Rima. My mother. She said it this morning. 'The Amman lady is coming today.' I told her it was Wednesday and nobody comes on Wednesday."

"Is she awake?"

"She's awake. Whether she's here is another question."

The front room was smaller than Hala remembered, or she was larger. There was the same gas heater, the same cushioned bench along three walls, and a television on mute showing a cooking program. Umm Salim sat in the corner of the bench, wrapped in a quilted housecoat, her white scarf fastened under her chin. She was very small. Hala remembered a broad, strong woman who had slapped the table to make a point. This one had a tremor in her hands that she kept folded under a blanket.

"Umm Salim," Hala said, and bent and kissed her on both cheeks. The skin smelled of rosewater and old wool. "It's Hala. Hala Sabbagh. From Amman."

The old woman looked at her. The eyes were clouded at the rim but intent, and for a moment there was nothing in them, only attention, the way a bird watches. Then they cleared.

"The one with the book," she said.

"Yes. The one with the book."

"You're thin. Why are you thin? Sit. Fatima, bring her something."

"I've brought dates." Hala put the box in the aunt's hands. She sat on the bench, close enough to touch. Her heart was going hard. "Umm Salim, I came to tell you about the book."

"Is it printed?"

Hala had practiced the sentence in the car, in three versions. None of them survived the old woman's face.

"No," she said. "It isn't. I haven't made it."

The old woman nodded as if this were a reasonable thing to hear. "These things take time. My husband's brother wanted to build a second floor and it took eleven years. He died before the roof." She patted the blanket. "But you'll make it."

"I want to show you something."

She opened the folder on her knees. She had thought about whether to bring the finished garments, photographs of the coat, the clutch bags, the dress in the exhibition poster. She had decided against it. She didn't want to be understood through her success. She wanted to be understood through the paper, the thing they had made together once, when she was a girl with a notebook and the old woman had drawn on the floor with her finger.

She took out the tracing of the cypress.

Umm Salim leaned forward. Her hands came out from the blanket, shaking, and hovered above the page without touching it. Her lips moved. She was counting.

"That's the tall one. For the chest." She counted again, and her finger traced the air an inch above the paper, and stopped. "No."

"No?"

"This isn't right." She said it mildly, the way she might have commented on a dish. "Where's the step?"

"The step?"

"At the foot. The last row. You go across, you go across, and at the end you step down. One stitch. Like a man who's tired and sits." She shook her head. "Here it's all level. Who did this? Did you do this?"

Hala looked at the tracing. At the base of the cypress, where the trunk met the ground-line, was a perfect row of twelve crosses. She remembered the afternoon she had drawn it. The old woman's sample, on the original dress, had an extra stitch dropped to the row below at the left end, one little cross hanging off the line like a loose tooth. Hala had taken it for a slip of the needle, something sixty years of work could be forgiven. She had drawn it straight, because the French designers who would look at it wanted clean repeats and no one would sew a cypress with a limp.

"I thought it was a mistake," she said. "I fixed it."

"A mistake." Umm Salim laughed, a dry sound like paper. "My mother did it. Her mother did it. Everyone in Isdud did it. It's how you know the cypress is from us, from the village. In Majdal they step up. In Beit Jibrin, there's no step, they're proud, they like it flat." She tapped the air. "The step is the place. Without the step it's a tree, any tree. It could be from anywhere."

Hala could not speak. She had drawn the cypress on forty thousand tags, on coats, on a bottle of perfume. It was level at the foot every time.

"And this?" The old woman, quicker now, pulled the next tracing from the pile herself. The almond blossom. "Where's the mourning stitch? There's a black one in the middle for my sister Zahra. I put it in after she died. You took it out?"

"I thought it was a different color mistake."

"A mistake." The old woman looked at her for a long time, and what was in her face was not anger. It was nearer to puzzlement, a patient and tired curiosity, like looking at a child who had cut a hole in a good cloth to see what was behind it. "You took out the places where we cried."

The aunt, Fatima, had come back with the tea. She stood in the doorway and said nothing.

"Umm Salim," Hala said, and found that her voice would not hold. She put the folder down. "I did something to you. Years ago. The paper you put your thumb on. It wasn't what I said it was."

"I can't read," said the old woman.

"I know. That's why."

"Fatima, did you hear? She says she did something." She turned back to Hala, and her eyes were quite clear. For one moment she was entirely there, a woman who had crossed a border at the age of eleven carrying a rolled-up dress. "What did you do?"

So Hala told her. She did not tell it well. She said that the paper gave her the right to use the patterns and sell them, and that she had sold them, and that the cypress was now famous and she was famous, and the old woman's name was in none of it. She said she had paid three hundred dinars. She said she had meant to write the book, and then there was a meeting in Paris, and the collaboration, and then a loan against the lease, and each time the book had seemed like something she could do in the next quiet month. She heard how it sounded. Every sentence was true, and every sentence was an excuse, and there was no way to say one without the other.

When she stopped, the gas heater ticked. On the silent television a man was folding eggs into a pan.

Umm Salim said, "Fatima. Bring me my bag."

Fatima went into the back and returned with a cloth sewing bag, faded to no color. The old woman rummaged through it with her trembling hands, past spools and a thimble, and came up with a half-finished piece, a strip of black linen about a forearm long. On it a border of small red crosses ran toward a cypress, unfinished at the top.

"I've been making this for Rima's wedding," she said. "She's not engaged. It doesn't matter. Take it."

"I can't take that."

"Not to keep. To hold." She pushed it into Hala's hands. It was heavier than it should have been, dense with thread. "Look at the border. Where does it stop?"

Hala looked. The last row of crosses ended in the middle of the cloth, in the middle of a stitch, where the needle had been left.

"Pick it up," said Umm Salim. "My hands won't do it anymore. They go like this." She held them up, the tremor visible. "Finish the row. And at the end, make the step."

"I'm not good enough."

"No," the old woman agreed. "You're not. Do it anyway."

Fatima brought a lamp. Hala threaded the needle with the red cotton, which took three tries because her own hands were not steady. She counted the crosses already laid, as she had been taught long ago in a different room, and set the needle in the next square of the linen. Cross over cross. The thread drew with a tiny rasp. Beside her, Umm Salim watched with her chin lowered, and when Hala went too fast the old woman put a shaking finger on her wrist.

"Slowly. You're not selling it."

Hala slowed. She did seven more crosses. The twelfth came at the end of the row, and she stopped with the needle above the cloth.

"Down," said the old woman.

She dropped the last stitch one row below the line. It looked wrong. It looked like a flaw, a thing a seamstress at Maison Verreaux would have picked out with a razor. She sat looking at it, and her eyes stung, which she resented, because it seemed a theft, to cry over this in front of the woman she had robbed.

"There," said Umm Salim. "Now it's from somewhere."

The museum was on the Citadel hill, in a renovated Ottoman building with a courtyard of lemon trees strung with lights. On Friday evening the invitation list was four hundred names: the minister of culture, the French ambassador's wife, buyers from Dubai, bloggers, a cluster of teenage girls from a design school who wanted selfies beside the mannequins. The exhibition was called *Threads of Return: Hala Sabbagh and the Embroidery of Palestine*. The title was hers, in white letters on the central wall, beside the portrait of herself in a linen jacket, her hand on a loom.

She had been there since four. The curator, Samia, a brisk woman in oversized glasses, had walked her through the rooms twice. "The wall text is in the proofs you approved," Samia said. "Printed and mounted. The foreword's by the ambassador. And the catalogue arrived from the printer this morning."

"The catalogue," Hala said. "Is my foreword in it?"

"Of course. You wrote it. 'A lifetime of listening to the hands of women.'"

"Take it out."

Samia stared at her. "It's printed."

"I know. I'll pay for a reprint. I'll pay for an insert if there isn't time. I want to add a page at the front, before the foreword. I have the text."

"Hala, the opening is in two hours."

"Then there's time to be annoyed with me."

She had written the page on Thursday night, and rewritten it four times. In the end it was short. It gave Khadra Abu Salim's name, the village of Isdud, the year of departure, and the forty-one patterns now in the collection. It said, in a sentence she did not allow herself to soften, that the rights to those patterns had been obtained under terms the woman could not read. It said that as of that day the rights were returned in full to her and her heirs, with a signed instrument from the lawyer she had called on Wednesday evening and who had said, over the phone, in a flat voice, *You understand what you're doing to the brand.* She understood. The French house would hear of it. The sleeve of the actress's coat would become a story with a different shape. She had a lease and sixteen employees, and she did not know what would be left in a year.

Samia read the page standing in the middle of the gallery, with the lemon-scented courtyard air coming through the open doors. When she finished, she took off her glasses.

"You know the ambassador's wife is on the list," she said. "And the people from Verreaux, who flew in."

"I know."

"You could do it quietly. A letter. Afterward, when—"

"I did the quiet thing," said Hala. "I did it for fifteen years."

Samia looked at her for some time. Then she put her glasses back on. "I'll call the printer. It'll be a loose sheet, tipped in. Not elegant."

"No," Hala said. "Good."

By seven the courtyard was full. Hala stood in the central room by the first case, in a plain black dress, receiving people's hands. Someone told her it was a triumph. Someone told her the cypress coat was the most beautiful object they had seen. She thanked them and kept her eyes on the door.

They came at half past seven. Rima first, in a long dark coat, her hijab pinned with care, holding the handles of a wheelchair. In it, wrapped in two blankets and wearing a clean white scarf, sat Umm Salim, looking at the lights and the people with the grave, approving air of an old woman at a wedding where she knows no one.

The crowd parted for the wheelchair without knowing why. Hala crossed the room. She did not rehearse anything. She bent and kissed the old woman's hand, and Umm Salim said, "So this is the house."

"This is it."

"Is the book here?"

Hala took the catalogue from Samia, who had come up behind her carrying it in both hands. The blue cover was glossy. Inside, tipped in before the title page, was a loose sheet printed an hour before, and on it, in Arabic and English, stood the name *Khadra Abu Salim* in type larger than Hala's own.

She did not hand the book over. She sat on the floor beside the wheelchair, in her good black dress, with a hundred people watching, and opened it on the old woman's knees, and put a finger beneath the first line.

"Rima, will you read it to her?" she said. "I'd like her to hear it from someone in the family."

Rima looked at her. Then she took the book, and in a clear, careful voice, as if reading a prescription, she began.

Behind them the room had gone quiet in the strange way that rooms do. The French ambassador's wife was whispering to someone. A man in a gray suit, who must have been from Verreaux, was already taking out his phone. Hala did not look at them. She watched Umm Salim, who had listened to the first line with her head tilted, and now looked down at the printed name and traced it with the tip of her trembling finger, though she could not read it. She traced it as she had traced the cypress in the air, counting.

"It stops," the old woman said, when Rima finished. "At the end. The letters step down."

"That's the Arabic, Teta," said Rima gently. "It's how it's written."

"No." Umm Salim shook her head, pleased, stubborn. "Look. It steps."

Hala looked, and then did not look, because the line was blurring. Nothing in the page had changed. The old woman's finger rested at the end of her own name, one stitch below the line, and the room went on talking around them, and the phones came up, and somewhere behind her Samia was telling someone, very evenly, that no, the exhibition would not be closing.

Hala stayed on the floor. After a while she felt a hand on her shoulder, light and shaking, patting her twice as one pats a child who has finally finished confessing and may now go and eat.

"Get up," said Umm Salim. "You'll ruin the dress."
