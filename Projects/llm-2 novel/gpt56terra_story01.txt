Han Haejin had known Minjae for fourteen months before they took a vacation together.

This seemed, to Minjae, a suspiciously long time.

“Fourteen months,” he said, standing in her kitchen with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, as if preparing to make an argument with his hands. “We have been together through three Christmases.”

“Two,” Haejin said.

“One was lunar Christmas.”

“That is not a thing.”

“It should be.”

He smiled at her over the stack of pale-blue bowls she had washed and left to dry. His smile had been one of the first things she liked about him. It was never cautious. He smiled with the expectation that the world would meet him halfway.

“Anyway,” he said, “I think it is time we saw each other under difficult conditions.”

“Difficult conditions?”

“Rain. Mosquitoes. A rental car with an unfamiliar navigation system. One bathroom.”

“We’ve had dinner.”

“Dinner is performance.”

She dried her hands on the towel hanging from the refrigerator handle. “And vacation isn’t?”

“No. Vacation is where character is revealed.”

“You sound like you are proposing a test.”

“Exactly.”

He had booked the plane tickets to Jeju before she had entirely agreed. Four days in late September, after Chuseok, when the summer crowds had gone and the wind began to change. He had found a pension on the western side of the island, a low stone house with a tangerine orchard behind it and a view, according to the photographs, of the sea from the bathtub.

“You have a bathtub?” Haejin had asked.

“I have a bathtub.”

“You booked it without asking me?”

“You told me last winter you wanted to see the island when the fields were yellow.”

“I said I wanted to see it someday.”

“This is someday.”

At work, Haejin told the pharmacy technician she was taking leave.

“A trip with your boyfriend?” the technician asked, leaning over the counter. “First trip?”

Haejin nodded.

The technician clicked her tongue sympathetically. “Bring separate chargers. And do not let him choose every restaurant.”

“Why would he choose every restaurant?”

“Because men who say they like to travel always have a list.”

Haejin did not think Minjae had a list. He was the kind of person who found restaurants by walking down side streets and looking through windows. He liked places with handwritten menus and chairs that did not match. He had taken her, on their third date, to a noodle shop near Euljiro where the owner had shouted at him for asking whether there was less spicy kimchi. He had been delighted.

“He likes accidents,” Haejin said.

The technician looked up. “That is worse.”

On the morning they left Seoul, Haejin arrived at Gimpo Airport forty minutes early, with a navy suitcase, a canvas backpack, and an envelope containing their reservation confirmations printed on paper.

Minjae saw the envelope before he saw her face.

“Oh,” he said. “You printed things.”

“In case the phone signal is bad.”

“It’s Jeju, not the Himalayas.”

“I know.”

He put one arm around her shoulders, kissed the top of her head, and took the envelope from her hand.

“Look at us,” he said. “A very balanced couple. You prepare for disaster. I prevent disaster by refusing to believe in it.”

“Give that back.”

He laughed and handed it over.

On the plane he slept with his mouth slightly open, his head tilted toward the window. Haejin watched the cloud tops spread under them, flat and white as rice laid out to dry. She had not told him that she had never taken a trip with anyone except family. As a child, vacations had meant visiting relatives in Busan or her grandfather’s village near Tongyeong, where her father would wake them before dawn to avoid traffic and complain for the entire drive that nobody had packed correctly.

When her father complained, he had made it sound as though the wrong arrangement of socks could lead to national ruin.

Minjae would have liked her father, Haejin thought. For an hour or two.

Jeju met them with a bright hard wind. The airport parking lot was full of rental cars, each with a different colored tag hanging from the mirror. Minjae had reserved a small white Kia. He walked around it solemnly while filming a video of the existing scratches.

“This is the most important part of the vacation,” he said. “Evidence.”

“You said vacation was not performance.”

“This is survival.”

The navigation system spoke in a woman’s soft, impatient voice. Minjae ignored it twice, then blamed it for taking them through a village road where a delivery truck had stopped in the middle of the lane.

Haejin sat with the reservation envelope in her lap. Out the window, black walls divided fields of buckwheat and garlic. The sky was too large. Wind bent the silver grass along the roadside in one direction.

“Are you hungry?” Minjae asked.

“Yes.”

“Good. There’s a place near Hallim with abalone porridge. It has two hundred thousand followers.”

“You have a list.”

“I have instincts.”

The restaurant had a line extending down the road. Haejin stood behind Minjae, listening to the wind snap at the paper flag above the doorway. After ten minutes, he turned.

“We can go somewhere else.”

“We’re already here.”

“You’re hungry.”

“I can wait.”

“But you don’t want to.”

“It’s fine.”

He looked at her for a moment. “Haejin-ah. You’re allowed to want things.”

“I know.”

“You say ‘it’s fine’ whenever you don’t want to argue.”

“It really is fine.”

His expression softened. “Then we’ll stay.”

They waited forty-five minutes. The porridge was expensive and excellent. Minjae took a photograph of the abalone before he remembered that Haejin disliked being photographed while eating, and he lowered his phone with an apologetic grimace.

That night, the pension owner, a narrow woman with permed gray hair named Mrs. Ko, showed them their room. There was indeed a bathtub by a wide window, although the sea was farther away than it had appeared in the photographs, a gray stripe between roofs and tangerine trees.

Mrs. Ko pointed to a laminated card on the table.

“If the wind gets stronger, keep the outer gate latched,” she said. “The weather says rain tomorrow afternoon. Nothing serious. On Jeju, they say that every day.”

“Is there a good place nearby for breakfast?” Minjae asked.

Mrs. Ko considered this.

“There is a convenience store.”

He thanked her warmly anyway.

After she left, Haejin unpacked her suitcase in the small wardrobe. Minjae did not unpack at all. He threw his duffel bag on the floor and opened a bottle of hallabong beer from the refrigerator.

“You fold everything before a four-day trip?” he asked.

“If I don’t, I can’t find anything.”

“You can find it. You just have to look.”

“That is the definition of not being able to find it.”

He sat on the floor beside her suitcase, drinking from the bottle.

“You know,” he said, “I’ve always wondered what you’re like in the morning.”

“I’m like this, but less polite.”

“Finally.”

She looked at him. “You’re very confident I will be unpleasant.”

“I am hoping for it.”

He held out the beer. She took it, drank, and made a face.

“Too sweet?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“You didn’t have to drink it.”

“You offered.”

“You don’t have to accept everything I offer.”

For a moment she thought he was teasing her. His eyes were smiling, but not entirely.

“Then I won’t,” she said, and handed the bottle back.

The next day they drove east along the coast with the windows cracked open. The rain had not begun, but clouds gathered above Hallasan in bruised layers. They stopped at a roadside stand for roasted corn and ate it standing beside the car. Minjae burned his fingers and shook his hand dramatically.

“You know what I like about this?” he said.

“The corn?”

“No. Being somewhere nobody needs anything from us.”

Haejin peeled a strip of charred husk from her corn.

“Nobody needs anything from us in Seoul either.”

“That’s not true. Your mother calls you every day.”

“She calls to ask if I ate breakfast.”

“And then tells you your aunt needs help moving furniture, or your cousin wants advice about his medication, or your brother forgot to renew something.”

“My brother is useless with paperwork.”

“He’s thirty-four.”

“I know how old he is.”

Minjae smiled, trying to soften it. “I’m just saying, you let everyone put a little basket on your head.”

“My family does not put baskets on my head.”

“No. You pick them up before anyone asks.”

The wind took a napkin from the dashboard and sent it fluttering under Haejin’s feet.

She bent to retrieve it. “You said you wanted nobody to need anything from us.”

“I said I liked it.”

“You make it sound like my family is a burden.”

“I make it sound like you never say no.”

She folded the napkin twice and put it in the door pocket.

At the next intersection, the navigation system instructed them to turn right. Minjae turned left.

“You missed it,” Haejin said.

“I’m taking the coast road.”

“It said there was construction.”

“The coast road is prettier.”

“It may take longer.”

“We’re on vacation.”

He gave the last word a lightness that made her feel foolish for replying.

They spent that afternoon at a museum in Seogwipo because rain began before lunch and came down so heavily that the roads shone black. The museum had an exhibition of old diving equipment: rubber suits, glass masks, rusted knives, photographs of haenyeo standing in groups with their hair tied back and their faces unsmiling.

Haejin stood before one photograph for a long time. The women had come out of the water with nets over their shoulders. Behind them, the sea was white with winter waves.

Minjae came to stand beside her.

“What are you thinking?” he asked.

“My grandmother used to know women like this.”

“On Jeju?”

“Near Geoje. Not divers. Fisherwomen.”

“Did she fish?”

“No. She ran a small store. But she said you could tell what kind of person someone was by how they came back from the sea.”

“And what kind were these?”

Haejin looked at the photograph again.

“Tired.”

Minjae laughed quietly. “That is a very pharmacist answer.”

“What is a roaster answer?”

He studied the image. “They look like they have a secret.”

“That doesn’t mean anything.”

“It can mean something.”

“You always say that.”

“What?”

“That things mean something when you don’t know what they mean.”

He turned toward her. “And you always act as if nothing means anything until it can be written down in a report.”

The museum was nearly empty. Rain struck the glass doors at the entrance. Haejin could hear a child whining somewhere in another room.

“I don’t do that,” she said.

“Okay.”

“I don’t.”

“Okay, Haejin.”

But the way he said it made the word feel like a lid being put on a pot.

That evening they ate black pork at a restaurant with plastic curtains instead of walls. The rain had eased to a mist. On the table grill, the pork hissed and curled. Minjae poured soju into their glasses.

“To our first real trip,” he said.

Haejin lifted hers. “We came on a plane.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

“I know.”

“To discovering our secret selves.”

“That sounds threatening.”

“It’s romantic.”

She drank.

Later, at the pension, he ran the bath and opened the window above it. Cold damp air entered the room. Haejin sat in the water with her knees up, watching the dark shape of the orchard sway outside. From the bedroom, Minjae played a song through his phone, something slow and acoustic that she did not recognize.

When he came in, he sat on the closed toilet lid and looked at her.

“Are you angry at me?” he asked.

“No.”

“Really?”

“Yes.”

“You were quiet at dinner.”

“I was tired.”

“Are you quiet because you’re tired, or are you quiet because you don’t want to tell me something?”

She let her wet hand trail across the surface of the water. “Do I have to answer correctly?”

“I’m asking.”

“You always ask like there is a correct answer.”

He was silent for a while.

“I don’t want us to become one of those couples who pretend nothing is wrong,” he said.

“Nothing is wrong.”

“You see?”

Haejin leaned her head against the tile.

She knew he was trying. That was the trouble. He wanted a closeness so complete that even a pause between them seemed like a locked door. At first she had found it touching. He remembered things she said in passing. He noticed when she changed shampoo. Once, when she had mentioned that she disliked the smell of peaches because of a childhood medicine, he had removed a peach candle from his café before she came to visit.

But sometimes he watched her face as if it contained a message he had a right to decode.

“I’m tired,” she said again.

He stood and brushed his teeth.

The next morning, the rain had stopped. Mrs. Ko left two tangerines and a bag of bread outside their door. There was a handwritten note: Weather clears after noon. Watch the wind.

They had planned to walk part of the Olle Trail near the western coast before driving to a small village where Minjae had booked a seafood dinner. He wanted to arrive before sunset, when the owner brought out tables facing the harbor.

“We have to leave by four-thirty,” he said over instant coffee. “Or we’ll miss it.”

“Okay.”

“You don’t sound excited.”

“I am excited.”

“You’re doing it again.”

Haejin looked at him over her cup.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

He took a breath. “I know you think I make too much of things.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You don’t have to say it.”

The wind rattled the windowpane.

Haejin set down her cup. “You asked if I was excited. I said yes.”

“Like someone answering a survey.”

“What do you want me to say?”

“I don’t know. Something honest.”

Her chest tightened. “You don’t believe me when I’m honest.”

He stared at her.

Then he looked away and began putting on his shoes.

The trail began beside a beach of dark volcanic stones. The sea was rougher than it looked from the pension, waves lifting and collapsing in gray-green folds. They walked between low walls through fields where horses grazed with their heads bowed. The path was marked with blue-and-orange ribbons tied to posts.

For the first hour, neither of them spoke much. Haejin listened to their steps on the wet ground, to the wind in the reeds. She began to feel calmer. This was what she had wanted from the trip: not happiness exactly, but distance. The island had no place for the small demands of her apartment, the blinking light on the water purifier, the weekly family group chat, the stack of insurance forms in the drawer by her front door.

Ahead of them, an older woman stood at a fork in the path.

She wore a bright purple hiking vest over a floral shirt and held a folded trail map at arm’s length. Her white visor kept lifting in the wind. Beside her sat a small wheeled suitcase, the kind Haejin’s mother used for visiting relatives.

As Haejin and Minjae approached, the woman looked up.

“Excuse me,” she called. “Is this the way to Gwakji?”

Minjae glanced at his phone. “Gwakji is behind you, I think.”

The woman’s face fell.

“I knew it,” she said. “I knew I had gone wrong.”

Haejin looked at the map. It was soaked at the creases. The woman had circled a section of the trail in red pen, but the circles did not correspond to where they were.

“Where are you staying?” Haejin asked.

The woman gave the name of a guesthouse in Aewol, several kilometers away.

“You can take a bus from the main road,” Haejin said.

“I was trying to find the bus. A young man told me to follow the blue ribbon, but I followed the blue ribbon for almost an hour and now I think he was joking.”

“He may have meant the trail,” Minjae said.

The woman looked close to tears. “My phone has no battery. I thought I would walk before breakfast and come back by ten. My daughter said not to go alone, but she thinks I am ninety.”

“How old are you?” Minjae asked.

“Seventy-two.”

“Then she is only a little wrong.”

The woman gave him an offended look. Haejin almost smiled.

“There is a road about twenty minutes that way,” Minjae said, pointing back in the direction they had come. “You can wait for a bus there.”

“Do buses come often?”

He checked his phone. “Every forty minutes.”

The woman nodded, but did not move.

Haejin looked at the suitcase. Its wheels were clogged with mud.

“We can walk with you to the road,” she said.

Minjae turned to her.

“It’s twenty minutes back,” he said quietly.

“Then twenty minutes.”

“We are more than an hour from the car already.”

“We can take a taxi from the road.”

“There may not be taxis.”

“We’ll figure it out.”

The older woman looked between them as if she had suddenly realized she was intruding on a private conversation.

“Oh, no,” she said. “Please go. I will be fine.”

Haejin bent to lift the suitcase handle. “Which guesthouse did you say?”

Minjae caught her wrist.

His hand was warm. Not hard, but firm.

“Haejin,” he said.

“What?”

He lowered his voice. “We have plans.”

“She doesn’t know where she is.”

“She knows where the road is now.”

“She has been walking in the wrong direction for an hour.”

“And we corrected her.”

The woman stood with both hands folded over the strap of her purse. The visor flapped in the wind.

Haejin pulled her wrist gently from Minjae’s hand.

“If she were your mother,” she said, “would you leave her here?”

“My mother wouldn’t come walking alone on a stormy trail with a suitcase.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

He looked past her toward the long wet path behind them. “My answer is that we can’t stop our lives for every person who makes a bad decision.”

It was not said cruelly. That was what made it so clear.

Haejin picked up the suitcase.

The woman began protesting at once. “No, no, young lady. You must not ruin your trip because of me.”

“It’s not ruined,” Haejin said.

Minjae gave a short laugh without amusement.

“Fine,” he said. “I’ll take the suitcase.”

He reached for it, but Haejin had already started walking.

For several minutes they went in silence, the older woman between them. Her name was Park Bokja. She lived in Suwon with her eldest son and had come to Jeju with a neighborhood hiking club, but had argued with the club leader the night before because he insisted everyone meet in the lobby at six in the morning.

“Six!” she said. “On vacation. I told him, ‘Chairman Choi, you are not my husband.’”

Minjae made a polite sound.

Mrs. Park told them about her late husband, who had hated travel and had spent thirty-six years in a machine parts factory. When their son was young, she had once asked to go to Jeju, and he had said, “What is on Jeju that is not on television?”

“Now I go whenever I can,” she said. “I have seen Jeju seven times. I will see it seven more if I live.”

The wind grew stronger. The path narrowed along a low bluff. To their left, the sea struck the rocks below.

Minjae walked faster. Every few minutes, he checked his phone. Haejin knew what he was calculating: the distance to the car, the remaining walk, the time before the restaurant closed. She was calculating it too. She knew they would not make the sunset table.

When they reached the road, there was no bus shelter, only a faded sign leaning slightly toward a field. The schedule taped beneath it had peeled away on one side.

Mrs. Park sat on the bench and looked at the road.

“I am very sorry,” she said.

“It’s all right,” Haejin said.

Minjae stood with his hands in his jacket pockets. “The next bus is in thirty-two minutes.”

“How do you know?” Mrs. Park asked.

“There’s an app.”

“Ah. Apps.” She sighed. “Everyone is very clever now.”

Haejin looked at the empty road. The clouds had lowered again, swallowing the hills.

“We should wait until she gets on,” she said.

Minjae looked at her.

“We can’t,” he said.

“You can go back to the car. I’ll wait.”

His face changed then, not into anger exactly. Into disbelief.

“You want me to leave you here?”

“I’m saying you don’t have to wait.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

“Then what do you mean?”

He glanced at Mrs. Park, who was looking determinedly in the other direction.

“I mean,” he said, lowering his voice again, “we came here together.”

“I know.”

“And now you’re asking me to walk away alone because you decided we are responsible for this woman.”

“She’s not a package I decided to take home. She needs to get on a bus.”

“And when does it end? If the bus doesn’t come, do we drive her to Aewol? If her guesthouse is closed, do we find her another one? If she loses her friends, do we spend the rest of our vacation helping her?”

Haejin stared at him.

A car went past without slowing. Its tires hissed on the wet road.

“You can say no,” he continued. “You can. It does not make you a bad person.”

“I didn’t say it did.”

“You act like it does.”

“No.” She swallowed. “I think you act like caring about someone means you have failed to care about yourself.”

“That is not fair.”

“Isn’t it?”

His mouth tightened.

“I wanted four days with you,” he said. “Not four days where you take calls from your mother, answer messages from your coworkers, and carry every stranger’s problem as if it belongs to you.”

Mrs. Park turned around.

“You are fighting because of me,” she said.

“No,” Haejin said.

“Yes,” Minjae said at the same time.

The bus appeared before either of them could speak again. It came around the curve in a blue-and-white blur, brakes sighing as it stopped. Minjae lifted Mrs. Park’s suitcase into the storage compartment without being asked. Haejin helped her up the steps.

Mrs. Park held Haejin’s hand tightly.

“You two should be good to each other,” she said.

Haejin nodded because the woman was looking at her with such hope.

When the bus had pulled away, leaving a smell of diesel in the wind, Minjae stood beside the empty road with his head lowered.

“We should go,” he said.

They walked back toward the trail. Neither suggested calling a taxi. The route to the car seemed much longer than it had before.

At one point Haejin slipped on the wet stones. Minjae reached out automatically and caught her elbow. His hand remained there for a second after she had steadied herself.

Then he let go.

They reached the restaurant after dark. The owner had given their outdoor table to another couple. Minjae said it was fine, that they could sit inside, but the restaurant was loud and smelled of grilling fish and wet jackets. They ate without speaking much. The seafood stew was too salty. Haejin could not finish it.

Back at the pension, the wind had blown several tangerines from the trees. They lay scattered across the path like small lanterns.

Minjae took his duffel bag from the floor and began packing.

Haejin watched him from the doorway.

“What are you doing?”

“I’m packing.”

“We leave tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“You said you never unpack.”

“I know.”

He folded a shirt once, badly, and put it into the bag.

“I don’t think I can do this,” he said.

“Do what?”

He sat on the edge of the bed.

“Always be in competition with everyone else who might need you.”

Haejin stood very still.

“You aren’t in competition with anyone.”

“It feels like I am.”

“That woman was lost.”

“I know she was lost.”

“She was seventy-two.”

“I know how old she was, Haejin.”

“Then why are we still talking as if I chose her over you?”

“Because you did.”

The room was silent except for the wind working at the outer gate.

Haejin looked at the suitcase near the wardrobe, at the clothes she had folded so neatly the first night. For the first time, the care she had taken with them seemed strange to her. She had packed for rain, for cold, for blisters, for an upset stomach. She had not packed for the possibility that there might be no way to explain herself to the person beside her.

Minjae rubbed both hands over his face.

“I don’t want to be the kind of man who tells you not to help people,” he said. “But I don’t want a life where there is always one more person at the door. One more emergency. One more reason our plans don’t matter.”

“Our plans mattered.”

“Not enough.”

She could have said that he was making too much of one bus stop, one old woman, one missed dinner. She could have reminded him of all the times he had been generous: paying for a stranger’s coffee, carrying boxes for the old man downstairs from his café, staying late to listen to a customer cry about her divorce.

But those were things he chose. He gave freely when his giving made the world larger around him. What frightened him was being drawn into someone else’s need, without invitation, without a clear edge.

And perhaps what frightened Haejin was the opposite: a life with edges so firm that she could not reach anyone outside them.

“I think you should sleep,” she said.

He looked up. “That’s all?”

“I don’t know what else to say.”

He nodded once.

In the morning, they drove to the airport in rain.

The rental-car navigation system kept telling them to take roads Minjae had already missed. Neither of them corrected it. At the rental return, he carried her suitcase from the trunk and set it beside her without looking at her.

Inside the terminal, he bought two coffees. He remembered she disliked sweet drinks and brought hers black.

She held the cup between both hands.

At the gate, their seats were not together. Haejin had booked them separately by mistake, choosing the cheapest fare before she knew Minjae would come. When she had noticed, he had laughed and said it would be good for them to have a little mystery.

Now he looked at his boarding pass and hers.

“I can ask someone to switch,” he said.

Haejin looked through the window at the wet runway, at the planes lifting into the low clouds one after another.

“No,” she said. “It’s all right.”

He waited as if expecting her to change her mind.

Then he nodded.

When they landed in Seoul, their phones began vibrating almost at once. Messages arrived. Work notices. Family photos. Promotions. A picture from Haejin’s mother of soup simmering in a pot, followed by: Did you eat properly on the island?

Haejin stood in the aisle while passengers pressed around her, lifting bags from the overhead compartments.

Minjae was three rows ahead. He turned once and saw her.

For a moment, in the narrow bright space between the seats, she thought he might wait for her.

Then the line moved, and he went with it.
