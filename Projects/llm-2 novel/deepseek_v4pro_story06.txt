The phone rang at 4:52 a.m., and Linh reached for it with the certainty of someone who has never received good news before sunrise. Her mother’s voice, hoarse and low, said, “Ông ngoại mất rồi, con.” Grandfather is gone. Linh pressed the phone to her ear and waited for the words to make sense. She sat up in the dark of her Thủ Đức apartment, one hand against the cold window, and watched a motorcycle bend around the corner. Outside, Ho Chi Minh City was still half-asleep. She asked how, when, why. Her mother said something about the kitchen floor, about a cup of water, about the neighbor hearing the dog bark. “Come home, Linh.”

She packed black clothes and her camera. Then she left before the morning rush.

The drive to Mỹ Tho took two hours. The highway gave way to the brown river, then to rice paddies stitched with palm shadows. Her mother’s house stood in a narrow lane near the Mekong, its yellow walls sweating from the recent rain. White cloth was already draped over the doorway, and the smell of incense met her at the gate. Aunts and cousins knelt inside, their heads wrapped in white bands, their faces damp. In the center of the front room, beneath the ancestor altar, lay her grandfather in a rosewood casket painted with phoenixes. His eyes were closed. His mouth was covered with a folded silk cloth. He looked peaceful, distant, like a man who had already practiced leaving.

The funeral lasted three days. Monks chanted in Pali. Children chased each other between fruit trays. Men played chess on the porch. Linh knelt beside her mother during the prayers, but her mind kept drifting. She tried to remember her grandfather not as the old man who had spent his last years pruning vegetables in the backyard, but as the man he had been when she was a child. He had taught her to read. He had told her stories about the delta before the wars, about floods that turned the rice fields into an inland sea. In recent years, he had grown silent. He sat for hours by the river, not speaking, as if he were listening for something.

On the third day, they carried him to the cemetery in a light rain. The casket was lowered into the earth. Linh’s mother threw the first handful of soil. The sound of it hitting the wood was smaller than Linh had expected. Then the men filled the grave, and the aunts planted jasmine around the new mound.

That evening, the house was emptier. Uncles left one by one. Linh helped her mother fold the chairs and wash the tea cups. They did not speak about him; they spoke about practical things: the death certificate, the donations to the pagoda, the ancestor altar that needed fresh fruit.

A week later, her mother asked her to clear out his room.

“You knew him better than your cousins,” she said, though Linh wasn’t sure that was true. “He would not want strangers touching his things.”

His room was at the back of the house, past the kitchen. It smelled of camphor and old paper. The bed was made, the mosquito net tied in a loose knot. On the wall hung a wooden crucifix blackened by years of incense smoke, although he had not been Catholic; it had belonged to someone else, a friend from the old days. No one remembered who. There was a bookshelf with cracked volumes of Vietnamese poetry, a row of French dictionaries, and stacks of lesson plans on yellowed paper.

Linh opened the wardrobe and removed his shirts. They were worn at the collars, buttons replaced with mismatched plastic. She folded them into a cardboard box. She emptied the top drawer: socks, reading glasses, a pocket watch that no longer worked. Then she came to the bottom drawer. It held folded trousers, but when she lifted them out, the base of the drawer seemed too high. She pressed on it. The thin board shifted. Beneath it, in a shallow hiding space, lay a blue biscuit tin with white embossed letters, its edges rusted. She lifted it out.

She sat on the floor. She expected documents, land deeds, maybe old money. Instead, she found a photograph.

It was black-and-white, the edges serrated. A young woman in a checked krama smiled past the camera. She had high cheekbones and deep, dark eyes. On either side stood two little girls in matching white shirts. They were in front of a wooden house on stilts, a papaya tree leaning behind them. Linh turned the photograph over. In faded blue ink, in her grandfather’s handwriting: *Srey, Sophal, Bopha – 1983.*

Beneath it lay letters, some in Vietnamese, some in an unfamiliar script. A khaki notebook bound with a rubber band. A dried frangipani flower, so brittle it broke when she touched it. A silver ring, too small for her finger.

She opened the notebook. The first entry was dated 12 March 1980. *Hôm nay đến Phnom Penh.* Today we arrived in Phnom Penh.

Linh read until the light outside the window changed from gold to gray. She read the whole thing, her legs going numb against the tile floor.

Her grandfather, Lê Văn Tư, had been sent to Cambodia after the war, part of a government program to help rebuild schools after the Khmer Rouge. He taught Vietnamese to Khmer children. He trained teachers. He translated documents. He lived in a rented room near the Russian Market. In the evenings, he wrote down what he saw: the ruined buildings, the smell of burnt rubber, the exhumed skulls in the pagodas. And then, in September 1981, he wrote about a woman named Srey Mao, a noodle seller who brought him breakfast. Her husband had died under the Khmer Rouge. She had a daughter, Sophal. She had a way of laughing with her whole body.

Linh’s hands trembled as she turned the pages. The handwriting became more careless, more alive. He described moving into Srey’s wooden house on the outskirts of Phnom Penh, teaching Sophal to read Vietnamese, planting a papaya tree in the yard. When Bopha was born in 1982, he wrote, *Tôi thấy mình là người cha hạnh phúc nhất thế gian.* I feel like the happiest father in the world. He wrote about boiling water, about buying powdered milk, about Srey singing to the baby in the dark.

Then the entries changed. In early 1984, his wife in Mỹ Tho sent word that their daughter, Hồng—Linh’s mother—was very ill and asking for him. There was a gap in the notebook. Then he wrote: *I am a man with two hearts, and both are beating outside my body.*

He decided to return to Vietnam. He told Srey he would come back for her and the girls. He promised. He wrote that he did not know if he believed it.

The last entry was dated May 1984. He was packing his suitcase. Srey had tied a red string around his wrist for protection. The string had worn away years ago.

Beneath the notebook were the letters Srey had sent him after he left. They were written in careful, simple Vietnamese, the handwriting full of loops. Linh read each one twice.

*Anh Tư, Sophal hỏi cha. Em đợi anh. Về với em.* — Brother Tư, Sophal asks for father. I wait for you. Come back to me.

Another, dated two years later: *Bopha bị sốt rét. Xin anh về.* — Bopha has malaria. Please come home.

A letter from 1988 told him that Srey had moved the family to a smaller house near the market because the old one was being demolished. She wrote that she still kept his books. Then a final letter, dated 1991: *Em biết anh không về. Đừng gửi tiền nữa. Chúng em ổn.* — I know you are not coming back. Don’t send money anymore. We are fine.

Linh sat in the quiet house and held the letters in her lap. Her grandfather had kept them all, hidden under the drawers. He had sent money, at least for a while. But he had never gone back. He had never mentioned Srey Mao to his wife, his daughter, his granddaughter. He had carried this silence for thirty-one years.

She felt not anger yet, but a cold, expanding ache, as if a room inside her had been opened and found empty.

Her mother appeared in the doorway. “Linh, are you hungry?”

Linh looked up. She wanted to say something, but the words felt too large. She slipped the letters back into the tin box and closed the lid.

“I’m okay, mẹ. Just tired.”

Her mother looked at the box but did not ask. “Don’t stay up too late. The mosquitoes will eat you alive.”

That night, Linh lay in the narrow bed of her childhood, staring at the ceiling. She could not stop thinking about the two little girls in the photograph. They would be around her mother’s age now. They might still be alive. The photograph had been taken in 1983. Sophal, the older daughter, would be in her forties. Bopha slightly younger. They might have children. They might still live in Phnom Penh.

She did not tell her mother. Not yet. First, she wanted to know if they could be found.

In the morning, she called her editor in Saigon. She said she needed a few more days. She invented a story about clearing documents. Then she took the photograph, the letters, and the address on the back of an old envelope into the city. She scanned them with her phone. The Khmer script she could not read.

Two days later, Linh flew to Phnom Penh.

The city rose out of the heat like a lung exhaling. Everywhere was the smell of river water, diesel, and frying oil. The streets were loud with motorcycles. French colonial buildings stood next to glass towers. Linh checked into a small hotel near the river and hired a translator, a young Cambodian woman named Dara, who had studied Vietnamese at university. Dara listened to the story without judgment.

“You want to find this woman?” she asked.

“Or her daughters,” Linh said. “The address is old. It might not exist.”

They started at the street near Psar Thmei, the Central Market. The number in the letter was gone. In its place stood a shop selling motorbike parts. The owner, a man in a greasy shirt, shook his head. Dara asked the neighbors. No one remembered a Srey Mao. The city had rewritten itself too many times.

They went to the market itself, its art deco dome hazy with heat. Dara led Linh past stalls of silver, silk, and fruit, stopping at a row of food vendors. She showed the photograph to an elderly woman who stacked limes into pyramids. The woman adjusted her glasses and laughed.

“Srey Mao! Where did you get this? She used to sell noodles near the east gate. Very good broth. Number one.” The woman peered at the girls in the photo. “Yes, yes. She had two daughters. Sophal and Bopha. They moved to Kandal province after the mother got sick, maybe fifteen years ago? Wait, more.”

Linh’s heart quickened.

“Do you know where in Kandal?” Dara asked.

The woman shrugged. “Around Takhmao? The older daughter, Sophal, came back to work. She sews. You can find her near the market, I think. She works at a shop called Sokha Tailors.”

“Does she still work there?”

“How would I know? She did five years ago. People come and go.”

Linh thanked her. She and Dara walked through the heavy heat to the west side of the market. They asked at a phone shop. A man pointed them down a narrow alley. The sign said SOKHA TAILORS in both Khmer and English. It was a small open-fronted shop with two old sewing machines and racks of dresses wrapped in plastic.

A woman sat at one machine, feeding a strip of red fabric under the needle. She was in her forties, with high cheekbones and dark eyes. Her hair was tied back. She did not look up when they entered.

Dara spoke in Khmer. “We are looking for Sophal.”

The woman’s hands stopped. She lifted her head. Her eyes went first to Dara, then to Linh. Then to the photograph in Linh’s hand. She did not say anything for a long moment.

“I am Sophal.” Her Vietnamese was rough but clear. She looked at Linh. “You are his granddaughter.”

Linh nodded. “Chào chị. I’m Linh.”

Sophal turned off the sewing machine. She stood, smoothing her skirt. She did not smile, but her face was not unkind. “Come to the back. We can talk.”

The back of the shop was a small room with a plastic table, a rice cooker, and a wall of fabric bolts. Sophal poured three glasses of water from a clay pot. Her hands were steady.

“He died,” Linh said. “My grandfather. Two weeks ago.”

Sophal exhaled, but she didn’t look surprised. “I felt that. Maybe I felt that.” She sat down. “You found something.”

Linh opened her bag and placed the photograph, the notebook, and the letters on the table. Sophal stared at the photograph. She picked it up with both hands, and for the first time her expression broke. Her mouth pressed into a thin line.

“My mother kept one like this,” she said. “She burned most of his letters after I was fifteen. But not this one. This is the only picture of our father.”

“Our father,” Linh repeated, the word strange.

Sophal looked up. “Yes. He was our father.”

She told Linh about life after 1984. Their mother waited, then stopped waiting. She opened the noodle stall again, raised her daughters alone. She never married. Every few months for the first years, money arrived from a man named Mr. Be, a Vietnamese trader. No letter came with it. Then one day, Srey wrote the last letter and told him to stop.

“But you learned Vietnamese,” Linh said.

“He taught us. Before he left. Afterward, my mother made us continue. She said it was not his language, it was ours too.” Sophal’s voice was matter-of-fact. “I don’t remember him much. I was five when he left. But I remember his hands. He used to carve little animals from wood.” She looked toward a shelf. On it stood a small wooden elephant, gray with age.

Linh felt tears behind her eyes. “I didn’t know he had another family. Nobody in Vietnam knew.”

Sophal nodded. “He kept it secret. Maybe not to hurt us. Maybe to protect himself.”

They sat in silence. The noise of the market filtered in from the street.

“My mother died in 2005,” Sophal said. “She asked me to find him, once, before I was married. I sent a letter to the old address in Mỹ Tho. It came back with a red stamp: not deliverable. Maybe he moved. Or maybe he didn’t want to be found.”

Linh thought of the address. Her grandfather had lived in the same house for over fifty years. She did not say this.

Sophal pulled a folded paper from a drawer under the table. It was a copy of a letter, written in Khmer. “This is what she wrote. Not the burning one. She dictated it to me, and I sent it. It came back.”

Dara translated quietly. The letter asked Lê Văn Tư if he was still alive, if he remembered them, if he wanted to see his daughters again. It said Sophal was getting married. It said Bopha had a son. It said, *I do not ask you to stay. Just come and see us once.*

Linh lowered her head. The letter had been returned unread. She imagined the postman pressing a red stamp onto the envelope while her grandfather watered his vegetables a few streets away. She didn’t know if it would have made a difference.

Sophal said, “Maybe he never got it. Or maybe he threw it away. I tell myself the first one.”
