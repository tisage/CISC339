Dandara Bezerra da Costa had been born twelve days after the last human launch from Earth.

Her mother liked to say that the timing was dramatic, but Dandara had never understood why. The engines had already burned. The planet had already shrunk into a blue coin behind the convoy. Whether she had arrived twelve days before or after seemed no more important than whether she had been born before breakfast.

Still, people remembered.

On the Peregrina, every child learned the departure date before multiplication tables. They learned the names of the drowned cities, the ash years, the long failures of the cooling towers. They learned that Earth had not exploded, as the youngest children sometimes imagined, nor had it been swallowed by the Sun. It had simply become a place where the air was too warm, the seas too high, the crops too unreliable, and the storms too clever.

The grown people who had seen it carried that knowledge in their faces.

Dandara’s avó Lídia carried it in a biscuit tin.

“Don’t look at me like that,” Lídia said, catching Dandara’s expression.

They were in Lídia’s narrow cabin near the old hydroponics spine, where the walls sweated faintly and everything smelled of mint roots and warm metal. On the bunk between them sat the tin, painted long ago with yellow flowers. Its edges were rusted, though rust had become a rare and precious thing aboard the Peregrina.

“I’m not looking at you any way,” Dandara said.

“Você has your mother’s eyebrows when you’re lying.”

“I have nobody’s eyebrows. I am an original design.”

Lídia snorted. She had been born in Salvador, on the coast of a country that no longer existed except in maps, recipes, and people’s mouths. She had crossed an ocean as a child, crossed another as a student, and crossed the dark between stars as an old woman. She still wore her white hair twisted in a cloth wrap patterned with tiny blue shells.

She tapped the biscuit tin.

“Take this with you tomorrow.”

Dandara folded her arms. “No.”

“You don’t even know what it is.”

“I know it is something from Earth.”

“It is something that was on Earth.”

“That’s worse.”

Her grandmother’s mouth tightened, but not in anger. Lídia had stopped being easily angered after the Fever Years. “You think the new place will break if it touches an old thing?”

“No. I think the people responsible for microbial safety will break me if I carry unauthorized cargo onto the first landing shuttle.”

“It’s sterile.”

“Everything old is supposedly sterile until somebody discovers mold in the ventilation ducts.”

Lídia sighed and lifted the tin’s lid.

Inside was a little clay bird, no longer than Dandara’s thumb. Its wings were spread in a clumsy V. One of them had a chip at the tip. The clay was a reddish brown, darker than any of the manufactured ceramics aboard the ship.

“It was made from the ground near Itapuã,” Lídia said. “Before I left. My sister gave it to me.”

Dandara looked at it despite herself.

“Is it supposed to be an andorinha?”

“Supposed to be. My sister was not a great sculptor.”

“It looks like a potato trying to escape.”

“Exactly like her.”

Dandara smiled. She could not help it.

Outside the cabin, the Peregrina gave one of its deep, structural groans. The ship had been turning for three days, slowly presenting its broad magnetic shield toward Candeia, the planet that had occupied every screen, every conversation, and every prayer for the last nine months.

It was larger than Earth, though only by a little. Its oceans were black-blue under the dense clouds. Its continents shone pale gold where the surveys had found mineral flats and low forests of something that resembled neither trees nor coral, though the botanists argued constantly about both comparisons.

Tomorrow Dandara would go down to it.

Not to Earth. Not to some recreated beach in the ship’s memory galleries, with false wind blowing through false palm leaves. A real place, under a real open sky.

She ought to have been excited.

Instead, she kept thinking of the tin.

“I’ll leave it here,” she said.

Lídia closed the lid. “All right.”

The quickness of her surrender made Dandara suspicious.

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“Avó.”

“What?”

“You want me to take it.”

“I want many things. I would like a proper cup of coffee. I would like to complain about the bus being late. I would like my knees to work without making little firecrackers every time I stand. We do not receive everything we want.”

Dandara looked away.

Lídia reached across the bunk and took her hand. Her skin had become translucent in places, the veins blue and delicate beneath it.

“Dandara,” she said. “When it rains there, let it get wet.”

“Get what wet?”

“The bird.”

“That’s why you want me to take it?”

“Yes.”

“That is the stupidest reason anyone has ever given me for anything.”

“Then you have been lucky in your reasons.”

Dandara did not take the tin when she left. But she thought of the small clay bird all night.

In the morning, the launch bay smelled of disinfectant, sealant, and the sour fear of people trying not to show fear.

Commander Imani Okafor stood beside the lander’s open hatch, reading names from her wrist display. There were six official members of the First Survey: Imani, two atmosphere technicians, a medic, a systems engineer, and Dandara, who had been assigned to deploy the weather mast and calibrate the surface relay.

Dandara had spent her childhood climbing maintenance shafts and following her mother through the algae gardens. Her mother, Joana, said that Dandara could repair anything so long as it had a panel to remove and a reason to be rude. That, apparently, qualified her to help establish humanity’s first foothold on Candeia.

As she reached the hatch, someone called her name.

Her mother stood behind the safety line with the other families. Joana da Costa had not slept; Dandara could see it in the bruised shadows beneath her eyes. She held herself very still, as if any unnecessary movement might shake the lander apart before it launched.

“Your helmet seal,” Joana said.

“It’s checked.”

“Check it again.”

“Mãe—”

“Again.”

Dandara checked it. Her mother nodded once.

Then she held out the biscuit tin.

Dandara stared at it.

“Avó said you might change your mind,” Joana said.

“You’re helping her smuggle contraband now?”

“Your avó raised me. I have survived worse things than a little contraband.”

“Is it really sterile?”

“Sterilized before launch. Scanned every year after. I read the reports myself.”

Dandara took the tin. It felt absurdly heavy.

Her mother kissed two fingers and pressed them to Dandara’s forehead, a gesture from a place Dandara knew only through old recordings.

“Volta inteira,” Joana said. Come back whole.

Dandara tucked the tin into the personal storage pouch inside her suit bag.

The lander dropped away from the Peregrina twelve minutes later.

For the first time in her life, Dandara saw the whole ship from outside.

The Peregrina had always been a world to her: corridors, gardens, classrooms, repair decks, sleeping quarters, spinning habitats. From the lander’s window it looked thin and fragile, a silver ring against blackness, with the long engine spine trailing behind it like a needle.

Beyond it hung Candeia.

The planet filled the forward view.

Nobody spoke for several minutes.

The clouds had breaks in them now. Through one, Dandara saw a broad sweep of dark water and a coast the color of rust. Something green-black spread across the inland hills in branching patterns. The sunrise caught the upper atmosphere and made a bright copper line around the curve of the world.

“It looks wet,” said Suresh, one of the atmosphere technicians.

“It is wet,” said Imani.

“I know. I mean—it looks wet.”

Imani did not answer. Her eyes were fixed on the view.

Dandara thought of Lídia’s bird.

The descent became noisy. The lander shuddered as it met the upper atmosphere. Pale fire crawled across the forward shields. Dandara gripped the straps across her chest and felt the return of weight, first gentle, then insistent.

The others had trained for this in centrifuge chambers, but no training could quite prepare the body for gravity that had not been measured against metal walls and spin.

Candeia pulled at her bones.

The landing site lay in a broad basin between low, ridged hills. Survey images had shown the ground as dry stone with a shallow freshwater reservoir three kilometers east. The weather mast would collect data for six months before the larger settlement modules descended.

The lander touched down hard enough to snap everyone’s heads forward.

For a moment there was only the ticking of cooling metal.

Then Commander Imani laughed once—short, disbelieving—and said, “We are down.”

The first steps were assigned by lottery, because no committee could agree on whose nationality, profession, age, or ancestor deserved the honor. The system had been announced as fair and denounced as insulting by almost everyone.

Dandara had not entered.

Imani went first.

Dandara watched from the airlock camera as the commander descended the ladder onto rust-colored ground. Her boot struck the surface and sank a few centimeters in dust.

“No collapse,” Imani reported. “Surface stable.”

Behind her, the horizon curved only slightly. The sky was not blue. It was a deep gray-green, bright near the sun and bruised purple toward the distant hills. Broad clouds moved overhead in slow, layered sheets.

Dandara stepped down second.

The ground gave beneath her boot with a soft crunch.

That was all.

No choir. No recorded speech. No sudden understanding. Just the pressure of her own weight, the hard slap of wind against her suit, and the fact that the world did not stop where the lander’s walls stopped.

She looked up.

The sky was too large. It made her dizzy.

“Dandara?” Imani said over the comm. “You all right?”

“Yes.”

“You’re standing still.”

“I’m looking.”

“We have a schedule.”

Dandara began to laugh, then caught herself. “Sorry, Commander.”

“Don’t apologize. Move.”

They worked for four hours.

The weather mast came apart in sections: tripod legs, sensor crown, lightning rod, communications dish. Dandara and Suresh carried the components across the basin while Imani marked each step with a beacon. The gravity was close enough to ship-standard that Dandara did not need to relearn her body, but every movement had a strange finality. There was no curved corridor ahead, no handhold within reach, no familiar hum beneath the floor.

When she looked back, the lander was small against the plain.

The thought made her mouth go dry.

At the end of the fourth hour, Dandara returned to the lander for a replacement coupling. She found the inner storage compartment open.

At first she assumed Suresh had been looking for tools. Then she saw the discarded thermal blanket on the floor.

A hand moved beneath it.

Dandara froze.

“Who’s there?”

The blanket shifted.

Tiago emerged, pale and cramped, his dark hair flattened against his forehead. He was sixteen and had the guilty expression of someone who had planned an excellent crime and forgotten the part where other people got angry.

For a second, Dandara could not speak.

Then she said, very softly, “I am going to throw you out of an airlock.”

“Please don’t shout,” Tiago said. “The commander will hear.”

“The commander is going to hear.”

“I just wanted to see it.”

“You hid in a descent lander.”

“I know.”

“You could have died.”

“I didn’t.”

“You may still.”

He sat up, wincing as he unfolded himself from behind the supply crates. He wore a youth-training undersuit, no helmet, no proper boots. His emergency oxygen mask lay beside him.

Dandara looked at the equipment list on the wall. There were no spare surface suits.

“How did you get past boarding?”

“Mateus was watching the cargo list.”

“Mateus is your friend.”

“He owes me.”

“Mateus is going to spend the next ten years scraping algae tanks.”

Tiago swallowed. “Are you going to send me back?”

“There is no sending you back. The lander does not return until the survey is done. You are here, you idiot.”

His face changed then. The bravado drained out of it, and Dandara saw her brother as he really was: a boy born under artificial lights, who had spent his whole life hearing adults say that Earth was lost and Candeia was salvation, as if either word could explain anything.

He held up something clenched in his fist.

The biscuit tin.

“You took that too?” Dandara said.

“Avó gave it to me.”

“She gave it to me.”

“She said you’d be too careful.”

“That sounds like her.”

Tiago looked toward the hatch, where the outer airlock indicator glowed green. “I want to put it somewhere.”

“No.”

“It’s just clay.”

“It is not just clay. It is from Earth.”

“That’s why.”

Dandara laughed without humor. “You don’t know Earth.”

“Neither do you.”

The words landed harder than she expected.

Outside, Imani’s voice crackled through the comm. “Dandara, status?”

Dandara stared at Tiago.

“Coupling is damaged,” she said. “I’m replacing it.”

“Two minutes.”

“Understood.”

Tiago’s fingers tightened around the tin. “Please.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because this place did not ask for our old things.”

He looked at her with a kind of fury. “You say that like you don’t care.”

“I care.”

“You don’t. You hate the archive nights. You hate when Avó tells stories. You hate that everybody acts like Earth is a person who died and wants us to feel bad forever.”

Dandara opened her mouth, then closed it.

He went on, quieter now. “I don’t want to plant it. I just want it to be here. I want one thing that was there to touch something that isn’t metal.”

The airlock warning chimed.

Dandara grabbed the tin from his hand.

“You stay in this compartment,” she said. “You do not touch anything. You do not open that hatch. If you move, I will tell Commander Imani exactly where you came from and let her decide whether to send you back in a cargo restraint.”

Tiago looked as if he might argue.

“Stay,” Dandara said.

She sealed the tin in the equipment locker and went back outside.

The weather mast was nearly assembled when the wind changed.

It had been moving steadily from the west, a low pressure against Dandara’s suit. Now it fell away. The silence that followed was so complete she could hear her own breathing.

Suresh lifted his helmeted head toward the hills.

“Commander,” he said, “the pressure is dropping.”

Imani checked her display. “How fast?”

“Faster than forecast.”

The clouds above the western ridge had thickened into a wall.

“Secure the mast,” Imani said. “We return to the lander.”

They moved quickly. The sensor crown locked into place with a satisfying click. Dandara drove the final anchor spike down with both hands.

Then the wind came back.

It hit the basin in a broad, roaring sheet. Dust tore across the ground in rust-red streamers. The lander vanished behind it for two seconds, then reappeared.

“Go!” Imani shouted.

Dandara turned toward the lander.

A white shape moved at the open outer hatch.

For a moment she thought it was a loose thermal cover.

Then she saw Tiago.

He had pulled on an emergency pressure shell, too large for him, with the helmet crooked over his shoulders. The suit’s yellow safety tether trailed behind him as he stumbled away from the lander.

In one hand he held the biscuit tin.

“Tiago!” Dandara shouted.

He either could not hear her over the wind or pretended not to.

He was headed toward a shallow depression beyond the lander, where the ground had cracked into dark plates. The rain began as he ran.

Not water at first. Hard, clear pellets that bounced off Dandara’s visor and hissed on the warm hull of the lander.

Tiago slipped. The tether snapped taut behind him, then dragged free from the hatch ring.

“Dandara!” Imani barked. “Do not pursue him.”

But Dandara was already running.

The storm flattened the world. Dust, ice, and rain swarmed against her visor. Tiago was a blurred yellow shape ahead, lurching through the darkening basin.

“Stop!” she yelled. “Your suit is not rated for this!”

He reached the depression.

The plates beneath his feet broke.

Tiago vanished to his waist in black mud.

Dandara dropped to her knees at the edge. The surface was not stone at all but a thin crust over saturated mineral silt. Tiago thrashed, trying to pull one leg free, and sank deeper.

“Don’t move!” she said.

“I can’t—”

“Don’t move.”

The wind tore his words apart. His helmet seal indicator flashed amber.

Dandara unclipped her own safety line from her belt and anchored it around one of the weather mast’s unused ground spikes. She crawled forward on her stomach, distributing her weight, the mud sucking at her gloves.

Tiago held the tin above his head.

Even then, he was holding it.

“You unbelievable idiot,” Dandara said.

“I’m sorry.”

“Good.”

She reached him. The ground cracked beneath her left elbow. She caught his suit harness and pulled, bracing her boots against a ridge of harder earth.

For a terrifying second, nothing moved.

Then Tiago’s leg came free with a wet tearing sound. He lurched forward, knocking them both onto solid ground.

The biscuit tin flew from his hand.

It struck a rock. The lid sprang open.

The clay bird tumbled into the rain.

Dandara lunged for it.

The rain had softened the dust coating its little wings. Red-brown water ran down its sides, pooling in the hollow of one palm. The bird did not dissolve. It only darkened, becoming almost the color of blood.

Tiago crawled beside her, coughing hard inside his helmet.

“It’s ruined,” he said.

“No,” Dandara said.

The storm passed as abruptly as it had come.

The clouds tore open eastward. A thin blade of sunlight struck the basin, turning every wet surface bright. Water ran in small silver threads across the rust-colored ground toward the shallow depression where Tiago had nearly disappeared.

For the first time, Dandara saw Candeia’s rain collecting in a place that was not a pipe, a tank, or a recycled tray.

She held the bird in her glove.

On one wing, beneath the washed dust, was an uneven scratch she had not seen before. Tiny letters, nearly worn away.

NÃO PLANTE. MOLHE.

Do not plant. Wet it.

Tiago saw it over her wrist display.

“Avó knew,” he said.

“Of course she knew.”

Back at the lander, Commander Imani was waiting in the airlock.

She did not shout. That was worse.

Tiago stood before her in borrowed ship clothes, shivering and muddy. Dandara stood beside him, holding the clean, damp biscuit tin.

Imani listened to the account without interrupting. When they finished, she looked at Tiago for a long time.

“You will be confined for the remainder of the mission,” she said. “When we return to the Peregrina, you will face the council.”

“Yes, Commander,” Tiago whispered.

Then Imani turned to Dandara.

“And you.”

Dandara waited.

“You disobeyed a direct order.”

“Yes, Commander.”

“You also prevented your brother from dying on my landing site.”

“Yes, Commander.”

Imani’s expression did not change. “Next time, try to arrange those things separately.”

Dandara nodded.

Imani glanced at the biscuit tin. “Was the artifact compromised?”

“No. It was sterilized. It got wet.”

“I see.”

For the first time, the commander’s mouth twitched.

“Put it away,” she said. “We have work.”

By the time the survey team returned to the Peregrina, the weather mast had sent its first full report: breathable-adjacent atmosphere, manageable pathogens, seasonal electrical storms, abundant freshwater in the basin and its surrounding channels.

Candeia would not be easy. Nobody had expected easy.

At the docking bay, the families waited behind the safety line.

Joana reached Dandara first, held her face between both hands, and inspected her as if searching for cracks. Then she embraced Tiago so fiercely that he made a wounded sound.

Lídia stood a little apart in her blue shell-patterned wrap.

Dandara walked to her and held out the biscuit tin.

“I got it wet,” she said.

Lídia opened the lid.

The clay bird lay inside on a piece of clean cloth, its little chipped wing shining dark red in the bay lights.

For a moment Lídia did not speak.

Then she touched it with one fingertip and said, “Bom.”

“That’s all?”

“What else should I say?”

“Maybe that you knew Tiago would steal it.”

“I did not know. I suspected. He has your mother’s eyebrows.”

“He has nobody’s eyebrows,” Dandara said automatically.

Lídia smiled.

Behind them, through the observation wall, Candeia turned beneath the ship: clouded, green-gray, immense. Dandara imagined the place where they had landed, the weather mast standing alone in the wide basin, its thin antenna pointed up at the alien sky.

“Avó,” she said, “what was Earth really like?”

Lídia looked through the glass.

For once, she did not answer immediately.

“It was too many things,” she said at last. “That was its problem. That was its beauty.”

Dandara stood beside her, holding the rail.

Below, a storm moved across the dark sea, flashing silently inside the clouds.
