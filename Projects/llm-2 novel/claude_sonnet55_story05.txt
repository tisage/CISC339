Bà Năm Thục pulled a live eel out of a durian in front of forty tourists, and Diệp, standing three steps behind with the tray, felt the same small glow of pride she felt every time. The eel was a trick. It lived in the false bottom of the tray, and Diệp had spent eight months learning to pass it from tray to fruit while the old woman's hands made large, easy gestures in the air. But the crowd gasped as if it were not a trick, and the Cái Bè floating market went on bobbing around them, hulls knocking, the pineapple seller shouting prices from her boat.

"Again tomorrow," Bà Năm said afterward, counting coins into a biscuit tin. She was small and dried-looking, with a gray braid she wore wrapped around her head like a rope on a bollard. "You rushed the eel. You always rush when there are foreigners. They aren't smarter than the locals. They just have better cameras."

"Dạ, Bà Năm."

"And stop calling me that when it's only us. Call me Cô."

"You told me Cô was for women who still had husbands."

"Did I?" Bà Năm frowned at the tin. "Well. Call me what you like."

Diệp was sixteen and had lived on the old woman's boat-house for two years. Her father had been a ferryman on the Tiền River, and one wet October he had gone out in the dark to pull a stranded motorbike off a sandbar and had not come back. The river kept him three nights. Diệp had sat on the landing with a bowl of rice and three sticks of incense, and she had told no one that she was going to stay there until it gave him back or took her too. On the third night, Bà Năm, whom Diệp knew only as the woman who did tricks at the market, came down the bank in her sandals and said, "Move over, child." She sat beside her and said nothing for an hour. Then she stood and walked into the water up to her waist, and she spoke to it. Diệp could not afterward say what language. A few minutes later her father's flat-bottomed boat came drifting out of the reeds against the current, with him lying in the bottom of it, whole and cold and decently on his back, as if he had lain down for a nap.

That was how Diệp had been able to bury him, and how she had come to have a teacher.

---

For a long time she thought the magic was the tricks. Then, in her second year, she began to understand that the tricks were what the old woman did so that the other thing could go unseen. The other thing was rare. In the dry season Ông Bảy's orchard at Phú Phụng went three weeks with no rain and his rambutan trees began to drop their fruit, and Bà Năm went out on his land at dusk with a bowl of water and a candle stub and was gone four hours. The next morning rain came and fell on exactly that orchard and a hundred meters around it, and the neighbors came out to stand at the edge of the wet, looking uneasy. Another time a baby had stopped breathing in the night in a house two canals over, and Bà Năm went in and shut the door, and what Diệp heard through the wall was not chanting but something closer to bargaining, low and reasonable. The baby cried.

Each time, afterward, Bà Năm slept for most of a day. Each time, she got up a little different.

Diệp noticed it first the way you notice a stair has been moved. After the rain, Bà Năm stood in the kitchen with a pan of bánh xèo and said, "Pass me the, the thing, the yellow flower," and Diệp, bewildered, handed her a bunch of chives. "No," Bà Năm said, "the one for the wrapping," and Diệp gave her mustard greens, and the old woman nodded and laughed it off. But there was a word she'd lost. It was a ridiculous word, nothing important. She didn't get it back for a month.

After the baby, a woman who sold lotus tea at the landing came every Thursday as she had come every Thursday for years, and Bà Năm looked up from her crossword and said, courteous as a stranger, "Good morning, chị. Is there something I can help with?" The woman laughed uncertainly. She was Bà Năm's sister-in-law.

"She's tired," Diệp told the woman afterward on the landing. "She had a long night."

"Tired," the woman said, and looked at Diệp strangely, and did not come the next Thursday.

Diệp began to keep a notebook. She did not let herself ask why. She wrote the dates of the workings on the left side of the page and on the right she wrote what she saw. *Word for the herb. Sister-in-law (Dì Mười). Name of the street where she grew up; asked me twice.* The right side grew longer than the left.

---

It was on the death anniversary of Bà Năm's husband that Diệp saw it plainly.

They kept the altar in the front room, a high shelf with a brass incense bowl, a plate of oranges, and a row of photographs in black frames. Bà Năm went through the ritual that morning briskly: matches, three sticks, a bow, a murmured word. She cooked canh chua and braised fish, his favorites, though Diệp noticed she had to ask where the tamarind was kept, a thing she had done ten thousand times. And when the incense was lit she stood before the photographs with her hands folded and Diệp stood beside her.

"Who's this one?" Bà Năm said. She was looking at the third frame from the left, a boy of about twenty in a pressed shirt, squinting in the sun, with ears that stuck out slightly.

Diệp's mouth went dry. "Cô," she said. "That's Tâm. That's your son."

Bà Năm looked at the photograph with attention. It was the attention of someone looking at a pleasant landscape. "He has a kind face," she said. "He went by boat, didn't he? In the bad years. I have some memory of having been told." She put out one finger and straightened the frame, which hadn't been crooked. "Well. We'll give him some fish, too."

She went to the kitchen. Diệp stood alone in front of the shelf and counted the frames. Eleven. And she began to think about what she knew of the dead: that her own father in the ground at the Phú Túc cemetery was held up there, in the red-clay dark, by something. By his name being said at the right time of year. By his daughter lighting the incense. By anybody at all remembering the exact sound of his laugh. That the dead had no other place to live.

She thought of the night the river returned him.

---

The camphor trunk at the foot of Bà Năm's bed was not locked. Diệp had always supposed it held blankets. She waited until the old woman went to the market, and she knelt, and under two quilts and a folded áo dài of a green silk gone yellow at the creases she found a ledger. It was an account book of the cheap kind, with a red cloth spine. The first pages were in a hand that was not Bà Năm's, a sharp and sloping hand, and the ink there had gone brown. On the first page, in that hand, was written:

*What the art takes it takes from what you are holding for the dead. It cannot be paid from your own life; your own life is not enough. It is paid from the ones who have no one else but you. When you spend them you do not grieve them, because the grief goes too. This is why it is called an art, and not a gift. Write the name before you spend it, so you will know afterward what you have done.*

Underneath, in the old woman's handwriting, a list. It was long. Diệp ran her finger down it. *Má. Chị Hai. Anh Ba Quang. Cô Út Lan, who fed me. The dog.* The dog. *The schoolteacher at Mỹ Tho.* Names, and beside each name a date and a few words, like receipts. *Rain, Phú Phụng. A child at Ấp Ba, breathing.* Twenty, thirty lines. Toward the bottom of the page, the writing became less steady, and then she saw a line she knew she would see:

*Tâm. My son. The river, for Diệp's father. 14 Oct.*

Diệp sat on the floor with the book on her knees. The boat-house creaked on its moorings. Somewhere outside a woman was calling to a child for lunch.

Under Tâm's name, one line further down, it said: *Anh Lượng.* Her husband. There was no date beside that one, and no purpose. The line had been written, and she had not yet spent it. It was waiting.

---

She waited until after dinner to say it. They ate on the deck, the lamp swinging a little, the river going dark and wide beyond the railing.

"I read the book," Diệp said.

Bà Năm put down her chopsticks and looked at her without any surprise. "Ah."

"You spent your son for my father."

"I did."

"You never said."

"No." The old woman picked a bone out of her fish with great care. "You were fourteen, and you were on a landing in the rain waiting to drown yourself. If I had said, I will give you your father but it will cost me my boy, what would you have said?"

Diệp's throat closed. "I'd have said no."

"Yes. You would have been right to. And then you would have drowned, or lived a little and been angry all your life at a river." Bà Năm shrugged, and the shrug was the terrible part. It was not brave. It was not even sad. "I look at it now and it seems a sensible exchange. I cannot tell you why. I look at that photograph and I feel nothing but a sort of politeness. I know there was something, because I wrote it down. But there's nothing in the place where it should be. It is like being told you once owned a house."

"That isn't *right.*" Diệp was crying now, and furious that she was. "He was your son, he was — and you're sitting here telling me it was a good price —"

"I'm telling you it was mine to spend." For the first time her voice sharpened. "And I'm telling you also that it was a mistake not to tell you the cost of the art before I taught you the art. That much I do know. I think the knowing remained in me because it was a different kind of knowing. I should have shown you the book in your first week. I was afraid you'd leave, and I —" She stopped. "I had no one else I was holding anything for. You understand? I looked at the list one day and I thought, who will hold the things I am holding, when I go. It's not a fine reason. But I think that is the reason."

"What happens when you run out?"

"I start on the living," Bà Năm said. "It can be done. Less force in it, and the person stands before you afterward not knowing who they are to you. I'd prefer not to." She looked past Diệp to the water. "But the dead do run short."

---

The salt came early that year.

Everyone in the delta knew about the salt. In the dry months the sea pushed up the river mouths farther each year, and the farmers fought it with sluice gates and earth dams and prayers. That March it came up the Hàm Luông channel two weeks before the usual date, and the gate at Ấp Tân Thạnh failed in the night with a groan that people heard from three villages away. By morning the canals feeding four hundred households' orchards and rice seedlings were running brackish. A man tasted his canal and spat. A woman wept on her own doorstep. By noon half the village had come to the boat-house.

They didn't say what they wanted. They stood on the bank with their hats in their hands, which was how Diệp knew. Ông Bảy was there. The lotus-tea woman, whom Bà Năm did not know, was there too, though she stood at the back.

"It'll be tonight," Bà Năm said, "when the tide turns." And she went inside to sleep.

Diệp followed her to the bedroom door. The old woman lay on her back with her braid unwound, looking old. The camphor trunk was open beside the bed, the ledger on the quilt.

"Which?" Diệp said.

"For a wall of water that size?" Bà Năm didn't open her eyes. "Anh Lượng, I think. Perhaps one more." She said it as if reading a grocery list. "He was a good man, I'm told. I keep his photograph polished."

"And after him?"

"After him I have my mother, and the sister, and I think I will be alright." But her mouth had a pinched look, and Diệp, who had now watched her for two years, understood that the old woman was lying, and that after Anh Lượng there would be almost no one left, and that one more working, and one after that, would take her to the living. To the lotus-tea woman. To the girl on the deck.

Diệp went out and stood in the sun on the landing for a long time.

---

She had watched the rite four times. She had not been taught it, because Bà Năm had been delaying that, and now Diệp understood why. But she had watched with the greedy patience of the apprentice who knows she is meant to be stealing. A bowl of river water. A candle stub. The name said three times, once to the water, once to the flame, once to the ground. Then the asking. She did not know how you made the asking. It had looked to her like a kind of listening.

At dusk, while Bà Năm slept, she took the bowl and the candle and the ledger and she walked the dike path alone to Ấp Tân Thạnh. The failed gate stood in the dark, a concrete mouth with the sea leaning on it. She could smell the salt and feel it in her teeth. The water had a push to it, a patient shoulder.

She knelt at the edge, and she opened the ledger to the first clean line, and she thought about whom she could spend.

Her father, no. She would not. The ground at Phú Túc had given her nothing to hold but him, and she held him daily: his ginger-colored tea, the dent in his thumbnail, the wrong way he whistled. Her grandmother, who had died when she was nine, a dry gentle woman with a smell of betel — no. But there was her mother. Her mother had died when Diệp was three. Diệp had almost nothing of her. A name on an altar, Lê Thị Hồng. A photograph in which she was not smiling. People said she'd had a lovely voice.

It was a thrifty calculation and she knew it. She told herself that you could not lose much of what you barely had. She wrote the name in the ledger with her pen in the failing light, *Lê Thị Hồng, má,* and beside it *Tân Thạnh, the salt,* and her hand shook. Then she poured the water, and lit the candle, and said the name once to the water, once to the flame, once to the ground.

And then, because she did not know how else, she asked.

It wasn't a spell. It was more like turning in a crowded house toward one particular voice. She said, *I am holding something for her. I'll give you what I'm holding. Hold the sea back.* And it answered. That was what astonished her: something with enormous slow attention turned, and considered her, and weighed what she was offering. For a moment she felt herself weighed as plainly as fish on a scale.

*Not enough,* it said, or the idea of it came to her. *This is thin.*

"It's all I have of her," Diệp said aloud. She was crying again. "It's very small."

*Then it is small.* The attention seemed almost kind. *Show me.*

And she understood what it wanted. It didn't want a name. It wanted whatever she actually had, held out where it could see. So she reached for it. She went into the dark room at the back of herself and found the whole of her mother that she possessed, which was one memory. She had never known she had it. She was perhaps three, on a rattan mat on a dirt floor, with rain on the roof, and a woman was pounding rice in a mortar with a long wooden pestle, thump and rest, thump and rest, and humming. The woman's back was to her. The humming had four notes and a turn at the end. The child watched the woman's shoulder blades move under a blue shirt. That was all. That was the entire thing.

She had thought it nothing. Held in her hands it was the one thing in the world.

*Thank you,* said the attention, and took it.

The sea stopped.

She felt it as a sound ceasing. The groaning of the dead gate went silent, and the water in the canal, which had been sliding inland in the dark with a steady hiss, slowed, and hung, and began for the first time in two days to run seaward, out to the mouth of the river, with the tide. Far down the dike, lanterns were coming. People had seen the canal turn.

Diệp knelt with the ledger in her lap. She was very tired. She read what she had written on the page, *Lê Thị Hồng, má,* and it was a name. She waited for something to happen when she read it. Nothing did. It was like reading the name of a town on a map.

---

She woke on the boat-house deck in the morning with the sun on her face, and someone had put a quilt over her. Her notebook lay open beside her, and on the last page in her own handwriting, which she did not remember writing, it said:

*Your mother's name was Lê Thị Hồng. She died when you were three. You spent her at Tân Thạnh for the salt. What you gave was a memory of her pounding rice and humming. You don't remember it now. That is what the gap is. It is not an illness. Eat something.*

She read it three times. She found she was not afraid, and understood that this was a symptom. She looked at the sky for a while.

When she came inside, Bà Năm was at the stove. She did not turn around. "I have been up since five," she said, "reading your notebook, which I hope you will forgive, and the ledger, which you left on the dike path, and which Ông Bảy's boy brought me with his hat in his hands like a boy returning a stolen cake." She turned the fish. "Anh Lượng is not in the book as spent. I want you to know I was angry."

"I'm sorry."

"You are not. And I am not angry. I thought I would be and I can't find it. That's a funny thing, to look for a thing in yourself and have it be gone." She put down the spatula. Her face worked briefly. "I did not teach you well. I taught you everything but the one thing, and you learned it anyway, and now it is in you, and I cannot take it out. Whatever you are now, I did that."

"You didn't pay for it," Diệp said. "I did."

"No," Bà Năm said, "I think we both did. Sit. Eat."

They ate in silence. The river outside was bright and ordinary. Along the bank, men were already out with shovels, shoring up the gate, and someone had a radio on, playing a song with a lot of brass.

After the meal, Bà Năm took the ledger from the shelf and sat with her pen over a clean page. "Dictate the line to me," she said. "How you want it written."

"I don't know what to say."

"Nobody does." The old woman wrote the date, and then, slowly, the name, and then beneath it: *Hummed while pounding rice. The salt.* She looked at what she'd written. "I will tell you something I have found. I don't feel Tâm. But I have the book, and I know he had ears that stuck out, because the photograph says so, and I know he left by boat, because you told me. I have been collecting him. Slowly. From you, from the lotus-tea woman, from the page. I think a person can be *rebuilt,* a little, out of what other people carry. It isn't the same. It is something."

"Is it enough?"

"For me, to know a thing is a thing I once had, and had well? It is what I have." She closed the book. "Tell me about your mother."

"I don't have anything."

"Then tell me what the notebook says, and what the neighbors say, and we'll begin there."

---

They did the death anniversary for Lê Thị Hồng in June, the way you do. Diệp found out the date from the old registry at the commune hall. She and Bà Năm cooked what the neighbors said Hồng had loved: sticky rice with mung beans, a dish of water spinach with fermented tofu. Dì Mười, the lotus-tea woman, who had come back in spring and been introduced to Bà Năm again, with great gentleness, as though for the first time, came by with a basket of rambutans and stayed to help. She told Diệp her mother had been a terrible cook but a wonderful singer. She said it quickly, looking away, as if it might hurt. It did not hurt. Diệp thanked her and wrote it in the notebook.

In the evening, when the others had gone home, Diệp stood at the altar with a stick of incense and a photograph of a woman who was not smiling. She bowed three times. She said the words that she had been taught to say. She did not feel the thing she had been told she should feel, and she stood a little longer, in case.

Then she carried the mortar out to the back deck, because the rice for tomorrow wanted pounding. She stood the pestle in it and lifted it and let it fall, thump, and rested, and lifted it again. Evening was coming down gold on the water. Somewhere in the pounding, she realized that she was humming. Four notes and a turn at the end. She had not decided to. It came up out of her chest the way breath comes, with no one's permission.

She stopped. The back of her neck prickled.

Behind her, the screen door creaked, and Bà Năm came out and settled onto the step with her tea. She sat for a while looking at the river. "What's that tune?" she said at last, mildly. "It's pretty."

Diệp stood with the pestle in her hands. Neither of them knew. The river went on past the boat-house, brown and wide and carrying the light out toward the sea. After a time she lifted the pestle again, and let it fall, and began once more to hum, and the old woman, who did not know the song either, tilted her head, and listened as though trying to learn it.
