The bottle rode in the passenger footwell wrapped in a page of last week’s Kathimerini, and every time Thanasis braked it rolled against the seat rail and he reached down with his left hand to steady it, eyes still on the road. His uncle Kostas made the tsipouro in November in the yard behind the house in Kato Achaia, with a copper still that was older than the two of them put together, and he filled whatever bottles came to hand and wrote on the label in ballpoint. This one said ΤΣΙΠΟΥΡΟ 23 — ΚΩΣΤΑΣ, the letters pressing through the paper. Thanasis had wiped the glass down with a rag and put it in the newspaper like something that had been paid for.

Going up Kifisias the light changed. In Nea Ionia the apartment blocks stood shoulder to shoulder with laundry on every balcony and men playing cards on the pavement outside the kafeneio with their chairs tipped back. Here, past the turn at Drosia, the walls got lower and the trees got older and the houses sat back behind hedges as if they had all day. He drove slower than he needed to. He had put on the ironed shirt his mother had left hanging on the door of his apartment, the one shirt that had survived his thirties, and he had shaved with a razor that was past done, and he had nicked the side of his neck below the jaw and dabbed it with a bit of toilet paper, which he had remembered to take off. He was aware of the iron smell of the shirt and of the fact that he did not have a jacket, and that everybody at this table would have a jacket.

Marilena was standing in the gateway when he pulled up, the black iron gate with the pattern of spears, and she was wearing a wine-coloured dress and holding her phone and she waved at him with the hand that had the phone in it. She had the kind of face that worked in any room, dark eyebrows, a smile that arrived late and stayed. He cut the engine.

“You found it,” she said.

“It’s the third time I’ve come.”

“I know. It’s a maze.” She bent and looked through the window at the passenger seat. “What’s this?”

“Tsipouro. From my uncle.”

“You brought tsipouro.”

“We drink it at Easter.”

“No, it’s nice,” she said, and he was already reaching down for it, so he could not see her face while she said it. “It’s very nice. Come in, everyone’s in the yard.”

He put the bottle under his arm like a football and followed her in. The garden was a real one, not a planted strip: a fig, two lemon trees, a bougainvillea that had been trained along the back wall for years and had made it partway up the second floor, and an almond tree in the corner that had already finished flowering. The lawn had been cut that morning; there were clippings still stuck to the wheels of the mower by the toolshed. Under the pergola the long table was laid with a white cloth and the good glasses, not the everyday ones, and on the concrete apron beside the house the lamb was on the spit, and Yiorgos Sarris was standing over it in a polo shirt with a glass of white wine in one hand and a long fork in the other, doing nothing at all to the lamb, which was exactly the way it should be done.

“Thanasi!” he called out. “Come, come. Look at this animal.”

They shook hands. Yiorgos had the grip of a man who shook a lot of hands, warm and brief and with a second hand on the forearm. Thanasis handed Marilena the bottle; she took it inside. The lamb was turning slowly, cracked over the coals, dark already at the shoulders.

“Kokoretsi’s already gone,” Yiorgos said, with the grief of a man who had eaten most of it. “You’re late.”

“I’m on time. I’m early.”

“You’re late for the kokoretsi. That’s the only time we keep here.”

Marilena’s mother came out of the kitchen door then with a towel over her shoulder. Kalliopi Sarri had been tall and had learned to stand as though she still were; she had short white hair and good earrings and she held her hands out to him from three steps away.

“Thanasaki. How are you, my child. You look thin.”

“I eat, Kyria Kalliopi.”

“Everybody eats. That’s not the same thing.” She looked at his neck, at the small nick, for a moment too long, and then she let go of him. “Go, sit. Nikos is inside with the baby.”

The baby was not a baby. She was three, and her name was Anna, and she was sitting on the tiled floor of the living room with a wooden jigsaw while Nikos, in a linen shirt, drank coffee and read something on his phone. Daphne, very pregnant, lay along the sofa with her feet on a cushion.

“Roussos,” Nikos said, standing up.

“Sarris.”

They shook hands. There was the business of the jigsaw, the greetings to Daphne, the correct remark about the pregnancy, all of which Thanasis accomplished. He sat down in the chair that faced the garden door so that he could see the spit and the table and the line of the hedge, and Anna brought him a piece of her jigsaw and he took it, and it was the horse’s face, and he put it in his pocket and she did not notice.

“How’s the marble?” Nikos said.

“The marble is fine.”

“What does that mean, the marble is fine?”

“It means the marble doesn’t care about anything.”

Nikos laughed. He worked in shipping, in a family firm that had been passed through his mother’s side, and he was, Thanasis had to admit, generous about it; he was not one of those men who pretend their father’s connections were their own. He asked questions and listened to the answers. It was worse than being sneered at, in a way, because it left you nowhere to stand.

Marilena came in and sat on the arm of Thanasis’s chair, half on him, and for a moment the whole arrangement was fine.

Lunch was at half past one, when the lamb was done, and by then the uncles had arrived. Babis was the only one of them, and Babis was enough. He was Kalliopi’s brother, retired from the National Bank for eleven years, and had been drinking since the church let out. The midnight service, then magiritsa at three in the morning, then a nap, then the wine. There was a whole country of men who took Easter seriously in that particular way, and Thanasis had uncles like that himself, so he knew the terrain.

He also knew what happened when the lamb came off the spit. Yiorgos and Nikos and Babis circled it with the fork and the long knife and the tray and produced a lot of noise and no forward motion, and after a minute Thanasis took the knife out of Nikos’s hand and did it himself. He cut down along the spine, opened the seams at the shoulder, and lifted the quarters off in the order they came, and the bones came out clean, and he did not cut his hand. It took three minutes. Yiorgos held the tray and watched.

“Where did you learn that?”

“My father. Then every summer in the village until he died. Then I did it instead.”

“You did it instead.”

“Somebody had to do it.”

Yiorgos nodded and carried the tray to the table. He said nothing else about it, but he put Thanasis at his right hand, and Kalliopi moved a plate to make room, and that was the whole negotiation.

They ate. The lamb was good. The wine was very good, better than anything Thanasis had drunk in months, and he drank it slowly because he knew what he was like when he drank other people’s wine too quickly. The table had thirteen at it and the conversation ran the way it ran at every table in the country on that day: about the year, the weather, the traffic on the national road, the price of everything. Where it differed was in the units. Nikos talked about a property in Saronida that had doubled. Daphne talked about the fee for the private school she had already registered Anna for, and how it came with a waiting list and a bond and how in the end you had to weigh it. Babis talked about the pension cuts, and how it was a scandal, and how he had friends in the ministry, and how he had known from the beginning. Thanasis ate his lamb and answered when he was asked.

And then, between the lamb and the eggs, it came.

“And you, Thanasi,” Babis said, leaning forward, the wine glass rocking in his hand. “You have your own place?”

“A floor in Nea Ionia. My mother is below.”

“Below,” Babis repeated carefully. “You bought it?”

“My father’s compensation bought it. There was an accident at the port. He was a stevedore. The company settled, and my mother bought a floor and we’ve stayed.”

“And a plot in the village?”

“No plot.”

“No plot,” said Babis, and then, because he was drunk and there was no reason not to, “and you fix other people’s headstones.”

Marilena’s hand came down on Thanasis’s knee under the table, quick and hard, a warning and an apology in one.

“I fix a great many things,” Thanasis said.

“I’m not saying,” said Babis, waving the glass, spilling. “I’m saying, the man has hands like a worker and mine are the hands of a man who moved paper. I’m saying that’s all. I’m saying the world is upside down.”

“Babi,” Kalliopi said.

“I’m saying nothing. I’m saying it’s Easter.”

“You’re saying nothing very loudly,” said Kalliopi, and there was a ripple of laughter, and the moment went the way those moments go, which is away, and Yiorgos refilled Thanasis’s glass himself.

Then the eggs. Kalliopi brought them in the bowl, dyed on Thursday, the deep red that only comes from onion skin and never from the little bottles from the supermarket, and Anna carried them around the table and everyone took one and there was the business of choosing the strong one, pretending you were not choosing, the tap of the shell against the shell.

“Roussos,” said Yiorgos, “against me.”

They knocked. Thanasis’s egg cracked. Anna shrieked.

“Again,” said Yiorgos.

The second one cracked too.

“You’re going easy on him,” Marilena said.

“I’m not going easy,” said Thanasis. “He has very strong eggs.”

“My eggs are from my own chicken,” said Kalliopi.

“There’s your answer.”

Yiorgos laughed until he had to take his glasses off. And then, with his glasses off, wiping them on his shirt, he said it in exactly the voice he would have used to offer a man a chair.

“You know, there’s a post at the clinic. Facilities. It’s nothing glamorous but it’s real — it’s a contract, ensima, the pension, all of it, and the hours are the hours. I’ve been looking for three months. The last one was a boy who couldn’t read a meter.” He put his glasses back on. “You’d be doing me the favour.”

The table did not go silent. That was the thing Thanasis would remember, later, on the balcony: that it did not go silent, that Daphne went on talking to Nikos about something else, that Babis asked for the bread, that Kalliopi stood up and started clearing the far side of the table, and that all of it, all of it, was turned in a single direction, the way a field of grass turns in the wind, and the direction was the understanding that this had been discussed.

He set his egg down in the little dish.

“Thank you,” he said.

“Think about it. Don’t answer now.”

“I’ll think about it.”

“It’s a real offer.”

“I know it is.”

Marilena was looking at her plate. Her ears had gone red, which was what happened to her; it was the first thing he had noticed about her, three winters ago, in the queue of a bakery in Kypseli, that her ears went red before she spoke.

“The plates,” she said. “Mama, I’ll do the plates.”

“Sit,” said Kalliopi.

“I’ll do them.”

She got up and started stacking, and Thanasis got up too, and for a moment they were both standing there with plates in their hands, and he looked at her and she would not look at him.

“Cigarette,” he said, and went out to the garden.

The spit had burned down to grey. He stood at the edge of the concrete and looked back through the glass door at the whole thing: Yiorgos at the head of the table with his glasses back on and his napkin on his lap, Nikos leaning in with his elbows on the cloth, Daphne with her hand on her belly, Anna under the table with the jigsaw, Kalliopi carrying four plates at once and doing it, Babis pouring himself more wine and not noticing that he was pouring it into someone else’s glass. They were not bad people. That was the part he could not get past. He had spent three years waiting for them to be bad people and they had declined to cooperate.

Marilena came out. She had taken off her shoes and she was holding a dish towel, and she stood next to him facing the house instead of him, which told him everything.

“You told them I don’t have ensima.”

“I mentioned it. In passing. Months ago.”

“You mentioned my back.”

“Because you came over that Sunday and you couldn’t sit down. You stood in my kitchen all evening and ate the soup standing.”

“So you looked at my back and you told your father.”

“I looked at your back and I told my father that I was worried about the man I live with.” Her voice stayed low; she was good at that, better than him. “That’s not a betrayal, Thanasi. That’s what people do.”

“I don’t live with you.”

“You stay.”

“I stay. It’s different. I have a floor.”

“You have a floor in Nea Ionia in the same building as your mother. Yes. I know. I’ve been to the floor.”

He looked at the almond tree. The last of its flowers had gone and the leaves were coming, a bright new green.

“You know what he was offering,” he said.

“A job.”

“A ladder. He was holding out a ladder for me to climb down onto.”

“And you’d rather keep climbing your own,” she said, and it was not a question, and there was no meanness in it, only the tiredness of a woman who had had this conversation in her own head already, many times, alone.

“It’s what I have.”

“You have me.”

He did not answer that, because anything he said would have been either a lie or an injury, and while he was not answering, the door opened and Yiorgos came out with two glasses and the bottle of tsipouro.

He had it unwrapped. The newspaper was gone and he had it in his hand like any bottle of anything, and Thanasis felt a pang of shame so precise that it hurt in the chest, not at the bottle, at the fact that he had wrapped it at all, that he had thought it needed the newspaper.

“This is your uncle’s?”

“Yes.”

“He makes it himself?”

“In his yard. Copper still. He does about forty litres a year and gives most of it away.”

Yiorgos turned the bottle in the light. “And the label is a pen.”

“The label is a pen.”

“My grandfather,” said Yiorgos, “had a plot at the back of the house in Agrinio and he made wine in a barrel and he poured it in a jug on the table and there was always something in the jug. And you know what happened to the plot.” He shrugged. “My mother sold it in eighty-one. It was nothing. There was nothing on it but the vines. It was worth nothing to anyone but us, and now there’s an apartment block and a bakery and I could have retired twice over.” He looked at the bottle again. “Your uncle’s is better than mine.”

“You’ve not tasted it.”

“I can tell.” He set it on the low wall. “Come tomorrow. Bring Marilena. We’ll open it and I’ll show you the clinic and you can see for yourself. I won’t ask you again.”

And he went back inside, and left them in the garden.

“He means it,” Marilena said.

“I know he means it.”

“Then what.”

He got out his cigarettes and lit one, and the smoke went up into the bougainvillea and stayed there.

“My father died at fifty-eight,” he said. “On the berth at Ikonio. My mother was forty-nine. He had hands like mine and the compensation paid for a floor and we moved into it, and for twenty years my mother has cleaned other people’s houses to keep the floor. She is sixty-eight and she cleans three houses a week, two of them in Politia, and last year she came home with a coat that one of them had put out for the cleaning ladies and she said to me, Thanasaki, it fits, and she was happy.” He turned the cigarette over. “Nobody in this house has ever had to take what was put out for them. That’s not their fault. But I’m not taking the coat.”

“Thanasis.”

“I’m not saying anything about your father. He’s a decent man. He offered me work and he offered me his own bottle.”

“Then what are you saying?”

He looked at her: barefoot on the paving, the dish towel, the wine dress, the red ears.

“I’m saying I don’t know how to do this,” he said. “The day. Not the marriage. The day.”

She did not answer, and they went back inside, and the afternoon went on the way afternoons do. Dessert. Coffee. Babis fell asleep in the chair with his mouth open and Kalliopi put a napkin over his hands. Thanasis carried the tray. He washed a rack of glasses with Nikos, who talked the whole time about a container ship and did not once mention the job, which was kind of him. At six, Marilena said they had to go; they were going to her friend’s in Kypseli, and then she was going to Thanasis’s mother’s; that had been the plan. He said his goodbyes. Yiorgos shook his hand with both hands and said, tomorrow, then. Kalliopi kissed him twice and said he was thin. The bottle stayed on the low wall in the garden. He did not take it.

In the car, Marilena put her hand on his leg and he rested his own over it, and they drove down out of the trees into the traffic on Kifisias, and neither of them said anything about tomorrow.

He dropped her at her friend’s at half past six, and she said, half an hour, and he said, fine, and she got out and stood on the pavement looking back at the car for a second before she went in, and he did not know what that second meant and did not have the courage to ask. He drove off and did not go to Kypseli and did not go to Nea Ionia either. He drove along Kifisias until it became Alexandras and then he went west, and the buildings got lower and the streets got loud and the balconies got close, and he parked, at last, badly, on the pavement outside the polykatoikia where he had lived since he was sixteen.

His mother was on the balcony on the second floor with the light on behind her and a basin of lentils in her lap, shelling them into a bowl.

“You’re back early,” she called down.

“I’m back.”

He climbed the two flights. Inside, the table was laid with the plastic tablecloth with the flowers on it, and there were two plates, and a plate of lamb with a towel over it on the sideboard, and the red eggs in a bowl, and the whole flat smelled of the onion skin and the tsoureki and the cigarettes his mother smoked on the balcony.

He could not explain to her about the day. He knew it because of the way she looked at him once, and did not ask, and went and got the lentils off the balcony and put them in water, and put the plate in front of him. He ate cold lamb with his mother in the kitchen with the television on low in the other room, and she told him about the church, about the candles, about Kyria Stavroula’s leg, about the procession and the traffic and how the young priest had done the whole thing too fast, and it was all right, and then it was ten o’clock and he went out to the balcony.

The gasworks were lit blue down the street. Somewhere on the third floor someone was watching the football, and somewhere below someone was doing the washing up. He lit a cigarette and looked at his phone, and there was a message from her.

I’m at my friend’s. It went fine.
Then, eleven minutes later:
I’m coming over.

Then:
I took a taxi as far as I could and I’m walking now, I’m near the National Bank, is it the one with the tiles?

He looked at the message. He typed something and deleted it and typed something else, and then the buzzer went, and he went down the stairs two at a time and out into the street, and there she was under the street lamp with her high heels in her hand and her feet in her stockings, and the tsipouro in the newspaper, held under her arm like a football, the way he had carried it that morning.

“You forgot this,” she said.

He stood there. Down the street the lights of the little shop, the one that stayed open all night for milk and cigarettes, went on.

“It’s the doorway after the National Bank,” he said.

“I know. I remembered.”

They went up. His mother was still in the kitchen, wiping the table, and she looked up when they came in, and looked at the feet, at the heels in her hand, and said nothing at all, and got a plate down from the shelf. Marilena sat down at the plastic tablecloth in her wine dress, and Elpida put the lamb in front of her with a fork and a knife, and the bowl of red eggs, and she took one and cracked it against the side of the plate without being asked.

“Strong one,” Elpida said.

“I picked it.” Marilena peeled the egg, and ate. Thanasis sat down across from her. His mother filled the glass at the third place and sat down too, to watch the girl eat, in the way of mothers, half-hostile and half-made-up, and for a while nobody said anything, and the television in the other room went on muttering, and outside a scooter passed with the radio going.

Thanasis put his elbows on the table. He looked at Marilena’s hands on the plastic cloth, at the shell, the red of the egg in its bowl, at the door to the balcony and the sheet his mother had forgotten outside, and he did not know what he was going to say, and he did not say anything yet.
