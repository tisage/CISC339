The trouble started with eleven planters, and by the time it was over the whole hillside had a name for it: the Sliver.

Tanabe Shigeru found the planters on the first Sunday of April, lined up along the concrete-block wall between his house and Okuyama Fumiko's like a row of small green teeth. They were white styrofoam fish boxes, the kind that came off the boats at Yawatahama with ice and sea bream in them. Each one held a pansy or a stalk of something leafy. He had been retired for four years from the Ehime Prefecture land survey office and, before that, from forty-one years of walking other people's property lines with a tripod over his shoulder. He did not think of himself as a man who noticed things. He simply could not help seeing that the boxes had moved.

He fetched the steel tape from the shed and knelt on the gravel in his slippers. The wall's footing, on his side, was clearly his. Beyond the footing, the old boundary was in theory six centimeters out, under the lip of the fish boxes.

He measured twice. He wrote the number on the back of his hand with a ballpoint pen, because the notebook was in the house and he didn't want to stand up again.

At ten o'clock he rang her bell, held his hand out in front of him as though presenting a diploma, and said good morning.

Fumiko was seventy-two, three years older than he was, and had run a tailoring and alterations shop out of her front room for forty years. She was small and straight-backed and had a way of looking at a person's hem before looking at his face. She looked at the numbers on his hand.

"What is that?"

"Six centimeters," Shigeru said. "Your planters. It's not a large matter. I only mention it so we both know."

"Know what?"

"Where the line is."

"I know where the line is," said Fumiko. "The line is where the planters are. They have been there since before your wife's hydrangeas."

This was true, in a way. There had been planters on that wall for years, ceramic ones, and Yoshiko used to lean over the blocks with her tea and talk to Fumiko about nothing for an hour at a time. Shigeru had never once measured anything. Then Yoshiko died, in the second winter of the pandemic, and the ceramic planters had cracked in the frost, and somebody had replaced them with styrofoam. He supposed it was Fumiko. He had not been paying attention. That was, he later admitted to no one, the whole point.

"The old planters were back from the line," he said. "These are six centimeters over."

"Then your wife's tea was six centimeters over, too," said Fumiko, and closed the door.

---

He moved the planters at night. He did it quietly, in his socks, with a flashlight in his teeth, and he felt a bit ridiculous doing it and a bit alive. Eleven fish boxes, each shifted six centimeters toward her house. In the morning he stood at the kitchen window with his rice and watched her come out in her cardigan, stop, and look at them for a long time. Then she moved them back, one at a time, and added two more.

So that was thirteen.

He ran a nylon string along the wall at the true line, as he understood it, pulling it tight between two iron pegs and checking it with a bubble level. The next morning her laundry was hanging from it: a row of blouses, two pairs of slacks, and a bath towel with a pattern of blue hydrangeas. They flapped in the sea wind like the flags of a small, hostile nation.

He painted a white line on the gravel. She painted a second line beside it, in a pink the exact color of a cheap eraser, an unmistakable finger-width to the left. Her cat, a fat gray tom named Boss, took to sitting between the two lines with the dignity of a customs officer.

Neither of them said a word to the other for three weeks, which, on a street where seventeen houses shared a single garbage pickup schedule, was a feat of discipline.

---

The neighborhood association met in the community hall on the second Thursday of the month, under a framed photograph of a long-dead mayor cutting a ribbon. The chairman, Hatta, ran the hardware store at the bottom of the hill. He was a round, anxious man who sold more tape measures to Shigeru than to everyone else in town combined and was quite sure this was no coincidence.

The agenda included the autumn festival, the proper sorting of cans, and a note in Hatta's own handwriting: *Tanabe-san / Okuyama-san: a matter between neighbors?*

"I'd just like to say," Hatta began, spreading his hands, "that we all have a great deal of respect for both of you."

"Eleven planters," Shigeru said.

"Thirteen," said Fumiko. "He's lost count."

"Thirteen. Six centimeters."

"Six centimeters of what?" said Fumiko. "Of air? You've measured air."

"Of my land."

"Well, if it's your land, you can come and get it."

A woman in the third row coughed into her sleeve. Hatta wiped his forehead with a hand towel printed with the logo of a fertilizer company. "What if," he said, with the desperate brightness of a man throwing a lifeline into a quarry, "what if we simply split the difference? Three centimeters each. Everyone moves a little."

They both looked at him as though he had proposed sharing a toothbrush.

"I don't accept a compromise on a measurement," said Shigeru.

"I don't accept a compromise on my planters," said Fumiko.

"Then a survey," said Hatta weakly. "A proper one."

"I am a proper survey," said Shigeru.

"Then you can't do it," Fumiko said. "You're a party. It would be like a man judging his own sumo bout."

There was a silence in which several people looked at their knees. Because she was right, and because he knew she was right, Shigeru said the thing he would regret for the rest of the year.

"Fine. Hire your own. Hire anyone. I'll tell you now, the number will be mine."

---

She hired Ishii Kenta.

This was a cruelty so refined that Shigeru wondered if she'd studied for it. Ishii was thirty-four and had worked under Shigeru for six years at the prefecture office before leaving to open a small private practice in town. He was a gentle, apologetic young man with a cowlick, and he was the only licensed surveyor within thirty kilometers who didn't charge extra for hills. He arrived on a Saturday with a total station, a prism pole, and an expression of complete misery, and set up on the street where he could be seen by everyone and speak to no one.

Shigeru stood on his own porch with his arms crossed and did not offer tea. Fumiko stood on hers. Ishii worked for two and a half hours with his shoulders hunched, shooting points along the wall, reading numbers off the screen, and bowing to each of them alternately, like a man trapped between two shrines.

When he was done he walked across the road and gave Shigeru the printout first, which was the only act of loyalty available to him.

"Sensei," he said. He had never stopped calling him that. "The line is here." He indicated a point on the sketch. "Three centimeters in toward Okuyama-san's side of where you've got it."

"Three centimeters toward *her*," said Shigeru.

"Yes."

"Then she's lost three. She's not entitled to complain."

"No, Sensei, she's—" Ishii looked at the sketch. "Sensei, I've done it from the north corner monument. If you used the south one, I can see how you'd get—"

"I used both."

"Then it's—" Ishii stopped. The wind moved his cowlick. "It's three centimeters the other way from Okuyama-san's planters. Her planters are *three* over, Sensei. Not six. You were—" He stopped again, because there was no polite end to the sentence.

"I was what."

"The numbers are not exactly the same."

Shigeru went inside, got his own instrument, a twenty-year-old theodolite that he had never really surrendered when the office modernized, and spent the whole of Sunday shooting the wall again. By evening he had it: three centimeters, toward Fumiko. He had been right in direction and wrong in magnitude, or Ishii was wrong, or the world was.

He and the boy had, between them, produced two lines six centimeters apart.

---

"A sliver," said Hatta. He had come up the hill with both printouts in a plastic folder and sat on a folding stool in Shigeru's genkan, because Shigeru would not let him farther in. "Between the two lines. Six centimeters wide, the whole length of the wall. That's, what, eleven meters?"

"Eleven point four."

"So it belongs to nobody."

"It belongs to somebody," said Shigeru. "It's just a question of who."

"Until it's settled," Hatta said, with the brightness of a man who had been rehearsing, "we could regard it as neutral. Common ground. A sort of—" He made a flat, hovering gesture with one hand. "A demilitarized zone."

Shigeru would later say he did not agree to this. But he did not refuse, and Hatta, who had been waiting forty years for a chance to say *demilitarized zone* in a community meeting, went down the hill glowing.

By the end of the week the Sliver was a battlefield.

She planted moss in it. She had gone out to the mountain road with a trowel and a bucket and returned with sheets of the stuff, lush as green velvet, which she pressed into the six-centimeter gap with the care of a woman laying a hem. Within three days she'd edged it with a line of small river stones, to *define the space*, she said, to Hatta, who relayed it, wincing.

He laid gravel. He bought a ton of it from a yard in Ozu, fine white crushed granite, and spread it from the Sliver's edge to his own wall with a rake, then swept the surface into long lines like a temple garden. Every morning he went out to check it. Every morning there were a few green fragments in it, and one or two river stones that had rolled. He picked them out with tweezers. He put them in a jar on the porch.

He didn't know why he kept the jar. It sat on the railing, an exhibit. Then there were nineteen stones in it. Then thirty.

She, he gathered, had begun keeping her own record. He caught sight of her once through the window with a notebook, writing something while looking at his gravel, and he felt the particular prickle of being observed by a professional.

---

By September the street had picked sides. The man who ran the noodle shop took Shigeru's; his wife, privately, took Fumiko's. Children were forbidden to cut across either yard and so cut across both, shrieking, in a thin hard line that no one could prove. A cousin of Fumiko's, visiting from Matsuyama, was seen photographing the gravel. Someone put an anonymous pamphlet in every mailbox titled *Boundary Etiquette: A Guide to Harmonious Neighborhood Living.* It had been printed on a municipal photocopier and had a coffee ring on the cover.

The persimmon tree was the next front.

It stood in the corner of Fumiko's yard, hard against the wall, and had been there longer than either of them. It was a sprawling old Fuyu, thick-limbed, black-barked, heavy every October with fruit the color of traffic cones. It had always dropped a certain amount of itself into Shigeru's yard, leaves and twigs and the occasional soft, sweet disaster of an overripe persimmon that landed on his car. In the old days Yoshiko had gathered them up in a basket and carried them back over the wall without comment, and Fumiko had given her half of them again.

This year the first fruit that fell on Shigeru's side was on the third of October. He picked it up with a plastic bag over his hand, as one handles evidence, and carried it to the gate.

Her door opened before he rang.

"It's yours," he said, offering the bag. "It fell on my property."

"Keep it."

"I don't want it."

"Then give it to the crows," said Fumiko.

"It is *your* tree."

"It's a tree that grows on a *line*," said Fumiko, with a certain satisfaction. "Which your sliver, I believe, is somewhere beneath."

He opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

Because she was, again, right. The trunk of that old tree stood almost exactly over the Sliver, rooted in the very spot that, by treaty, belonged to nobody. He had not thought of it. In forty-one years he had never once been out-argued by a woman holding a cardigan closed at the throat.

He went home and began, that evening, a ledger.

Each persimmon that fell on his side was recorded: date, time, weight on the kitchen scale, condition. Each was placed in a small paper bag labeled with a number. At the end of the week he delivered the bags to her step in a cardboard box with a covering letter, typed, setting out in four paragraphs that the contents were *returned without prejudice to either party's claim*.

The box came back two days later. Inside was an invoice. It was handwritten, on a tailor's receipt pad, for *sap damage to one (1) bicycle seat, ¥400*.

He had no idea what she meant. Then he went to the shed and found, to his horror, that she was right. There was a tacky brown stain on the seat that he'd never noticed. It had been there since the summer.

He paid the four hundred yen. He put the coins in an envelope. He felt, lying awake afterward, a deep and shameful happiness that he couldn't account for at all.

---

On the eighteenth of October she cut the tree.

He heard the saw from the kitchen. He went out in his slippers and saw her on a stepladder, in rubber boots and a sun visor, with a folding pruning saw, taking off every branch that overhung his side. She did it with grim, even strokes and without once looking at him. The limbs fell on her own side of the wall with a heavy crash. By noon the old persimmon, which had been a great round green dome, looked like a person who had been given a terrible haircut, with all its weight thrown to one side and the whole western half stripped bare.

"Under the new law," she called down, without being asked, "I am entitled to trim my own branches before you ask me to."

"That's not what the law—" he started.

"And the fruit," Fumiko said, "stays on *my* side. It's all on my side now. Isn't it? Look at that."

It was. Forty or fifty fruit hung on the east side of the tree in the afternoon light, round and bright, a lopsided chandelier. The tree leaned toward her house like a drunk toward a wall. Shigeru had stood in front of enough structures to know what a bad load path looked like.

"The tree is unbalanced," he said.

"The tree is fine."

"It has all its weight on the one side. It's a big tree. If a typhoon comes—"

"If a typhoon comes," said Fumiko, "it'll come for you first. You're on the sea side."

He opened his mouth. He thought of Yoshiko, who would have said something sharp and then something kind and then handed over a basket of fruit. He closed it and went back into the house and wrote on a card, in his best draftsman's hand, *Please have the tree inspected*, and put it in her mailbox, where it stayed.

---

Typhoon No. 21 was an ugly one. It came up out of the Bungo Channel on the night of the twenty-sixth, having already killed three people in Kyushu, and by eight o'clock the whole hill was roaring. Shigeru had taped his windows in X's, as he did every year, and brought in the potted plants, and sat at the kitchen table with the radio and a flashlight and a thermos of barley tea. The rain was coming sideways, harder than he'd ever heard it hit the glass. He thought: *the gravel*. Then he thought, absurdly: *the stones in the jar*.

At ten-forty there was a crack from outside, a flat sound like a rifle shot, and then a longer groaning, wooden, sustained, a sound that made the hair on his arms stand up.

He knew what it was before he got to the window. He could see nothing but rain. He pulled on his raincoat over his pajamas, took the flashlight, and went out the back, and the wind took the door out of his hand and slammed it against the wall.

The persimmon tree was down.

It had gone over the wall. That was the first thing he saw in the beam of the flashlight: the whole top-heavy mass of it laid across the blocks, with the root plate torn up out of the earth like a black dish held up to the sky. The wall had buckled under the weight. Half of it was down, the blocks tumbled in the gravel. Branches lay across his carport. A great limb had come down across the back corner of Fumiko's house, and he could see the dark hole in her roof where tiles had been torn away and the water was already pouring in.

Her kitchen light was on. Then it went out.

"Okuyama-san!"

The wind took the word away. He went over the wall, over what remained of it, over the white gravel he'd raked and the green moss she'd planted, now all churned together in one black stew, and he did not think about the line at all. He thought only that she was seventy-two and alone, and that her son was in Osaka. He pounded on her back door.

"Okuyama-san!"

She opened it with a candle in one hand and a bucket in the other. She was in her nightgown and her rubber boots. She looked at him, and then past him at the tree and the broken wall.

"It's in the sewing room," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. "The water. It's coming in on the machines."

They worked for two hours. He found a blue tarp in her shed and she held the flashlight while he went up the ladder with a hammer in the rain and tacked it over the hole, one corner at a time, cursing in the dialect his father had used. She carried the machines to the dry room, one by one, and the bolts of cloth, in her arms, ignoring his shouts to let him do it. The sewing machines were old black Singers with gold decals. She carried them like infants. When it was done they stood in her kitchen, soaked, steaming, not speaking, and she made tea on the gas ring and put it into his hands without asking whether he wanted any.

He drank it. It was very strong. It was the first thing she had given him in two years.

At some point the wind dropped.

---

By dawn the storm had gone through, trailing a low yellow light across the bay. The hill looked as though it had been shaken in a box. There were roof tiles on the road, a bicycle in a tree, and a blue plastic bucket lodged in the top of a telephone pole. The two of them stood in the ruin of the wall and looked at the fallen persimmon, and its roots, and the great raw crater where it had stood.

"Look at that," Fumiko said.

At the bottom of the crater, in the clay where the root plate had been, a small gray shape stuck up out of the mud. It was a concrete post, square-sided, about the size of a milk carton, with a brass disk set into its top.

He climbed down into the hole without being asked. The clay sucked at his boots. He knelt and wiped the disk with his thumb.

It was a boundary marker. A real one. The brass was stamped with the mark of the old Matsuda Survey Office, which had been absorbed into the prefecture in the eighties, and beneath the stamp, scratched into the concrete around the plate, while it was still wet, were two initials and a year.

*S.T. 83.*

He stared at it for a long time.

He remembered, suddenly and completely, a day in the spring of 1983, a young man with a borrowed level and a bag of cement mix, setting the stakes on this slope before there were houses on it, only a row of wet red stakes in the grass and the sea beyond. He remembered the post. He remembered pushing it down beside a young persimmon sapling that someone had already planted there, a sapling that was not on anyone's plan. He had set the marker beside it so as not to disturb the roots, and the sapling had grown up around it, and over it, and swallowed it whole.

"What does it say?" said Fumiko, from the rim.

"It's the original marker," he said. "From the subdivision."

"Where's the line?"

He took the tape from his coat, which he realized had been in the pocket the whole night, and found the center of the brass disk. He measured to the surviving end of his white gravel line. Then to her pink paint. He did it twice, because he always did it twice. He had to do it three times, in fact, before he believed it.

"It's in the middle," he said.

"In the middle of what?"

"The Sliver. The marker is three centimeters from my line and three from Ishii's. It's exactly halfway."

"Of course it is," Fumiko said.

He climbed out of the hole. His hands were cold and shaking. "That's—it isn't possible. It's only possible if we were both off by the same amount in opposite—"

"It's possible," she said. "Hatta said so in April. Split the difference."

"That was a guess. He's a *hardware* man."

"Then he guessed right. I imagine it was the first time."

They both looked at the post. The sun was coming up now, thin and flat and brassy, and it caught the little disk in the clay and lit it up like a coin.

"There's a rule about this," he said slowly. "A tree on the boundary. A tree growing on the line. Under the Civil Code it's—"

"Shared," said Fumiko.

"You looked it up."

"I looked it up in April." She sniffed. "I looked it up the day you came with the tape on your hand."

He turned to face her. She was standing in her ruined yard in rubber boots and a man's raincoat, with mud to the knee and her gray hair coming loose. She did not look triumphant. She looked tired, and a little grim, and somehow very small. He thought: *she has known for six months, and she has not once told me.* He did not know whether that was an insult or the other thing.

"So it's half mine," he said.

"Half the tree. Half the fruit."

"What fruit?"

She pointed.

On the ground, scattered across both yards and the wreck of the wall and the black earth around the crater, lay the persimmons. Forty, fifty, more. Most had burst. But many had come through whole, bright and heavy and orange, rolled in the mud like dropped lanterns. Beyond them the fallen crown of the tree still held a few dozen more, hanging from the broken branches at an angle of absolute disgrace.

---

They did it on the Sunday, after the roofer had come and gone and Hatta had been up the hill twice with his towel and then, wisely, stopped coming. The tree could not be saved. The city sent a man with a chainsaw to cut away the trunk, but Fumiko told him to wait until the fruit was in, and he looked at the sky and looked at his watch and agreed.

Shigeru stood on one side of the trunk with a plastic crate and she on the other, and they picked.

It was not peaceful. They argued about every fruit. He said that one was on her side and she said it was on his; he produced the tape and she produced her own, a yellow tailor's tape that hung round her neck like a stole. He drove a pair of pegs into the mud, one on each side of the brass marker, and ran a string between. Anything above the string was his. Anything below was hers. Anything on the string was resolved by a coin, a hundred-yen piece, which Fumiko tossed with a flick of the thumb that suggested a dissolute youth.

By four in the afternoon they had counted a hundred and twelve persimmons that were fit to eat. Fifty-six each. It had come out to exactly fifty-six each, which neither of them mentioned.

"They'll have to be peeled," said Fumiko, looking at her crates. "Before they go off. Hoshigaki. You hang them up."

"I know what hoshigaki is."

"Yoshiko used to do them under your eaves." She said it flatly, looking at the fruit. "The sun's better on your side. The sea wind. You get the sweetness that way."

He had not heard his wife's name from anyone else in three years. He looked at his hands. The tape was still in them.

"I have string," he said.

"I have the knife."

They sat on his back step as the light went amber, with a bucket between them, and she peeled and he tied. She took the skin off each fruit in one long unbroken ribbon, turning it against the blade without looking, the way she must have turned ten thousand hems. He knotted each stem to the cord with a surveyor's clove hitch, then strung the cords along the eaves of his house, row on row, where the persimmons hung heavy and bright against the gray tile like a rank of small lamps. The ribbons of peel piled up at their feet.

At some point he noticed that the cat, Boss, had climbed into the empty crate and was asleep in it. At some other point he noticed that the white gravel and the green moss were mixed together in the mud in a way that no one would ever sort out, and he found that he did not much want to.

"Six months," he said, when the last cord was up.

"Hm."

"You knew about the tree. Why didn't you say?"

Fumiko cleaned the knife on her apron. "You never asked me," she said. "You only measured."

The sun went down behind the roof of the house across the road, and the persimmons on the eaves turned from orange to red. He got up and went inside and came back with two cups, and they drank, not looking at each other, watching the fruit.

In the morning the stake would still be in its hole, and the wall would still be down, and Ishii would come with his total station and the forms to register the marker, and Hatta would want to know whether they would put up a new wall, and if so, whose it would be. Nobody had decided anything. But the string was strung from his eaves to hers, the length of both houses, a hundred and twelve persimmons, hung evenly, in a line that no one had measured at all, and a few of them already glowed.

"Your side's sagging," said Fumiko.

"It is not."

"It's four centimeters low."

He looked. He sighed. He went to get the ladder.
