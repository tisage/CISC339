When Zaynab bint Karim took the package, it weighed less than a pomegranate.

It lay in the old man’s palm wrapped in oilcloth, bound with blue thread, and sealed with a blob of red wax stamped by a signet she did not recognize. He had brought it to the Route House after evening prayer, when the courtyard was full of tired men arguing over mule fees and the price of dates.

“You are Zaynab al-Karim?” he asked.

“I am.”

“You have crossed the Salt Road.”

“Twice.”

“And the Wadi of Knives?”

“Once.”

He looked at her boots, at the sand worked into the leather, and seemed to approve.

“My name is Musa al-Nuri. This must reach Bir al-Sidr.”

Zaynab did not take the parcel. “Bir al-Sidr is beyond the Red March.”

“Yes.”

“The Red March belongs to Harith ibn Salim now.”

“Men say many things belong to him.”

“His riders shoot couriers.”

“They shoot merchants too. Yet merchants continue to travel.”

“Merchants travel with twelve camels and bribe money.”

Musa set the package on the low table between them. “I have bribe money.”

At that, the keeper of the Route House, old Fawzi with his white beard dyed orange by henna, stopped pretending not to listen.

“How much?” Zaynab asked.

Musa named a sum large enough to buy a mule, a saddle, and perhaps a small house in the poorer quarter of Qasr al-Raml.

Zaynab looked again at the parcel.

“What is in it?”

“Something that must reach Bir al-Sidr.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I can give.”

She leaned back. In the courtyard, someone struck up a reed flute. The tune was thin and mournful under the clatter of cooking pots.

“You want a fool,” she said.

“No. I want a courier.”

“A courier knows whether she carries silk, letters, medicine, or a knife meant for a king.”

“Why?”

“Because silk can be abandoned. Letters can be burned. Medicine should be kept cool. And a knife for a king gets you hanged.”

Musa’s mouth twitched, but it was not a smile. He was perhaps sixty, perhaps eighty; his face had the dry, folded look of a man who had spent his life outdoors. Beneath his brown cloak, his left hand shook.

“Open it,” he said.

Fawzi made a noise in his throat. “Musa—”

“Let her.”

Zaynab picked up the parcel. The oilcloth was stiff and smelled faintly of cedar. She turned it over in her hands. No rattle. No liquid slosh. No give beneath the wrapping.

“You have sealed it.”

“I have.”

“You say I may open it?”

“Yes.”

She drew the little blade from her sleeve. Before she could touch the wax, Musa put his hand over hers.

“Not here.”

She looked into his eyes.

“Then no,” she said, and handed the package back.

For the first time, the old man seemed relieved.

Fawzi spat into the dust. “You ask a girl to cross Harith’s land carrying a secret, and you will not tell her what it is.”

“I will tell her this much,” Musa said. “If it reaches Bir al-Sidr, people who have done no harm may live. If Harith gets it, he will use it to make them obey him. If it is destroyed, the desert will keep its silence.”

That could have meant anything. It could have meant a treaty, a map, the name of a traitor, a recipe for gunpowder. Zaynab had carried stranger things. Once, for a widow in Hamat, she had carried a locked copper box containing nothing but the shaved hair of a dead son. The widow had paid half her savings to have it carried eighty miles because the boy had died in the wrong town.

But the Red March was different.

The Red March had once been grazing land between the cities of Qasr al-Raml and Hajar. Then the wells failed, the governors argued, and Harith ibn Salim gathered deserters, debtors, and men who liked being feared. He took the road stations, then the cisterns, then the old railway embankment. He taxed caravans until they stopped coming. He hanged three messengers from the gate of Marwah and left their leather bags dangling from their necks.

Zaynab had seen one of those bags.

She was not eager to become another.

Musa placed a leather purse beside the package. It landed heavily.

“Half now,” he said. “Half when you return.”

“Who will know if I return?”

“I will.”

“You are old.”

“I am not dead.”

Zaynab studied him. “And if I do not?”

“My sister in Bir al-Sidr will pay the remainder to whomever you name.”

Fawzi coughed softly. He knew, as Zaynab did, that the old man’s word mattered more than a written contract.

She thought of her mother’s roof, patched each winter with mud that dissolved in the first hard rain. She thought of her younger brother Khalil, who had begun talking about joining a caravan guard because a guard earned more than a boy who mended harnesses. She thought of the jar of coins beneath her sleeping mat, not nearly enough.

“When?”

“Tonight.”

“You want me to leave tonight?”

“Harith’s men are searching the east road at dawn.”

“Why?”

Musa looked at the package.

Zaynab laughed once, without humor. “You are very good at making me regret things before I have agreed to them.”

But she took the purse.

By midnight, the city gates of Qasr al-Raml were shut, and Zaynab was riding north through the dry riverbed called Wadi al-Ajaj. Behind her, the lamps of the city trembled in the dark like coals in a brazier. Ahead, the moon lit the pale ribs of the land.

She rode her gray mare, Sahlah, who was narrow-chested, bad-tempered, and clever enough to avoid snakes. The package was not in her saddlebag. That was the first place anyone would look.

It lay inside a hollowed wooden heel beneath the sole of her left boot.

The boot made her limp slightly. She hated that. A courier should never attract notice through clumsiness. Still, she preferred a limp to a bullet.

For two days she kept off the main roads, following dry gullies and abandoned irrigation channels. She saw no one except a family of goat herders far off on a ridge, their animals moving like black beads through the thorn scrub. Once, just after dawn, she found a dead fox caught in a rusted wire snare. Its mouth was full of sand.

On the third afternoon, she reached the edge of the Red March.

The boundary was marked by nothing official. No wall, no gate, no stone pillar. Only the road changed.

The old royal road had been paved with fitted blocks of black basalt. Beyond the border, the stones had been torn up in places to make trenches and barricades. The date palms that once lined it were cut down. Their trunks lay bleached and split in the ditches. A burned truck rusted beside a milestone painted with Harith’s emblem: a red hand.

Zaynab dismounted and led Sahlah into the shadow of an overturned water tower.

She had planned to cross by night, following the railway embankment west for six miles, then cutting through a region of salt flats where horses left poor tracks. It was dangerous but possible.

Then she saw the smoke.

It rose behind the embankment in a black twisting column. A moment later came the faint crackle of rifle fire.

Sahlah raised her head.

“Quiet,” Zaynab whispered.

She climbed the tower’s fallen ladder and looked through a crack in the iron frame.

A small caravan had been stopped on the road below: two wagons, four mules, perhaps ten people. Red-scarved riders circled them. Zaynab could not hear the words, but she could see the gestures. One of Harith’s men pulled a man from a wagon by his shirt. Another overturned a basket, scattering onions over the road.

A woman ran toward the onions. A rider struck her with the butt of his rifle.

Zaynab lowered herself slowly.

She could wait until dark. The riders might move on.

Then she heard a child crying.

She shut her eyes.

Her father had been a courier before her. When she was twelve, he had returned from a winter crossing with frost in his beard and blood soaking one sleeve. He had sat her down beside the brazier while her mother stitched his arm and told her the first rule of the road.

A courier carries what she is paid to carry. A courier does not become a soldier, a judge, or a fool.

“What if someone needs help?” Zaynab had asked.

Her father had looked at her with tired eyes.

“Then you ask whether helping them delivers your message, or buries it.”

She had hated him for that answer for years.

Now, below the water tower, the crying stopped suddenly.

Zaynab climbed down. She took the rifle from its scabbard beside her saddle. It had belonged to her father too, though its stock was cracked and the sight bent slightly left.

She tied Sahlah’s reins to a bent pipe and walked toward the road.

The sun was behind her. The riders did not see her until she was close.

She did not aim at them.

She aimed at the closest mule.

The shot exploded across the flat land. The animal screamed and collapsed in its traces. One wagon lurched sideways. Its driver fell beneath it. Men shouted. Horses reared.

Zaynab fired again, this time at the road in front of the riders, and then ran back toward the tower.

Bullets chased her. One struck the iron frame above her head with a shriek. Another kicked dust against her ankle.

She flung herself behind the broken tower wall. For three breaths she heard nothing but her own lungs.

Then the caravan survivors began to run.

Not toward her. South, away from the road, vanishing among the gullies.

Good, she thought. Go.

A rider came around the tower.

Zaynab waited until he was nearly on top of her, then fired from below the line of the wall.

The man fell backward off his horse. The horse bolted, dragging one stirrup through the dust.

There was no time to see if he was dead.

Zaynab ran to Sahlah, cut the reins, and mounted. She rode west, not toward the embankment but toward the open flats, where there was no cover and no road.

Behind her, someone shouted, “There! The woman!”

A rifle cracked.

Sahlah stumbled but kept going.

By sunset, Zaynab’s mouth tasted of metal and her shoulder throbbed from the recoil. She had lost the riders after crossing an old salt pan where the surface broke beneath hooves into sharp white plates. She did not stop until darkness swallowed the horizon.

Then she found shelter in the remains of a pumping station.

Its roof had fallen in long ago. Sand lay in drifts across the tiled floor. A rusted pipe rose from the ground like the neck of some extinct animal.

She watered Sahlah with two careful cupfuls from her skin, rubbed the mare’s legs, and checked for wounds. There was only a graze along the flank, shallow but bleeding.

“You are tougher than you look,” Zaynab told her.

Sahlah pinned her ears.

“Yes. I know. You look very tough.”

Zaynab made a tiny fire in the lee of a wall and warmed hard bread over the coals. She had just put the first piece in her mouth when a voice said, from the darkness beyond the broken doorway:

“If I were you, I would not make smoke.”

Her hand went to the rifle.

“Come closer,” she said.

“I prefer not to.”

The voice was young. Male. Hoarse.

“Then speak from there.”

“I saw you by the water tower.”

“So did others.”

“I saw you shoot at Harith’s men.”

“That makes me popular.”

A figure stepped into the firelight with both hands raised.

He was perhaps seventeen. Dust covered his hair and face. He wore a shirt too large for him, a torn vest, and one boot; the other foot was wrapped in strips of cloth. Blood had dried down the side of his neck.

“I am not armed,” he said.

“Everyone says that.”

“I have a knife.”

“Where?”

He pointed to his waistband.

“Throw it.”

He pulled out a short kitchen knife and tossed it across the tiles. It skidded near Zaynab’s knee.

“Now you are not armed.”

He looked at the bread.

“You were with the caravan,” she said.

“My uncle’s caravan.”

“Where is he?”

The boy said nothing.

Zaynab looked toward the doorway. “They got away.”

“Some did.”

“And you?”

“I ran the wrong direction.”

“That is a common mistake.”

He took a cautious step nearer. “Are you going to Bir al-Sidr?”

Zaynab’s fingers tightened around the bread.

“No.”

He swallowed.

“You are. Harith’s men said there was a courier. They said a woman with a gray mare had stolen something from Qasr al-Raml.”

“I did not steal anything.”

“They have your description.”

“Then you should leave.”

“They will find me.”

“They will find you more quickly standing beside me.”

The boy looked at the fire, then at Sahlah. “I know a way across the flats.”

“So does every goat in this country.”

“Not this way. There is an old storm drain under the railway. It opens near the Shrine of Daughters. Riders cannot take horses through it.”

Zaynab studied him. “Why tell me?”

“Because I want to get out.”

“And why would I take you?”

His eyes moved to the road outside, where nothing stirred.

“Because you shot at them,” he said.

That was not an answer either, but it was an honest one.

His name was Ammar. His uncle had traded salt and onions between the villages, paying Harith’s tolls because refusal meant losing the wagons altogether. That morning, the riders had searched the caravan for weapons and letters. They found neither. Then one of them had recognized Ammar’s uncle as a man who had once supplied food to a village that resisted Harith’s tax collectors.

“They took him,” Ammar said.

“Where?”

“To the old cement works.”

Zaynab knew the place. Everyone did. Harith’s men kept prisoners there because the kilns were deep and the windows were barred with steel mesh.

“Your uncle may still be alive,” she said.

Ammar looked at her sharply, as if he had not allowed himself that thought.

“Maybe.”

“But we are not going there.”

His expression closed.

She handed him a piece of bread.

He took it.

Before dawn, they left the pumping station. Zaynab gave Ammar the spare sandals from her saddlebag and made him walk ahead for the first hour so she could watch him. He led them through a maze of old drainage cuts choked with reeds turned gray by salt. Twice they stopped while patrols passed on the railway embankment above.

At sunrise, they reached the storm drain.

It was an arched opening beneath the tracks, half buried by sand. A rank smell came from inside.

“This?” Zaynab asked.

“It goes under the line.”

“It looks like a place rats would refuse.”

Ammar crouched at the entrance. “There is room.”

“For you.”

“For the horse too. The floor is low in the middle.”

Sahlah sniffed the darkness and refused to move.

Zaynab tugged the reins. “Come, daughter of a donkey.”

Sahlah planted all four feet.

Ammar found a handful of dry grass caught beneath a thorn bush and held it under the mare’s nose. “She likes this?”

“She likes dates, oats, and not being insulted.”

“She is a horse. She does not know she is insulted.”

“Sahlah knows everything.”

But at last the mare followed them in.

The drain was colder than the outside air. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. Their footsteps echoed against the brick walls. Zaynab had to duck beneath the curved ceiling, and Sahlah’s packs scraped both sides.

After perhaps a hundred paces, they found the first body.

It lay face-down in the thin stream that ran along the center of the tunnel. The clothes had rotted away in places. No face remained.

Ammar stopped breathing for a moment.

“Keep walking,” Zaynab said quietly.

There were others farther in: two men, a woman with a child’s bracelet caught around one wrist. Zaynab did not ask who they had been. The tunnel made no distinctions among the dead.

At the far end, sunlight showed as a pale slit.

Then voices came from above them.

Ammar froze.

The voices were muffled through the brick ceiling, but one of them was clear enough: “Search every culvert. She cannot have crossed the line.”

Zaynab pulled Ammar and Sahlah back into the dark.

They waited.

A drop of water landed on Zaynab’s cheek. Sahlah shifted her weight. Ammar’s breathing came fast and shallow.

“What are they looking for?” he whispered.

“Me, apparently.”

“No. The thing you carry.”

“You know nothing about it.”

“I know Harith has sent men from every post. He does not do that over one courier.”

Zaynab said nothing.

“He wants it badly.”

“So?”

“So perhaps you should know why.”

“I told you, I do not.”

“You took it without asking?”

“Yes.”

Ammar stared at her in the dark. “That is stupid.”

Zaynab almost smiled. “You have known me one night. You are already very wise.”

The voices faded. Hooves clattered along the tracks overhead.

They waited another hour before emerging.

The land on the other side of the railway was worse than the Red March. It had been an orchard district once. Rows of dead citrus trees stood in the dust, their branches twisted and bare. Canals ran empty between them. The houses had been stripped for wood, doors, and roof tiles.

At noon, they found a well.

The stone mouth was cracked, but water glimmered far below. Zaynab lowered her cup and pulled up a thin muddy measure. It tasted bitter, but Sahlah drank greedily.

Ammar sat beside the well wall, staring east.

“You can still turn south,” Zaynab said. “There are villages near the foothills.”

“And leave you?”

“Yes.”

“They are looking for you.”

“They are looking for a woman with a gray mare. If I were you, I would avoid both.”

He gave her a long look. “You are going to Bir al-Sidr even if it kills you.”

“I am being paid.”

“No one pays enough for this.”

Zaynab looked down at the water in her cup.

She thought of the purse hidden in the lining of her coat. She thought of the second half promised on return. She thought of Khalil, saying he would never be a courier because he did not want to die for other people’s secrets.

“No,” she said. “No one does.”

They rode again.

Toward evening, they came upon a small mud-brick house beside a field of dead barley. Smoke rose from its chimney.

Zaynab wanted to pass it. Any inhabited house in the Red March might hold a spy, a patrol, or someone too frightened to say no to either. But Ammar saw the mare favoring her wounded flank and said, “There is an old woman there. Her name is Um Hatem. She has goats. She will have salve.”

“You know her?”

“My uncle traded with her.”

“That is not the same as trusting her.”

“No,” he said. “It is not.”

Still, Zaynab let him lead them to the door.

The old woman who opened it was nearly blind. Her eyes were pale and clouded, but she held a shotgun across her forearms as steadily as a soldier.

“Ammar?” she said.

“Yes, khalti.”

“Your uncle?”

The boy lowered his head.

Um Hatem looked past him at Zaynab, at Sahlah, at the rifle.

“Come in,” she said.

Her house was clean and dark. Dried herbs hung from the rafters. A child slept under a patched blanket in one corner: a girl of perhaps five, thin as a reed, with fever-bright cheeks.

“My granddaughter,” Um Hatem said when she saw Zaynab looking. “Her mother went to the hills for medicine three days ago.”

“Has she returned?”

“No.”

Zaynab helped Ammar tend Sahlah’s wound while Um Hatem boiled goat milk with crushed leaves. The old woman did not ask their business. That was a kindness, and Zaynab accepted it as one.

But when night came and the child began coughing, Um Hatem sat beside the bedding and pressed her hand to the little girl’s chest.

“She needs water,” she murmured. “Clean water.”

“There is the well,” Ammar said.

“The well is poison now. Not enough to kill a grown man quickly. Enough for a child.”

Zaynab looked at the packet in the heel of her boot.

For the first time since leaving Qasr al-Raml, she wondered if it had something to do with water.

Musa’s words returned to her: If it reaches Bir al-Sidr, people who have done no harm may live. If Harith gets it, he will use it to make them obey him.

Perhaps it was a map to a hidden spring. Perhaps it was a device to purify wells. Perhaps it was something worth more than one little girl’s lungs struggling in the dark.

She hated herself for thinking that.

After Um Hatem slept, Zaynab took off her left boot.

Ammar watched from across the room.

“What are you doing?”

“Nothing.”

“You said the package was hidden.”

“I did not say that.”

“You have been limping since the water tower.”

Zaynab pried out the wooden heel. Inside was the oilcloth parcel.

Ammar’s face changed. He had imagined something grander, perhaps—a gold seal, a jewel, a pistol. “That is it?”

“That is it.”

“Open it.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I promised.”

“You promised a man who did not trust you enough to tell you what you were carrying.”

“That is often how trust works on the road.”

He watched her turn the parcel in her hands.

The red wax seal had cracked in the heat. Only a thin line held it together now.

Zaynab could open it. She could reseal it badly, perhaps. Musa would know, but Musa was far away, and the Red March was all around them.

The child coughed again.

Zaynab put the package back in the heel.

“We leave before dawn,” she said.

Ammar looked at the sleeping girl. “You are going to leave her?”

“I am going to Bir al-Sidr.”

He did not speak to her after that.

Before dawn, Um Hatem woke them with a hand over Zaynab’s mouth.

Outside, horses stood in the yard.

Red scarves moved past the shutters.

Zaynab lay still. Ammar was awake beside the hearth, his eyes wide. Sahlah gave a soft anxious snort in the lean-to behind the house.

A fist struck the door.

“Open in Harith’s name!”

Um Hatem’s face became very still. She reached for the shotgun.

Zaynab caught her wrist.

“No.”

The old woman’s hand shook, not with fear but fury.

Another blow landed on the door.

“Open!”

Zaynab looked at Ammar. “Back wall?”

He nodded toward the kitchen alcove.

Um Hatem whispered, “There is a hatch under the grain bin. It leads to the old cistern.”

“And from there?”

“Nowhere.”

“Then it will do.”

The door splintered.

Zaynab shoved the package into Ammar’s hands.

“What?” he mouthed.

“Put it in your shirt.”

His eyes widened.

“If they find me, run.”

“I can’t—”

“Do it.”

He tucked the parcel against his chest. Zaynab kicked the grain bin aside, lifted the wooden hatch, and pushed him down into the black opening. Then she climbed in after him. Um Hatem lowered the hatch overhead.

They crouched in darkness amid the smell of stale water and damp stone.

Above them, men entered the house.

“Old woman,” someone said. “Where is the courier?”

“I have not seen a courier.”

“A gray mare is tied outside.”

“My daughter’s mare.”

“You have no daughter.”

“I had one yesterday.”

There was a slap. Ammar flinched.

Zaynab pressed her hand over his mouth.

Boots crossed the floor. Pots crashed. Someone tore down the hanging herbs.

Then a man said, “We know she came here. We found tracks.”

“Then perhaps she left.”

“She carries a package.”

“I have no use for packages.”

“The emir has offered silver for it.”

At the word emir, Zaynab felt Ammar tense.

Harith had begun calling himself emir, then. That was new.

Above them, another voice said, “Captain, there is a boy’s sandal outside.”

Silence.

Then the first man spoke, softer now. “Ammar al-Qasim. Your uncle’s boy.”

Um Hatem did not answer.

“We took his uncle yesterday. He has been very stubborn. It would be a shame if he learned the boy was stubborn too.”

Ammar moved beneath Zaynab’s grip. She held him harder.

The old woman said, “You are welcome to search my house.”

“We are.”

The hatch shifted.

Dust fell through the cracks.

Zaynab had one bullet left in her rifle. The rifle was upstairs, propped beside the hearth. Her knife was at her ankle. Ammar had a kitchen blade. Two weapons against at least six men.

The hatch lifted an inch.

Then Sahlah screamed.

It was not the sound of an ordinary frightened horse. It was a shrill, enraged trumpet that filled the whole house. There came a crash, then a man shouting. Hooves struck wood. Another crash.

The hatch dropped.

“Catch that animal!” someone yelled.

Zaynab did not wait.

She pushed the hatch up, climbed out, and found the room in chaos. Sahlah had kicked through the lean-to wall and burst into the house. One rider was down against the far wall, clutching his face. Um Hatem stood near the door with the shotgun, smoke curling from its barrels.

Zaynab seized her rifle. Ammar scrambled after her.

“Out!” she shouted.

They ran through the broken lean-to into the yard. Sahlah was limping but alive. Zaynab swung into the saddle and reached down for Ammar.

He did not take her hand.

Um Hatem stood in the doorway, shotgun empty, her white hair loose around her face. Two armed men were coming around the side of the house.

“Go!” she shouted.

Ammar looked at her.

“Go, you little idiot!”

He grabbed Zaynab’s wrist, and she hauled him up behind her.

They rode hard through the dead fields as bullets snapped after them.

They did not stop until the house had vanished behind the rise of the land.

By then, Ammar was crying silently against Zaynab’s back.

She did not tell him not to.

At midday, they reached the Shrine of Daughters, a cluster of weathered stone domes built over graves no one remembered. There, sheltering under a collapsed arch, they found a man waiting.

He wore Harith’s red scarf.

Ammar drew the kitchen knife.

The man lifted both hands.

“Do not,” Zaynab said.

“He is one of them,” Ammar whispered.

“I can see that.”

The man was middle-aged, broad-shouldered, with a gray beard and a rifle slung across his back. His red scarf was faded nearly pink. He looked at Zaynab, then at Sahlah, then at the boy.

“You are difficult to catch,” he said.

Zaynab raised her rifle. “You have made the effort.”

“My name is Faraj.”

“I did not ask.”

“I was sent to bring you alive.”

“And?”

“And I am tired of being sent.”

Ammar spat into the dust.

Faraj glanced at him. “Your uncle is alive.”

The boy went pale.

“Where?”

“At the cement works.”

“You lie.”

“I have lied for Harith for six years. I know the difference.”

Zaynab kept the rifle level. “Why tell us?”

Faraj looked at the ruined shrine. “Because yesterday I watched a man beat an old woman for two cups of water. Because this morning I watched Captain Mahmud order three houses burned to find a parcel he has never seen. Because Harith says he rules for order, and every month there is less order and more hunger.”

“You expect gratitude?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

“The thing you carry. What is it?”

“I do not know.”

He laughed once, dryly. “That may be true. Harith’s men do not believe it.”

“What does Harith believe?”

Faraj’s gaze went toward the north, where Bir al-Sidr lay beyond two more days of hard country.

“He believes it can wake the wells.”

Ammar looked at Zaynab.

“Wake them?” she said.

“There are pumping stations under the old salt basin. Built before the wars. Most are dead. But Bir al-Sidr has one that still breathes, they say. It draws deep water and cleans it.”

“Then why does Bir al-Sidr need this package?”

Faraj shook his head. “I only know what men whisper. Musa al-Nuri was chief engineer at Qasr al-Raml before he disappeared. Harith thinks Musa kept the key to the basin controls.”

Zaynab thought of the old man’s shaking hand.

Something that must reach Bir al-Sidr.

Not a map. Not a letter.

A key.

Faraj stepped aside from the archway.

“The northern road is watched,” he said. “There is a dry aqueduct that runs west. Follow it until you see three black hills. Then turn toward the smallest. There is a gate below it.”

“Why help us?”

“Because I cannot leave with you.”

“You could.”

He smiled without pleasure. “My sons are at Harith’s camp.”

Zaynab lowered the rifle by an inch.

Faraj pulled the red scarf from his neck. He tossed it at her feet.

“Take the horse tracks east first,” he said. “Make them think you went toward Marwah.”

Then he walked away through the graves, unarmed except for the rifle on his back.

Ammar watched him go.

“Will they kill him?” he asked.

“Yes,” Zaynab said.

They followed the aqueduct.

It was a narrow raised channel of old concrete, broken in places, winding across the flats like the spine of some buried beast. By sunset, they saw the three black hills.

Behind them, on the horizon, dust rose in a long red-brown plume.

Riders.

Zaynab urged Sahlah onward.

The smallest hill was not a hill at all but a mound of rubble covering the entrance to an underground station. At its base stood a steel door half concealed by thorn bushes. Beside it was a square panel of dark glass.

No handle. No lock.

Ammar jumped down and wiped sand from the panel.

“There,” he said.

On the glass, barely visible, was the outline of a hand.

Zaynab took off her boot.

Her fingers shook as she pried out the wooden heel. The package had become warm from her body, the oilcloth stained with sweat.

Ammar stood close beside her.

“Open it,” he said.

This time, she did.

The wax came away in brittle flakes.

Inside the oilcloth was a metal case no larger than a man’s palm. It was made of dull silver, with a narrow slot along one edge. No keyhole. No writing except a single name etched in tiny letters:

MUSA AL-NURI.

Beneath the case lay a folded piece of paper.

Zaynab opened it.

The handwriting was cramped but steady.

Zaynab,

If this has reached you, forgive an old man his caution. The case contains an activation wafer for the Sidr Basin purifier. It will run for twelve years if the station can be brought online. It will not make water from nothing, nor feed every village, nor mend what has been broken. But it can give clean water to more people than Harith can easily starve.

My sister knows the work. Trust her, if she is still alive.

Musa.

That was all.

Ammar read over her shoulder.

“Twelve years,” he said.

“Yes.”

“That is why they want it.”

“Yes.”

The dust plume drew closer.

Zaynab looked at the handprint panel. “What do we do?”

Ammar held out the metal case.

There was no place to insert it, no visible seam.

Then, as his thumb brushed the narrow slot, the case clicked open.

Inside was a thin black wafer, translucent at the edges. It caught the last light of the sun and seemed to hold a blue glow deep within it.

Ammar lifted it carefully.

The panel beside the door lit up.

Words appeared in pale green script.

AUTHORIZED ENGINEER REQUIRED.

Then another line.

PLACE ACTIVATION WAFER.

A slot opened beneath the handprint.

Zaynab heard riders in the distance.

“Do it,” she said.

Ammar slid the wafer into the slot.

For one moment, nothing happened.

Then the earth groaned.

The steel door shuddered and rolled sideways into the hill. Cold air breathed out from the darkness below.

Inside, lights flickered awake one after another, descending into the ground.

Ammar stared.

Zaynab grabbed his arm. “Move.”

They led Sahlah into the station just as the first shots came from the ridge.

Bullets struck sparks from the door frame.

Zaynab found a lever inside and pulled it. The door began to close, slow as an old man rising from prayer.

A rider appeared beyond the narrowing gap, red scarf flying, rifle raised.

Zaynab fired her last bullet.

She did not know whether she hit him. The door sealed with a heavy metallic boom, and the world outside vanished.

For a while, there was only darkness, dust, and Sahlah’s frightened breathing.

Then the station lights steadied.

They stood at the head of a long stairway descending beneath the hill. Far below, machines hummed. Pipes ran along the walls, thick as tree trunks. The air smelled of metal, wet stone, and something clean Zaynab had almost forgotten: water.

A voice crackled from a speaker overhead.

“Who has activated Station Seven?”

Ammar looked at Zaynab.

She looked down at the empty metal case in her hand.

“My name is Zaynab bint Karim,” she said. “I have a delivery for Bir al-Sidr.”

There was a pause.

Then the voice said, very softly, “Musa sent you.”

At the bottom of the stairs waited a woman in a blue work coat, gray-haired and straight-backed. Beside her stood three other people, all armed, all staring at the mare, the dust-covered boy, and the courier with one boot in her hand.

The woman came forward.

“I am Layla al-Nuri,” she said. “My brother is alive?”

Zaynab looked at the old engineer’s note.

“Yes,” she said. “He said to trust you.”

Layla closed her eyes.

For a moment, the hard lines of her face loosened. Then she reached out for the empty case, held it against her chest, and began giving orders.

The station woke around them.

Water thundered somewhere deep below. Old pumps coughed, shuddered, and turned. A bank of glass gauges brightened one by one. Through a window at the end of the chamber, Zaynab saw a vast underground reservoir, black and shining beneath the lights.

Ammar stood beside her, silent.

“What happens now?” he asked.

Layla did not look up from the controls.

“Now,” she said, “we see whether the pipes still reach the villages.”

Outside, Harith’s riders pounded on the sealed door until long after midnight.

Inside, clean water began to flow north through pipes buried beneath the Red March.
