Title: claude_sonnet55_story02

================================================================================

The coffin had flown from Turin in the hold of a plane, and Agata Lo Cascio had signed for it at the cargo terminal in Palermo at half past one. By three they were on the state road climbing into the interior, the hearse humming in the heat and the dead woman's daughter in the passenger seat, holding a handbag in her lap like a small animal she had been asked to look after.

Agata did not usually let the family ride up front. Her father had never allowed it. The family followed in their own car, he used to say, so that everyone had a little distance to keep their face in. But Signora Rosalba Randazzo had come off the plane with no car and no one to meet her. She was sixty-eight, in a navy dress that was too warm for August, and she had looked at the long black Mercedes and then at Agata and said, "Can I come with her? I've never left her alone in a vehicle."

So she came, and she talked. She talked about Turin, where her mother had lived thirty-one years in a flat on Via Nizza and never once admitted the bread was good. She talked about the pension office, and the priest who had said the funeral Mass in a Piedmontese accent so thick her mother would have walked out. The last wish, repeated every Christmas for a decade, had been Montalbo, the family plot under the cypresses, beside a husband dead since 1971. Nobody had thought to ask whether anyone remained in Montalbo to open the gate.

"Turi Impellizzeri," Agata said. "The gravedigger. He's expecting us by five. The cemetery closes at six, but he'll wait."

"You've spoken to him?"

"Three times."

Signora Randazzo nodded and looked out at the burnt fields, the yellow stubble, the wind turbines turning slowly on the ridges like something being decided. "You have a calm voice," she said. "On the phone. I thought, this one won't make it worse."

Agata drove. She had been told this before, in nearly those words, and she had stopped hearing it as praise. It was a quality, like being tall. Eleven years ago she had not had it. Eleven years ago, if someone had said *calm* about her, she would have taken it as an insult and thrown something.

---

The village appeared at twenty past four, a heap of ochre houses on a hill, a bell tower, a green banner strung across the entrance road. FESTA DI MARIA SS. ASSUNTA. She had forgotten the date. Or she had known it and filed it where she filed things that could not be helped, because the funeral trade in Sicily lives alongside the calendar of saints and has learned not to argue with it.

The road went in under the banner and stopped. Not figuratively. Just past the first houses a line of cars was pulled up nose to tail, and beyond them a crowd filled the street from wall to wall. Over their heads, in the white heat, she could see the Madonna: a plaster Virgin on a litter, rocking forward on the shoulders of twenty men, her blue mantle stiff with gold thread and pinned banknotes. Behind her a brass band was playing something slow and wildly out of tune.

"Oh," said Signora Randazzo. "Oh, no."

"It'll move."

"Does it move?"

"It goes around the village and returns to the church. An hour. Maybe a little more."

"Turi closes at six."

"Turi waits," Agata said, and heard her own calm voice come out of her mouth, level as a table. She turned off the engine. The hearse ticked in the heat.

A man in an orange vest was walking along the line of cars, rapping on windows and gesturing, *back up, back up, park there, nobody through*. He was a big man, heavy through the shoulders and belly, with a sunburnt scalp under a cap and a loud, ruined voice, the voice of someone who spent his days shouting over machinery. He was joking with the drivers as he went. She heard him say something in dialect about a mother-in-law, and two men laughed. He reached the hearse, slapped the roof once, lightly, the way you would pat a horse, and bent to the driver's window.

"Signora, no chance, you can see for yourself, the Madonna has right of way, even over the—"

He stopped.

She had been looking at his hand on the door frame. The left one was missing the top joint of the first two fingers, ending in smooth, shiny pads. Then she looked at his face and saw nothing she knew in it, nothing at all: the jowls, the little eyes pushed into the flesh, the white stubble. And then he did something with his mouth, a short pull to the left, as though a thread had caught on a tooth, and she knew it. That was how Nino Spadaro had used to begin a sentence he wasn't sure he was allowed to finish.

She was biting the inside of her cheek. She made herself stop.

"Agata," he said.

His voice had dropped. For a moment it was nearly the voice she remembered, which had been soft, slightly nasal, and apologized before it spoke.

"Nino."

"*Madonna mia.*" He took his cap off and put it back on. "What are you doing in a... What is that? Is that a hearse?"

"It's a hearse."

"Is that—" He looked past her at the woman in navy, and his face rearranged itself with a speed she found almost admirable. "Signora, my condolences. I'm so sorry. I didn't see."

"Thank you," said Signora Randazzo, who was looking from one of them to the other with the pained alertness of a person who has stumbled on a private matter.

"We have to get to the cemetery by six," Agata said.

"The cemetery." He turned and looked at the crowd, the Madonna, the band. He seemed to count something. "The cemetery's on the Via Calvario, the far side. There's no way around the procession from here. Not for this." He tapped the roof of the hearse again, not lightly this time. Then he said, "Give me ten minutes."

---

She watched him go up the street, shouldering through the crowd, and thought: *he doesn't recognize me either.* It was true that he had said her name. But he had said it as if it were a wrong answer he'd given to a different question, and the face he had looked at, her face, had come to him from a long way off.

She had cut her hair eight years ago. It had been black and down to her waist; it was now short and iron-gray at the sides. She wore a black suit and low black shoes and a small gold cross that had been her mother's, which she did not believe in but wore because families looked for it. At twenty-two she had had a ring in her nose and a bad opinion of everyone over thirty, and she'd had, he used to say, the walk of someone being chased. She no longer walked like that. She walked like someone who has been asked to carry something heavy across a room and doesn't want to spill it.

"Who is he?" said Signora Randazzo.

"Someone I knew in Palermo. At university."

"A friend."

"Yes."

Signora Randazzo let that sit for a moment, and then said, with great delicacy, "He does not seem to have enjoyed seeing you."

"He was surprised."

"Mm."

In the heat, with the band droning, Agata remembered a night in 2001 on the roof of a student building in Via Maqueda. Nino had been reading her a poem he'd written about a drowned fisherman. The poem was bad, she thought, though she'd said it was beautiful. Someone in the street below had set off a *cipollotto*, one of those cheap firecrackers, and he had flinched so hard he'd dropped the page off the roof. He had gone white and then apologized. He apologized for flinching. He explained, in a voice that seemed to have no bottom, that his grandfather had made fireworks in a shed outside Bagheria and that, as a boy, he had stood too close once when a battery went off wrong. After that he could not stand the noise, any noise that came without warning, a door, a balloon. He said it was the one cowardly thing about him. She'd said no, it wasn't cowardly, it was a weakness, and she'd kissed his ear. She'd been so sure of the difference.

Then there had been Milan, the firm that offered her a job, the apartment on the Naviglio she'd already half-rented. *You'll come*, she'd said. *In the autumn. When you've finished.* And he had said yes. She had gone, and he had not come, and in the spring he had written a letter, not a long one, that said only that his father had been hurt and he had to go home to Bagheria for a while. The "while" had gone on, apparently, until she'd stopped asking. She had built, in her mind, a plain and bitter account: he'd been afraid. He had wanted a reason, and his father's hand had been that reason. Poets stay home.

Eleven years of her life had been arranged around that account without her ever once checking it.

---

He came back in nine minutes, in a white van with SPADARO — ARTIFICI E SPETTACOLI PIROTECNICI on the side in red letters and a painted starburst. He pulled alongside and leaned across the passenger seat to roll the window down.

"There's a lane behind the Franciscans'. It's tight, you'll scrape your mirrors, but it comes out above the cemetery. I'll go in front. Stay close. Some of the boys are moving a truck."

"Is it safe for the hearse?"

"I'll walk it myself if you want."

"Nino."

"What?"

She didn't know what. "Thank you."

He looked at her for a second longer than was useful. Then he put the van in gear.

They went up through a gap between two houses she would have said was a wall, and the Mercedes' mirrors folded in with a tick like a pair of knuckles. Signora Randazzo held her handbag and said the rosary under her breath, a Piedmontese rosary, brisk and unembroidered. Cats scattered from the doorsteps. A woman on a balcony shook out a tablecloth and watched the hearse go by with the flat interest of someone seeing a fact confirmed.

At the top of the lane, a flatbed truck stood across the way, loaded with long cardboard tubes and wooden frames, and four men were pushing it with their shoulders while a fifth steered. Nino was out of the van, directing with both arms, bellowing. "*Ahi, ahi, piano!* Not that way, you animal, you'll take the wall with you!" The truck groaned through. One of the men glanced at the hearse and made the sign of the cross with a cigarette in his hand. Then they were through, and the road opened out in front of them, white in the sun, and the cypresses stood up at the end of it.

---

Turi Impellizzeri was waiting by the gate in a blue work shirt, a cigarette behind his ear and his hands folded in front of him in a manner that told her he'd been rehearsing the posture. The grave was open in the Randazzo plot, a tidy dark rectangle with the earth heaped under a green tarp. A priest was there, too, a boy of perhaps thirty, sweating into his surplice, who had been sent by the parish because the old priest was walking behind the Madonna. It was five-forty.

They did it quickly and well. That was the other thing Agata had learned: that speed and tenderness were not opposites, that you could be fast and still let a person feel the moment had been waiting for them. She and Turi and two of Nino's men, who had followed the van up the hill without being asked and stood now with their hands clasped and their caps off, carried the coffin from the hearse to the gravesite. Signora Randazzo walked behind it with her back very straight. The priest said what priests say. At the words *requiem aeternam*, Signora Randazzo made a small sound, not weeping, more the sound of a held breath being let out through a narrow gap. Agata, standing a pace behind her, put one hand flat between her shoulder blades. She did it without deciding to. Signora Randazzo leaned back into it, very slightly, for the length of one breath.

Nino stood a little apart, by a stone angel with no nose, with his cap in his missing-fingered hand. He did not look at Agata. He looked at the coffin with an expression she had not seen on him before, and which, she thought, might have been the first one in the afternoon that he hadn't put on for someone's benefit.

They lowered the coffin. Turi took up the shovel.

And from the village, a long way off and below, came a flat enormous clap, then another, then a rolling thunder that came up the hillside and shook the cypress needles.

The priest flinched. Turi swore under his breath and crossed himself. Signora Randazzo lifted her head.

"What's that?"

"The fireworks," said Nino. He had gone grey under his sunburn. "Madonna. They weren't meant to start until nine. That's Cosimo, that idiot, I told him *nine*..." He was already moving. "Forgive me, Signora. I'm sorry. I'll stop it."

"No," said Signora Randazzo.

He stopped.

"Don't." She was looking down the hill, to where smoke had begun to unfurl, lazily, over the rooftops, white against the bleached sky. A second salvo went up. Even in daylight you could see the shells open, flecks of color scattering without much light behind them, pale and bright like torn paper. "She loved this. When I was little she used to take me up on the roof in August, in Montalbo. Every year. The whole village on the roofs. She said it was the only time God was loud enough to be heard." She almost laughed. "Leave it. It's fine. She'd have wanted it."

So they finished with the village exploding quietly beneath them. Turi shoveled, the earth ticked on the lid, and the priest, who had stopped trying to compete, simply stood with his book closed. Agata watched Nino. At each report, a muscle in his jaw jumped. His eyes shut a fraction of a second before the sound and opened a fraction after. It had not gone away. Whatever had been done to him in his grandfather's shed was still there under all that weight and noise, and he had walked straight into it, and stayed in it, and let it hurt every day for a living.

She understood then, with a clarity that was not comfortable, what she had never let herself think. He hadn't stayed because he was afraid. He had gone back to the thing he was afraid of.

---

Afterward, Signora Randazzo went to sit in the shade of the cemetery wall with Turi, who produced a thermos of coffee and two small glasses from the pocket of his work trousers, and who seemed to regard the widow's patience as a personal honor. The priest left. Nino's men drifted down toward the village.

Agata found herself standing at the hearse with the rear door open, wiping her hands on a cloth, and Nino a few feet away.

"I didn't know you did this," he said. "Funerals."

"My father died. Six years ago. Someone had to."

"I'm sorry. I liked him. He gave me *sfincione* once and told me I was too thin to be a poet." The mouth did its pull to the left. "He was right."

"He's the one who told you that?"

"He told me many things. You weren't there for most of them."

It wasn't unkind. That was the thing. She waited for it to be, and it wasn't.

"Your father," she said. "The letter. You said he was hurt."

"Yes."

"I thought—" She stopped. "I thought you'd found an excuse."

Nino looked at her for a while. The sky behind him went on flowering and fading, sound arriving after the light, late and heavy.

"His hand," he said. "Both hands, the right one worse. A shell went in the mortar and didn't clear. He was looking down the tube to see why." He held up his own left hand and regarded the stumps with mild interest, as if they were something he had bought secondhand. "Mine was later. My own stupidity. I was thirty, I was angry, I wasn't paying attention. I won't say it was an excuse, Agata. It was a job. Someone had to do it. There were eleven men on the payroll."

"You hated it."

"I did. I do, a bit. Every time." He shrugged. "But I'm good at it. I never thought I would be. You'd be surprised how much of it is poetry, the order you fire things in, what you let the dark do between them. That's the part nobody sees. The silence is where the shape is." He stopped, embarrassed. "Listen to me."

"No. Go on."

"There's nothing to go on with." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I didn't recognize you. Did you notice? I stood there at the window and I was explaining the right of way to some stranger in a black suit with a priest's voice."

"I don't have a priest's voice."

"You have a voice like a closed door, a good one, that you want to knock on gently." He laughed, short. "Twenty years ago you'd have gotten out of that hearse and started a fight with the Madonna."

"I'd have won."

"You would have." He was smiling now, truly, and it changed his face. For an instant the jowls and the sunburn were only weather, and underneath was the boy on the roof. "I thought about you, you know. Not constantly. That would be a lie. But sometimes, when I was setting up a *finale*, I'd think, Agata would find this vulgar."

"I would."

"Yes."

"I probably would find it vulgar."

"You'd say so in front of everyone."

"I would."

They stood there. Down in the village the fireworks had gone quiet, the afternoon salvo done, and the band had struck up again somewhere, tuba and cymbals, wavering up the hill.

"What happened to your hair?" he said.

"I got tired of it."

"It was a lot of hair."

"It was."

"It suits you, I think. I can't tell. It's like meeting your own handwriting." He looked at the cross at her throat, at the black shoes. "Do you still run into the sea with your clothes on?"

She had not thought about it in years, not once. It was a thing she'd done on the beach at Mondello in March, in 2000, in a green dress, shouting at him to follow, and he had stood on the sand holding her shoes.

"No," she said. And then, because it was true and because she could think of no reason to hold it back: "I took my shoes off in Cefalù two summers ago. At a funeral party. The family went swimming and I put my feet in the water up to the knees. Nobody saw."

"That's something."

"It's not much."

"It's something," he said again, and it was the old voice, soft and nasal and apologizing for nothing.

Across the cemetery, Signora Randazzo had risen, brushing off her skirt. Turi was packing the thermos. Agata folded the cloth and put it in the back of the hearse and shut the door, and the click was loud in the stillness.

"I have to drive her to her cousin's," she said. "Then back to Palermo. It's three hours."

"Of course."

"You'll be busy tonight."

"I will. Nine o'clock. Forty minutes, the big show. The *finale* is the best part, if I say so." He hesitated. "Stay, if you like. You could watch from the roof of the sacristy. The boys put chairs up. It's the best seat, you can see everything, and you can only hear half of it."

It occurred to her that this was an invitation made with great care by a man who knew exactly how much it cost to make it, and that she could answer it with the closed-door voice, the courteous one. She tried the sentence in her head. *Thank you, but I have the drive.* It was a perfectly good sentence. She had said it, in different words, to a hundred people.

Down the road, beyond the cypresses, the Madonna was starting back toward the church, a blue speck above the heads, and a puff of smoke from some stray rocket rose after her like an afterthought.

"Which half would I hear?" Agata said.

"The second," said Nino. "The quiet one."

She looked at him. She was biting the inside of her cheek again, and this time she let herself.

"I'll take the signora to her cousin," she said. "I'll be back by half past eight."

"There's a chair," he said. "I'll keep one."

He walked off down the hill toward his van with his heavy, slightly rolling step, and she watched him go. In the shade of the wall, Signora Randazzo gave her a long look, and then, with enormous tact, turned to Turi and asked him about his grandchildren.

Agata got into the hearse. The seat was hot through her trousers. In the mirror, the cypresses, the white road, the gate that Turi was closing; in the empty passenger seat, where the handbag had been, a small crumb of earth from the signora's shoe. She started the engine and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, listening to the tick of the metal cooling, and to the bells in the village, which had begun again, just slightly out of time with one another.

Then she drove down to collect the widow.
