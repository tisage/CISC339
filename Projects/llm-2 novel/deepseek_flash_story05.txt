The forge was the first thing awake in Hrafndal. Before the cows, before the gulls, before the light had climbed the eastern ridge and spilled down into the valley, Halla Eiríksdóttir had her fire going. She had learned to wake before the fire, to feel the cold of the floorboards on her feet, to pull on her boots and her wool coat and step out into the dark, and by the time she reached the forge the coals were still alive under their blanket of ash, and she would coax them back to life with a breath and a handful of birch bark and a few sticks of kindling.

Her hands knew the work. They had known it for twenty years. She had learned at her father’s side, and he had learned at his father’s, and the forge in Hrafndal had stood at the edge of the village, by the stream, for four generations. The walls were black with soot, the floor packed earth, the anvil worn smooth in the center where a thousand hammers had struck. She loved the smell of it—iron and smoke and the sharp tang of the quench trough. She loved the sound of the bellows, the sigh and roar of the fire. She did not love the winter, but she loved the forge in winter, the way it held heat against the dark.

She was thirty-eight years old. Her hair was the color of ash and she wore it braided tight against her head. Her arms were strong and her hands were scarred, the palms calloused, the knuckles thick. She had a face that people called handsome rather than pretty, and a way of looking at you that made you want to tell the truth. She had been a widow for six years. Her husband, Bjarni, had been a fisherman. He had gone out one morning in a small boat and the sea had taken him, and she had never remarried. She did not think of herself as lonely. She had the work.

The village of Hrafndal lay in a valley that ran down to a fjord, and in the winter the sun rose late and set early, and the days were short and blue. There were forty farms, a church, a trading post that opened in the spring, and a chieftain named Óttar who was old and tired. The people here were farmers and fishermen and shepherds, and they came to Halla for their knives and their axes and their plow blades and their horseshoes. She made nails and hinges and hooks and buckets. She did not make weapons. This was known.

She had made a weapon once, when she was young, before she knew better. It had been a spearhead, for a man who was going to a war in the south. She had been proud of it. It was beautiful. The man had taken it and gone, and she had never heard what became of it. Her father had told her: a smith’s work goes out into the world and does what it does, and you cannot follow it. He had made a sword once, long ago, for a chieftain in the next valley. The chieftain had used it in a feud, and the feud had killed Halla’s mother. Halla had been five years old. She did not remember her mother’s face. She remembered only the empty place at the table, and her father’s silence, and the way he had never made another weapon. She had kept his rule. It was not a rule she spoke about. It was simply a thing she did not do.

On the morning the girl came, Halla was working on a knife for Einar, her brother’s son. Einar was twelve, and he had been coming to the forge for two years, learning to work the bellows, learning to hold the tongs, learning to watch. He was a good boy, patient and quiet. He had a knack for it. This morning he was at the bellows, and the fire was white-hot, and Halla was drawing out a blade from a piece of old iron, a knife for scaling fish. She had promised it to him for his birthday.

She heard the door open, but she did not look up. She was used to people coming and going. She heard footsteps, then a voice, a girl’s voice, young and tired. “Are you the smith?”

Halla finished the stroke, turned the iron, and looked up. The girl was standing just inside the door, and the cold came in with her. She was thin, with dark hair and a face that was too old for its years. She wore a cloak that had been good once, and boots that were worn through. She carried a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She was maybe seventeen. She looked like she had walked a long way.

“I am,” Halla said.

“I need a sword,” the girl said.

The fire hissed. Einar stopped working the bellows. Halla set down her hammer.

“I don’t make swords,” she said.

The girl did not move. “They told me in the village. They said you’re the best smith in the valley. They said you can work anything.”

“I can work anything,” Halla said. “I choose not to make weapons.”

“Then you’re a coward,” the girl said.

Halla looked at her. There was no anger in the girl’s face, only a kind of desperation. She was not trying to insult her. She was stating a fact.

“What’s your name?” Halla asked.

“Ingunn. Ingunn Þórólfsdóttir. I come from Steindal.”

“I know Steindal,” Halla said. It was two valleys over, a day’s walk, maybe more in winter. She had been there once, years ago, to shoe a horse. “What happened to you?”

Ingunn did not answer. She set the bundle on the floor and unwrapped it. Inside was a lump of metal, roughly the size of a fist, dark and heavy. It was not like any iron Halla had ever seen. It had a sheen to it, a faint shimmer, like oil on water. And when Ingunn touched it, it seemed to give off a warmth, a subtle heat that was not the heat of the forge.

“My father found it,” Ingunn said. “A year ago, in the spring. It fell from the sky. There was a light, and a sound like thunder, and it struck the hill above our farm and made a hole in the ground. He dug it out. He said it was a gift from the gods. He said it would bring us luck.” She paused. “It didn’t.”

“What happened?” Halla asked, though she already knew. She had heard rumors, even in Hrafndal. The killing in Steindal, the dispute over land. The law had done nothing.

“Ketill,” Ingunn said. “Ketill killed him. Over the grazing land. He had men with him, and my father had no one. I have a brother. He’s ten. I am the only one left who can do anything.” She looked at Halla. “I want a sword. I want to make Ketill pay.”

Halla was silent for a long moment. She looked at the girl, at the worn boots, the too-thin frame, the set of her jaw. She thought of her mother, dead in a feud. She thought of the sword her father had made, the sword that had done the killing. She thought of the way a thing made in fire goes out into the world and does what it does.

“No,” Halla said.

Ingunn did not plead. She did not cry. She looked at Halla, and something in her face closed, like a door. She picked up the bundle and wrapped it again. “Then I will find someone else,” she said. “Or I will do it myself.” She turned and walked out into the snow.

Einar said, quietly, “She looked cold.”

Halla picked up her hammer. “Get back to the bellows,” she said. But she did not strike the iron. She stood for a moment, listening to the wind, and then she went to the door and looked out. The girl was walking down the path, a small dark figure against the white valley. She walked slowly, like someone who had nowhere to go. Halla watched her until she disappeared behind the trees.

She went back to the anvil. She finished the knife. But her mind was not on it. She kept seeing the girl’s face, and the lump of metal, and the way it had seemed to hold the light.

That night, she dreamed of the sky. She was standing in a field, and above her the stars were falling, slow and silent, like snow. One of them fell toward her, and it was not a star but a stone, and it was warm in her hand. She woke with the taste of iron in her mouth.

Two days later, Ingunn came back.

She was thinner, if that was possible. The snow had turned to sleet, and she was wet and shivering. She stood in the doorway of the forge and did not speak. She just held out the bundle. Her hands were shaking.

“I can’t take it back,” she said. “I tried. I went to the smith in the next valley, and he wouldn’t touch it. He said it was cursed. I went to the priest, and he said to pray. I prayed. It didn’t help.” She looked at Halla. “I hear it. At night. I hear it calling. It wants to be something. It wants to be made.”

Halla took the bundle. She did not mean to. Her hands reached for it before she had decided. The metal was warm, warmer than it should have been, and when she touched it she felt a strange vibration, like a plucked string, like a heartbeat. She had never felt anything like it.

“It’s not cursed,” Halla said. “It’s just metal. It’s from the sky. It’s different.”

“Then make it,” Ingunn said. “Make it into something. I don’t care what. I just want it to stop.”

Halla looked at the girl. She thought of her father, and the rule, and the empty place at the table. She thought of Bjarni, and the sea, and the silence that had filled her house after he was gone. She thought of the way a thing made in fire goes out into the world.

“Come in,” she said. “You’re freezing.”

She gave the girl a bowl of soup and a place by the fire. She let her sleep in the loft. And the next morning, she began to work the star-iron.

She did not know why she did it. She told herself it was to help the girl, to give her something to focus on, to keep her from doing something foolish. She told herself it was the metal itself, the challenge of it, the way it resisted and rang and seemed to want a shape. She told herself she would make something harmless—a knife, a sickle, a brooch. But she knew, even then, that it would be a sword. The metal wanted to be a sword.

The work was hard. The star-iron was not like bog iron, which was soft and forgiving. It was dense and stubborn. It did not want to heat, and when it did heat it did not want to move. It rang under the hammer with a sound that was almost a voice, a low hum that seemed to come from inside the metal. Halla worked it for three days. She folded it, and folded it, and folded it again, and each time the pattern that emerged was finer, more complex, like the grain of a tree that had grown in a strange climate. She barely slept. She ate little. She stood at the anvil and struck the iron, and the iron struck back.

On the second night, she dreamed again. She was in the forge, but the forge was in the sky, and the stars were all around her, and the anvil was a cliff of stone. A voice spoke. It was not a human voice. It was a pressure, a warmth, a whisper in her bones. It said: Make me.

She woke with her hands aching. She went to the forge and worked.

On the third day, the sword took shape. It was a long blade, double-edged, straight, with a fuller down the center. The pattern in the steel was like the night sky, dark with points of light. She made the hilt from bog oak and brass, and she wrapped the grip in leather. She quenched the blade in oil, then in water. When the hot steel hit the water, the water hissed and boiled and turned black, and a sound came out of the trough, a sound like a sigh, like a breath being released after a long time. The fire in the forge flared and then sank. The sword was done.

It was the most beautiful thing Halla had ever made. It was also wrong. She knew it the moment she held it. It was too light for its size, and too warm, and when she gripped the hilt she felt a kind of eagerness, a hunger, that had nothing to do with her. The sword wanted to be held. It wanted to be used.

Ingunn came down from the loft and saw it. Her eyes went wide. She reached for it, and Halla almost did not let her take it. But she did. The girl lifted the sword, and it fit her hand as if it had been made for her. She held it up, and the light from the forge ran along the blade, and the pattern in the steel seemed to move, like stars turning.

“It’s perfect,” Ingunn whispered.

“Be careful,” Halla said. “It’s not like other swords. It’s—” She did not know how to say it. “It has a will.”

Ingunn looked at her. “Good,” she said.

She left the next morning. Halla watched her go, the sword in a scabbard at her side, and she felt a coldness in her chest that had nothing to do with the winter.

She tried to go back to her work. She made a hinge. She mended a pot. But her thoughts kept going to Ingunn, and the sword, and the way it had felt in her hand. She began to hear things. A faint hum, at the edge of hearing, like a bee in a far field. It came to her at night, when she was lying in bed, and it seemed to come from the south, from Steindal. She told herself it was the wind. She told herself it was nothing.

A week later, a man came to Hrafndal with news. Ketill was dead. Ingunn Þórólfsdóttir had killed him. She had walked into his farm in the middle of the night and found him in his bed and cut him down. But that was not the worst of it. The worst was that she had kept cutting. She had cut him long after he was dead. His wife had found him in the morning, and the room had been—the man would not say the word. He said it looked like something wild had been there. Ingunn had fled. No one knew where she was.

Halla listened. She did not speak. She went back to her forge and stood in the dark and listened to the hum. It was louder now. It was not coming from the south anymore. It was closer.

Ingunn came back on the third night. She came to Halla’s door, and she was not the same girl. Her eyes were dark and bright, and she moved like someone who had not slept in days. She had the sword with her. She held it in her hand, not in its scabbard, as if she could not bear to let it go. Her knuckles were white on the grip.

“Take it,” she said. “Take it back. I don’t want it.”

Halla reached for the sword. Ingunn did not let go. Her hand tightened. She looked at the blade, and her face twisted, and she said, “I can’t. I can’t let it go. It won’t let me.”

Halla took hold of the blade, and the sword grew hot. It burned her palm. She did not let go. She pulled, and Ingunn cried out, and for a moment they were both holding it, and Halla felt the will of the thing, the hunger, the joy of being used. It wanted to be wielded. It wanted blood. It wanted her.

She wrenched it from the girl’s hand, and Ingunn fell back, sobbing, and Halla stood there with the sword in her hand. It was hot, and it was singing, a low thrumming note, and she felt it in her teeth, in her bones. She wanted to swing it. She wanted to see what it could do. She wanted—

She dropped it. It fell to the floor of the forge and rang like a bell. Ingunn was on her knees, her hands over her face.

“Go home,” Halla said. Her voice was hoarse. “Go back to Steindal. Forget it. Forget all of it.”

“I can’t,” Ingunn said. “It’s in my head. It talks to me.”

“It won’t, if you leave it here. Go. Now. Before I change my mind.”

Ingunn looked at her, and there was something like gratitude in her face, and something like pity. Then she got up and walked out into the dark, and Halla was alone with the sword.

She did not sleep that night. She sat by the fire and watched the sword on the floor. It lay there, beautiful and still. But she could feel it. It was not still. It was waiting. It was patient. It had all the time in the world.

In the morning, she tried to destroy it. She built up the fire until it was white-hot, hotter than she had ever made it, and she put the sword in the coals. It did not soften. It did not glow. It drank the heat and stayed dark. She took it out and laid it on the anvil and struck it with her heaviest hammer. The hammer bounced. The anvil cracked. A piece of it fell to the floor, and the sword was unmarked. She tried to break it over her knee. She tried to bury it. She tried to throw it in the stream. Each time, she could not do it. Not because it was hard, but because it would not let her. When she tried to let it go, her hand would not open. When she tried to turn away, her feet would not move. It was not a voice, not exactly. It was a desire, a pressure, a will that was stronger than her own.

On the third day, it spoke to her. She was standing at the anvil, and the sword was in her hand, and she had not meant to pick it up. She looked at it, and the pattern in the steel moved, and she saw Bjarni’s face. He was standing in the doorway of their house, the way he used to, with his wet hair and his half-smile. He said her name. He said: Come with me.

She knew it was not him. She knew it was the sword. But she wanted it to be him, and that wanting was enough. She stood there for a long time, holding the sword, looking at the ghost of her husband, and the sword said: I can give you what you want. I can give you anything. Just use me.

She thought of Ingunn. She thought of Ketill. She thought of her father, and the sword he had made, and the empty place at the table. She thought of the way a thing made in fire goes out into the world and does what it does. She thought of all the things she had made—the knives, the axes, the plow blades—and how they had gone out and done their work, and how she had never been able to follow them. She had made something, and now it wanted to make her.

She looked at the ghost of Bjarni, and she said, “You’re not him.”

The ghost smiled. It was not his smile. It was too knowing, too hungry. The sword grew warm in her hand.

She turned and walked out of the forge. She walked through the village, past the houses, past the church, past the frozen fields. She walked down the valley toward the fjord. The sword was in her hand, and it was warm, and it was singing, and she could feel it trying to turn her, trying to make her raise it, trying to find something to cut. She did not let it. She walked. The wind was cold, and the snow was deep, and her boots were wet, and she did not stop.

She reached the fjord at dusk. The water was black and still, and the mountains rose up on the other side, and the sky was the color of iron. She waded in. The cold took her breath, but she kept going, up to her waist, up to her chest. The sword was hot now, hotter than it had ever been. It burned her hand. It was trying to make her drop it, or maybe it was trying to make her keep it. She could not tell anymore. She could hear it screaming, a high thin note, like a wire about to break.

She thought of Bjarni. She thought of the sea, and how it had taken him, and how she had never been able to say goodbye. She thought of her mother, and her father, and the forge, and all the things she had made. She thought of the girl, Ingunn, and the life she might still have, if she could forget.

She opened her hand.

The sword fell. It hit the water and sank, and the water boiled around it for a moment, and then it was gone. The hum stopped. The cold came in, all at once, and Halla stood there, shivering, her hand blistered and bleeding, and she was alone. She waded back to shore. She fell to her knees on the frozen ground. She stayed there for a long time, until the stars came out.

She walked back to Hrafndal in the dark. She was half-frozen by the time she reached her door. She built up the fire and sat by it and did not sleep. In the morning, she went to the forge. She swept the floor. She mended the anvil as best she could. She took a piece of ordinary iron, soft and plain, and she began to make a plow blade. The hammer rang. The fire burned. It was the same as it had always been.

Einar came in at midday. He looked at the cracked anvil and the bandage on her hand, and he did not ask. He was a good boy. He went to the bellows and began to work.
