Title: claude_opus55_story02

================================================================================

The envelope was in the drawer of the altar table, under the incense, where his mother kept the things that mattered: the deed to the house, his father's medals, the receipt for the motorbike they had sold in 2009. It was a plain brown envelope, the kind the post office used to sell in packets of ten, and on the front, in her careful schoolroom hand, she had written *Chị Sáu*.

Bảo did not open it for eleven days. He didn't need to. He could feel the thickness of the bills through the paper. And he knew what it was for, or thought he did, because they were the last words his mother had said that made sense: *Còn tiền chị Sáu.* There's still Sáu's money.

After that she'd said other things, about the water buffalo at her uncle's house in Long An, about a dress she wanted to wear, about Bảo's father, dead nineteen years, who she said was waiting at the end of the alley with his bicycle. The fever did that near the end. Bảo had heard it from enough people by then to recognize it. He had held her hand and told her yes, Ba was waiting, and yes, the dress was ironed. Then the volunteers from Phổ Quang pagoda had come with their cart and their masks and their list, and he had walked behind the cart to the pagoda and watched, and come home with a ceramic jar he still couldn't bring himself to put on the altar beside his father's photo. It sat on the dining table where his mother used to sort morning glory greens.

That had been in the third month. Now it was the seventh, or the beginning of the eighth. The calendars had stopped meaning much. The electricity came on for two or three hours most nights, enough to charge a phone that had no one to call. The water still ran, which people said was because a handful of engineers at the Thủ Đức plant had simply refused to leave. Bảo believed it. You heard things like that now, small stubborn stories about people who kept doing their jobs after the jobs had stopped existing.

He had been a chemistry teacher at a high school on Nguyễn Kiệm Street. The school was still there. He had walked past it once, in the fifth month, and seen that someone had written on the gate in red paint, *KHÔNG CÓ AI*. No one here. He did not go past it again.

On the eleventh day he opened the envelope. There were twenty-three bills, mostly two-hundred-thousand notes, and some fifties, folded flat and old. Four million six hundred thousand đồng. Before the fever, it would have been a month's rent on a decent room, or a good used motorbike's down payment. Now it would buy nothing at all. In the markets that had come back, around Phú Nhuận and up near Gò Vấp, people traded rice for batteries, batteries for cooking oil, cooking oil for antibiotics, which no one had. Paper money was used to start cooking fires.

Behind the bills was a small school notebook with a blue cover, the kind with a cartoon cat on the front. He didn't look inside it. He put everything back in the envelope, put the envelope in the canvas bag he used to carry exam papers, and went out to find Sáu.

---

He knew almost nothing about her. This was the first problem, and he walked the length of the alley before he admitted how large it was.

She sold lottery tickets. He knew that. For years, as long as he could remember, every afternoon around four, a thin voice had come down the alley: *Vé số đây, vé số Kiến Thiết đây*. His mother would go out to the gate with a ten-thousand note and come back with a ticket, and sometimes she wouldn't come back for half an hour, and he would hear the two women talking in the low, rising rhythm of the countryside. He had never paid attention. He had been marking papers. He had been on his phone. He had been thirty-four, then thirty-six, then thirty-eight, living in his dead father's house with his mother because it was cheaper, and because it was easier, and because neither of them had ever brought up the idea that he might do otherwise.

What else? She was from Bến Tre. His mother had said so, once, when she brought home a bag of coconut candy. She had a bad hip; she walked with one shoulder lower than the other. She wore a cloth hat, not a nón lá, a soft one, pale green. She was older than his mother, because his mother called her *chị*, older sister. And Sáu wasn't a name at all. In the South, it meant only that she was the fifth child born to her parents, counting from Hai, because the eldest was always Hai. There were probably ten thousand women in Saigon called Sáu. There had been.

The alley was quiet. Of the thirty-one houses, he knew of four with people still living in them. Old Mr. Toàn at number 9, who had survived by never leaving his house at all and now refused to believe the fever was over. The Hứa family at 22, a mother and two teenage sons, who grew vegetables in Styrofoam boxes on their roof. A young couple at 27 he'd never been introduced to. And himself.

He knocked at 22. Mrs. Hứa came to the gate with a trowel in her hand.

"The lottery woman," she said. "With the hip. Yes, I remember her. My husband used to buy from her." She looked down at the trowel. "I haven't seen her since the beginning. Since the second month, maybe. She kept coming for a while, you know. Even after they closed the draws. My boys used to laugh at her."

"Do you know where she lived?"

"No idea. Why?"

He didn't know how to answer that. He touched the canvas bag. "My mother owed her money."

Mrs. Hứa gave him the look that people gave each other now, the look that held a whole conversation without anyone having to speak. Then she said, "She used to sit outside the pharmacy at the corner of Phan Đình Phùng. In the shade, when it was hot. Ask Hiển."

---

Hiển was still at the pharmacy. He had been the pharmacist there before, a fat man with gold-rimmed glasses, and now he was a thin man with gold-rimmed glasses who sat inside the steel shutter, which he kept rolled halfway up, with a machete across his knees. The shelves behind him were empty. Everyone knew they were empty. He sat there anyway.

"Sáu Bến Tre," he said. "Sure. She sat right there." He pointed with the machete at a patch of pavement under the tamarind tree, where there was nothing now but dry leaves and a plastic stool turned on its side. "Twelve years she sat there. Every day after lunch. She'd buy her tickets in the morning from the agent, walk the neighborhood till noon, sit here through the heat, then walk again till they were gone."

"Do you know where she lived?"

"District 8, I think. Somewhere along a canal. She took the bus home." He took his glasses off and rubbed his eyes. "She told me once she'd been doing this since 1994. Since her husband drowned. Can you imagine? Walking, every day, for thirty years. And her hip like that."

"Do you know her name? Her real name?"

Hiển thought about it, frowning. "No," he said finally, and seemed surprised. "Twelve years. I gave her free paracetamol for that hip. I never asked." He put his glasses back on. "The agent would know. Quách. He had the agency on Huỳnh Văn Bánh. All the sellers around here got their tickets from him."

Bảo thanked him. As he was leaving Hiển said, "What do you want her for?"

"My mother owed her money."

Hiển laughed, not unkindly. It was the first laugh Bảo had heard in maybe a week. "Money," Hiển said. "That's good. Go on, then. Pay your debts."

---

The agency on Huỳnh Văn Bánh had a sign above it, red and yellow, *ĐẠI LÝ VÉ SỐ QUÁCH HƯNG*, and the front had been smashed in. Not recently. Weeds were growing through the broken glass on the floor. Inside, tickets were everywhere, drifts of them, thousands and thousands, printed in pink and green and blue, stamped with the names of provinces, Tây Ninh, An Giang, Bình Thuận, Đồng Nai, each one for a draw that had never happened. Some of them had blown out into the street. Bảo had seen tickets like these all over the city, in gutters, stuck to walls, and never wondered where they came from.

"Looking for something?"

A woman stood in the doorway at the back of the shop. She was perhaps thirty, with her hair cut very short, the way many women had cut it during the fever because someone had said it helped with the sweats. She held a broom.

"I'm looking for Mr. Quách."

"My father," she said. "He's gone." She said it plainly. Everyone said it plainly now. "I'm Thúy."

He told her about Sáu. He told it badly, he thought, too many details about the pale green hat and the hip and not enough about why. When he finished, Thúy leaned the broom against the wall and studied him.

"You know you're the first person who's come here," she said, "looking for a lottery seller."

"Are other people coming here?"

"People come for the building. For the safe. There's nothing in the safe. They come for paper to burn. A man came last month looking for his wife, because she used to buy tickets here, as if we'd have her address." She shrugged. "Everyone's looking for someone. But nobody's looking for the sellers."

She took him into the back room. It was clean, much cleaner than the front; she had swept it, had made a bed in the corner on a pile of flattened cardboard. On a metal shelf were rows of ledgers bound in black. Her father had kept records of every seller who'd picked up tickets: how many, which provinces, how many returned unsold. Sellers paid for their tickets upfront and could return some of the unsold ones for a partial refund. It was all there.

"He wrote down names and addresses in case anyone ran off with stock," Thúy said. "Which some did." She pulled down a ledger from the year before the fever and opened it on the counter between them. "Sáu. Let's see."

There were thirty-one women listed as Sáu, or with Sáu in front of their names: Sáu Lùn, Sáu Chợ, Sáu Mập, Sáu Cà Mau. One was simply Bà Sáu, with a phone number that rang to nothing when Thúy tried it on her phone. One was a man.

"Bến Tre," Bảo said. "District 8. A bad hip."

Thúy ran her finger down the column. Her nail was cracked and dirty. "My father wouldn't have written down the hip," she said. "But here." She tapped a line. *Sáu (Bến Tre) — Trần Thị Lựu — Rạch Ông, Q.8 — Hẻm 14, cạnh cây gòn.* By the kapok tree. Then a phone number, crossed out, and another phone number, also crossed out.

"Trần Thị Lựu," Bảo said. He tried it out loud. It felt strange in his mouth, too formal, like reading out an exam roll.

Thúy copied it onto the back of an old ticket and handed it to him. Then she said, "My father liked her. She always paid on time. She returned the fewest tickets of anybody. He used to say she could sell a ticket to a dead man." She stopped, hearing what she had said. "Sorry."

"It's all right."

"Is it a lot of money?"

He thought about lying. "Four million six."

Thúy looked at the drifts of worthless paper on the floor of the shop, the pink and blue and green, and then back at him. She didn't laugh, the way Hiển had. She said, "The bridges are bad. Chánh Hưng especially. There's a group on it. Take the Nhị Thiên Đường bridge if you can, and have something to give them."

---

He went the next morning on his bicycle, because the motorbike had no petrol and there was no petrol to give it. He packed water, a cold rice ball wrapped in banana leaf, the canvas bag with the envelope, and, after some hesitation, his father's old watch, a heavy Seiko that hadn't run in a decade but looked as though it might.

He hadn't gone farther than Phú Nhuận market since the fever. The city south of it was a place he recognized only by the shapes of buildings. Nguyễn Văn Trỗi Street, which used to be a solid river of motorbikes at this hour, was empty from one end to the other, so empty that he rode down the very middle of it, over the faded white lines, and heard the tick of his own chain. The trees along the street had grown wild in six months; branches hung low enough to brush his head. Shop signs had faded. A café he'd been to with colleagues every Friday for two years had a tarp over its doorway and a family living inside; a child watched him go by and didn't wave.

In District 3 he passed a wall that someone had covered in names. Hundreds of them, in marker and paint and chalk, some with dates, some with a single word beside them: *mẹ*, *con*, *anh*. Mother, child, brother. He slowed, almost stopped. He had written his mother's name on a wall like that near the pagoda. He didn't know what it was for. Nobody did. People did it anyway.

At the canal he turned west. The water was low and green and smelled like it always had, which was strangely comforting. On the far bank, District 8 was a tangle of low houses and iron roofs, some burned, most not. He found the Nhị Thiên Đường bridge and rode up the slope of it, and at the top three men were sitting in plastic chairs under a beach umbrella.

They were young, younger than his students had been. One had a pistol stuck in the waistband of his shorts in a way that suggested he'd seen it done in films. The other two had nothing, only the confidence of being three and not one.

"Where are you going, teacher?" the one with the pistol said. It was a good guess; Bảo had the look, he supposed, the glasses and the collared shirt and the canvas bag.

"Rạch Ông. To find someone."

"Who?"

"A woman who sold lottery tickets."

They thought this was very funny. One of them laughed so hard he had to lean on the bridge rail.

"What's in the bag?"

He thought of the envelope. He thought how easily they'd take it if he told them it was money, and then how quickly they'd throw it into the canal when they understood what kind of money it was. He wasn't sure which would be worse. He reached into his pocket instead and brought out his father's watch.

The one with the pistol turned it over in his hands, held it to his ear, frowned. "It's not running."

"It needs a battery," Bảo said, which was a lie. It was a mechanical watch. It needed cleaning, a jeweler, a world with jewelers in it.

The young man studied him. Then he shrugged and put the watch on his wrist, where it slid down loose over his knuckles, and waved Bảo across.

---

Rạch Ông was a narrow canal off the bigger one, lined on both sides by houses built out over the water on stilts, wood and corrugated iron and sheets of blue tarp. Many of them were empty; you could see straight through some of them to the water underneath. Others had laundry hanging and smoke coming from cooking fires. The kapok tree was easy to find. It was the tallest thing for blocks, a great pale trunk with branches like arms held out, and the seed pods had burst open and covered the alley beneath it in soft white fluff, so that it looked as though it had snowed.

Hẻm 14 was hardly wide enough for the bicycle. He walked it in. At the end of the alley, where the boards went out over the water, an old man sat fishing with a line tied to a stick.

"I'm looking for Trần Thị Lựu," Bảo said. "Sáu. Who sold lottery tickets."

The old man did not look up from the water. "Sáu's at home."

Bảo's heart did something complicated. "She's alive?"

"Alive. Doesn't go out anymore." The old man pointed with his chin. "Since the boy."

The house was the last one on the left, built half over the canal, with a green door. Someone had planted chili peppers in old paint tins along the front step, and they were thriving, bright red against the dark wood. Bảo stood at the door for a while before he knocked. He had been focused on finding her and hadn't thought about what came next, and now he found that he didn't know what to say, and that his hands were shaking.

He knocked. After a long time, the door opened.

She was smaller than he remembered, or he hadn't remembered her at all, only her voice in the alley and the pale green hat. Her hair was white and pulled back tight. One shoulder sat lower than the other. She looked at him without recognition.

"Bà Sáu," he said. "I'm Bảo. Kim's son. From the alley on Phan Đình Phùng. Number 17."

Her face changed. She put one hand on the doorframe.

"Kim," she said. Then, "Kim's gone?"

"In the third month."

She nodded slowly, several times, as though agreeing with something someone had said a long time ago. "I thought so," she said. "When I stopped going. I thought, Kim's probably gone, and I'll never know." She stood aside. "Come in. Come in, sit down."

---

The house was one room. There was a sleeping platform, a small altar, a gas ring with no gas, a clay stove instead. Through the gaps in the floorboards Bảo could see the green water moving. On the wall above the altar hung a photo of a boy of about fourteen in a white school shirt and red scarf, grinning with too many teeth. Incense had burned down to stubs in front of it. Beside the altar, leaning against the wall, was a wooden board with a strap, the kind sellers hung around their necks, and still clipped to it were rows of lottery tickets, faded nearly white by the sun.

She made him tea from leaves that had been used before. He drank it. They sat on the floor because there was nowhere else to sit.

"My grandson," she said, when she saw him looking at the photograph. "Tí. His parents went to work in Taiwan when he was two. I raised him." She didn't say anything else about it.

Bảo took the envelope out of the canvas bag and held it out to her.

"My mother wanted you to have this. It was the last thing she said. *Còn tiền chị Sáu.* I don't know what she owed you. But it's yours."

Sáu didn't take it. She looked at the envelope, at her own name, the name that wasn't a name, in Kim's handwriting, and her mouth pressed tight.

"She didn't owe me anything," she said.

"Then what is it?"

"It's mine." She reached out and touched the envelope with two fingers, not taking it, only touching it. "I gave it to her. A little at a time. Nine years." She saw his face and almost smiled. "Where would I keep money, here? Look at this place. Someone breaks in every year. The canal floods. The rats eat paper. And I didn't want a bank. Banks want you to sign things." She made a small motion with her hand, as if writing in the air, then let the hand fall. "I can't write. Can't read, either. Numbers, yes. Lottery numbers I can read. Words, no."

"So my mother kept it for you."

"Your mother kept it for me." She did take the envelope now, and opened it, and took out the blue notebook with the cartoon cat on it, and set the money aside on the floor without looking at it. "And she kept this. So I'd know it was all there. She'd read it to me sometimes, when I asked."

She held the notebook out to him.

"Read it," she said. "Please. I haven't heard it in a long time."

---

He opened it. The first page said, in his mother's hand, *Sổ tiết kiệm của chị Sáu*, Sister Sáu's savings book, and below it a date nine years ago. Then lines, entry after entry, each one a date and an amount, the amounts small: twenty thousand, fifty thousand, thirty thousand. And beside many of them, in smaller writing, a line or two more.

He began to read.

"Fourteenth of March. Twenty thousand." He looked at the smaller writing. "*Rain all afternoon. Sáu sat under our awning for an hour. Told me about the coconut trees on her father's land. Said they were so tall the boys used to climb them and sing at the top so their mothers would know where they were.*"

He glanced up. Sáu had closed her eyes.

"Go on."

"Twenty-second of March. Fifty thousand. *Sold out early. Good day.* Second of April. Ten thousand. *Hip bad. She would not take paracetamol from me, said Hiển at the pharmacy already gave her some. Too proud. I put an extra ten in and told her she miscounted.*"

Sáu opened her eyes. "She did that?"

"It says so."

"That cheat," Sáu said, and her voice cracked down the middle.

He read on. The pages were full of small things: a fight with the agent over returned tickets, a Tết bonus, a day Sáu found a five-hundred-thousand note in the gutter and kept it, *and I said the gods sent it, and she said the gods don't walk on Phan Đình Phùng, and we laughed so hard Mr. Toàn came out to see what was wrong.* Tí passing his entrance exam to the good secondary school. Tí needing glasses. The money for the glasses being taken out, recorded carefully, *Minus 400,000, glasses for Tí, Sáu chose the blue frames.* And then going back up again, slowly, ten thousand at a time.

And in among them, unexpectedly, there was Bảo.

"Ninth of September," he read, and stopped. Then went on. "Thirty thousand. *Bảo came home after ten again. Didn't eat. I left rice on the table and he didn't touch it. Told Sáu about it and she said, children are like the canal, Kim, they go out with the tide and come back with the tide, you can't hurry the water.*"

He couldn't continue for a moment. Sáu waited. Through the floorboards the canal moved.

"I don't remember saying that," Sáu said finally. "Sounds like something I'd say."

He read the next page, and the next. He was in there four or five more times. *Bảo got his teaching award. He didn't tell me. I found the certificate in his bag.* And: *Bảo said he might apply for the job in Đà Nẵng. Sáu says let him go. I said I know. I said I know.* He had never applied. He had forgotten that he had ever thought about it. She had not.

The last pages were from the fever. The handwriting grew less careful.

"*Second month. No draws now. Sáu still comes. I told her go home, it isn't safe, nobody is buying. She says the tickets don't sell themselves. I bought ten. She said what for, there's no draw. I said there will be. She cried a little. I gave her rice.*"

"*Third month. Ten thousand. Sáu says Tí is sick. She will not come for a while. I told her I will keep everything safe. I will keep everything safe.*"

That was the last entry. Below it was the total, written and rewritten, the final figure underlined twice: four million six hundred thousand.

Bảo closed the notebook.

For a long time neither of them said anything. Somewhere down the canal a man was calling to someone, a single name, again and again, without answer, and then he stopped.

"What was it for?" Bảo said. "The money. What were you saving for?"

Sáu looked at the photograph on the wall. "For his university. He wanted to be an engineer. Bridges." She laughed, softly. "Can you imagine. A boy from Rạch Ông, building bridges."

The money lay on the floor between them. Twenty-three bills, nine years of afternoons in the heat. She picked them up, one by one, squared them, and put them back in the envelope, and then she put the envelope on the altar in front of the photograph of her grandson, and lit the last stub of incense she had, and pressed her palms together, and bowed three times.

When she sat back down she held out her hand for the notebook. He gave it to her. She held it against her chest.

"Did she keep them?" Sáu asked. "The ten tickets."

He had them. He had found them in his mother's purse after the cremation, ten tickets for a Tây Ninh draw on a date that had come and gone without anyone turning the drum. He'd almost thrown them away. He took them out of the canvas bag now, where he'd put them without quite knowing why, and laid them on the floor in a row.

Sáu leaned over them, reading the numbers, her lips moving. She touched one, then another.

"She always took the last ones," Sáu said. "Whatever I couldn't sell. So I could go home early." She looked up at him. "There was never any draw for these."

"No."

"So they haven't lost," she said.

---

He asked her to come back with him. He said it awkwardly, the way he said everything important, as though it had occurred to him only that moment, when in fact he'd been thinking it since the third or fourth page of the notebook. There were empty houses all along the alley. The Hứa family had vegetables on the roof. Hiển had his shutter and his machete. Here, by the canal, she was alone, and the floor had gaps, and someone broke in every year.

"My hip," she said. "I can't walk that far anymore."

"I have a bicycle."

She looked at him as if he were simple. "You want me on the back of your bicycle. Like a schoolgirl."

"Yes."

She didn't answer. She turned the notebook over in her hands. Then she got up, slowly, one hand on the altar, and went to the corner where the old ticket board leaned against the wall, and lifted it by its strap, and blew the dust off it.

---

They crossed back over the Nhị Thiên Đường bridge in the late afternoon. She sat sideways on the rack over the rear wheel, her pale green hat tied under her chin, the board across her knees, one hand gripping the back of his shirt. She weighed almost nothing. On the upslope he had to stand on the pedals, and she said, "Slowly, slowly, teacher, you'll kill us both," and he laughed, and was surprised to hear it.

The three young men were still under their beach umbrella. The one with the pistol stood up when he saw them, and then saw who was on the back, and stopped.

"What's this?" he said. "You found her?"

"I found her."

"What's she got?"

Sáu answered before Bảo could. "Lottery tickets." She held up the board. "Kiến Thiết, Tây Ninh, Đồng Nai. Lucky numbers. You want one?"

The young man stared at her. His friends began to laugh again, but he didn't, quite. He was looking at the board, at the rows of faded paper.

"There's no draw," he said.

"Not yet," said Sáu.

He looked at the watch on his wrist, his father's watch, Bảo's father's, which had not run in ten years and would not run again. Then he reached out and took a ticket off the board, a pink one, and studied it, and folded it once, carefully, and put it in his shirt pocket.

"How much?" he said.

"For you," Sáu said, "the price is: let us go."

He let them go.

---

It was dusk when they came down Phan Đình Phùng. The electricity hadn't come on yet. Hiển's shutter was halfway up and he was sitting behind it, and when he saw them go by he stood, and took off his glasses, and put them on again. Bảo didn't stop. He turned into the alley and rode slowly past the empty houses, number 3, number 5, number 9 where Mr. Toàn's curtain twitched, past the Hứa house with its rooftop of green, and stopped at number 17, under the awning where it had rained one afternoon nine years ago, and where two women had sat for an hour talking about coconut trees.

Sáu got down, stiffly. She stood in the alley with the board hanging from its strap around her neck and looked up and down it, at all the closed gates.

Then she lifted her chin and called out, in a thin, high voice that Bảo had heard every afternoon of his life and had never once listened to, and that went down the whole length of the alley and came back off the walls:

"*Vé số đây. Vé số Kiến Thiết đây.*"

At number 22, a gate opened.
