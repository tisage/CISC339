The bus from Caruaru let Gilvan off at the edge of Santa Luzia do Brejo a little after two in the afternoon. No one was in the square except a dog asleep in the shade of the bandstand and a boy selling roasted peanuts in paper cones he had rolled from pages of a school notebook. Gilvan bought a cone, read the multiplication tables on the inside of the paper while he ate, and decided the town would do.

He was wearing the linen jacket, which in that heat was a kind of argument. Nobody in the Agreste wore a jacket in November unless he had come from somewhere that required one. He had a leather portfolio under his arm with the brass clasp polished, and a card case in his breast pocket. The cards said *Dr. Augusto Brandão Teles, Genealogista. Instituto Luso-Brasileiro de Pesquisa Heráldica. Lisboa, Recife.* There was a phone number for the Recife office. If anyone called it, Nair would answer in the clipped, faintly Portuguese voice she had perfected over thirty years. She called the voice *Dra. Fátima*, and she had once kept a judge on the line for forty minutes discussing the inheritance law of 1858.

The method was old, older than Gilvan. Nair had learned it from a man who had worked it in the interior of Paraíba in the seventies, and he had learned it from someone else. You found a town where one surname ran thick through the population, the way a single vine will take over a fence. You arrived with a story about a nineteenth-century ancestor. He was a Portuguese merchant, a *comendador*, who had made a fortune in sugar or cotton or slaves, had died without a clear will, and had left an estate that the Portuguese courts had kept frozen for a hundred and forty years. The estate had finally been released to the legitimate descendants. The descendants had only to prove their line and contribute to the costs of the proceedings: notarization, translation, certified copies, the lawyers in Lisbon. The figures you mentioned for the estate itself were large enough to stun and vague enough to never be checked. The figures you asked for were small, and then a little larger, and then there was a final payment that everyone had to make together, because the court required a single unified petition.

In Santa Luzia do Brejo the surname was Brandão. Gilvan had found it in the phone listings and in the death notices of the regional paper. Of the four thousand people in the municipality, Nair estimated that perhaps six or seven hundred carried it, and most of the rest had married into it.

He walked to the church first, because you always walked to the church first.

---

The priest was a young man from Garanhuns with a sunburned neck and the anxious look of someone who had been given a parish too early. He was delighted by the genealogist. He brought out the baptismal registers from the back room, cloth-bound books the color of dried tobacco, and let Gilvan turn the pages with his own hands while the fan in the corner pushed the hot air from one wall to the other.

"The Comendador Brandão," Gilvan said, tapping the portfolio. "Bento Brandão de Almeida e Sousa. Came from Viana do Castelo in 1841. We know he had at least three sons in this region. We believe one of them settled here."

He did not know any of this. Nair had invented Bento Brandão de Almeida e Sousa on a napkin in a bakery in Boa Viagem, the way she had invented a dozen others. But the beauty of the thing was that the books were full of Brandões, and one of them, sooner or later, would be named Bento, or would have a father named Bento, or a godfather. And once the town found him, the town would never let him go.

"If you want to know who's who," the priest said, "you should talk to Iracema."

"Iracema?"

"Iracema Lins. Foto Lins, on the Rua do Rosário. Her father photographed everyone in this town for fifty years. Baptisms, weddings, first communions, funerals. If there's a Brandão who ever stood still, Seu Ademar took his picture." He paused. "She's a Brandão on her mother's side. And people listen to her."

Gilvan wrote the name in his notebook, and underlined it twice, which was how he marked the people who mattered.

---

Foto Lins had a glass front, and in the window was a display of portraits faded to the colors of weak tea: a bride under a veil like a mosquito net, a soldier in a beret, a baby propped on a velvet cushion with its eyes closed so that Gilvan could not tell if it was sleeping or dead. Inside it was cool and dim and smelled of something sharp and chemical that he would later learn was fixer. There was a counter, a painted backdrop of a garden with a balustrade, and a wooden studio camera on a tripod, enormous and antique, covered with a black cloth, which he assumed was decorative until he saw the fresh fingerprints on its brass.

The woman who came from the back room was drying her hands on a rag. She was perhaps forty, with gray already coming in at the temples and left alone there, and she wore reading glasses pushed up into her hair. She looked at the jacket first and then at him.

"If you want a 3x4, the machine at the pharmacy is cheaper," she said. "If you want a good 3x4, it's eight reais and you'll have to wait until tomorrow."

He gave her the card. She read it and turned it over and read the blank back too, which few people did.

"Padre Jonas told me about you," she said. "He called an hour ago. He was very excited."

"He's a kind man."

"He's a young man." She set the card on the counter, squared to its edge. "What do you want from me, Dr. Teles?"

He gave her the speech, the gentle version, the one for intelligent people. He was not, he told her, asking anyone for anything yet. He was in the earliest stages of research. He needed to establish the lines of descent before any of it could go forward. He understood that her father's archive was extraordinary. He would be very grateful for her help, and of course he would pay for her time.

She listened with her arms folded. When he finished, she said, "Can I see the documents?"

This was the moment, always. Nair's rule was that you never refused. Refusal was the only thing that made people suspicious; openness made them lazy. He opened the portfolio on the counter and spread it out: the letter from the Lisbon court on heavy cream paper, the certified extract of the will with its ribbon and red wax seal, the family tree drawn in brown ink in an elegant nineteenth-century hand. Nair had a man in Olinda who did the calligraphy. The seal she had made herself.

Iracema pulled her glasses down onto her nose. She looked for a long time, and touched nothing.

"Do you mind," she said, "if I photograph them? For my records. My father kept records of everything."

"Please," he said.

She went into the back and came out with a camera much smaller than the one on the tripod, a heavy black thing with a lens like a drinking glass, and photographed each page laid flat on the counter under a lamp she angled with great care. She worked quickly and without speaking. When she was done she thanked him and told him to come back Thursday, after lunch, when the studio was closed, and she would show him the negatives.

He left feeling the small cold pleasure of a lock clicking open.

---

He rented a room above the bakery from a widow named Dona Corrinha Brandão, who told him in the first ten minutes that her grandfather had been named Bento, and that she had always known, *always*, that there was something about her family. That night he called Nair from the bench in the square.

"The photographer," Nair said. "That's good. That's very good. Get the photographer and you get the town."

"She photographed the documents."

A pause on the line, the sound of Nair lighting a cigarette in her apartment in Recife, eight floors above the Avenida Conde da Boa Vista. "So what? Let her photograph them. A photograph of a forgery is a photograph."

"I know."

"So why are you telling me?"

He didn't know, so he said goodnight.

---

On Thursday the studio was locked, and he knocked, and she let him in and locked the door behind him and led him through the curtain into the back.

He had expected a darkroom. There was a darkroom, a narrow closet with a red bulb and trays and a line strung with clothespins. But most of the back of the building was given over to shelves, floor to ceiling, of narrow wooden boxes, hundreds of them, each labeled on the end in a small square hand. *1961 — Casamentos. 1962 — 1ª Comunhão. 1974 — Enterro, Sr. Abílio Brandão Filho.*

"Glass plates up to about 1965," Iracema said. "Then film. My father didn't believe in throwing anything away. When he got sick he made me promise not to sell any of it. A museum in Recife offered once." She shrugged. "He said they'd put it in a basement and nobody would look at it. Here, at least, I look at it."

She pulled down a box and opened it. The glass plates stood in grooves like the pages of a book. She lifted one by its edges and held it up to the window, and he saw a family standing in front of a mud-brick house, a man and a woman and seven children, all of them with their white eyes and black teeth, the way negatives turn people into negatives of themselves.

"Antônio Brandão Neto," she said. "And Maria das Dores. 1958. The fourth boy is Corrinha's father."

"You know them all?"

"My father wrote it all down." She tapped a ledger on the shelf. "And I grew up here. I know their noses."

They worked through the afternoon. She found the plates; he wrote the names in his notebook and drew lines between them. It was supposed to be theater. He was supposed to nod and make notes and look for a Bento. But the plates kept coming, the faces kept appearing in the window light, upside down in their tones, and he found himself asking questions that had nothing to do with the con. Who was the woman in the man's hat? Why were these brothers standing so far apart? What happened to the baby on the cushion?

"Died that winter," she said. "People photographed babies when they were sick. It was sometimes the only picture there would be."

At six the light went orange and then gray. She closed the boxes and made coffee on a hot plate in the corner, very sweet and very strong, in two glasses that did not match.

"You're good at this," she said.

"I've done it a long time."

"I mean you're good at looking." She blew on her coffee. "Most people look at a photograph and see if they're in it. Then they stop."

---

The town began to come to him. It happened faster than it usually did, and he knew it was because of her, because word had gone around that Iracema was working with the genealogist, and Iracema did not waste her time. They came to the bakery in the mornings and waited for him with their documents in plastic bags: birth certificates, marriage certificates, land deeds from the twenties typed on onionskin, letters from relatives in São Paulo. They had found a Bento. In fact they had found four, which was good, because it meant four branches would compete to prove themselves closest to the trunk.
