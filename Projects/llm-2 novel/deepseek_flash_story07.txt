================================================================================

The last ferry to Donggang pulled out at 4:40 with a long, unapologetic blast of its horn, and Lin Si-yu stood at the end of the pier with one hand still raised, as though the boat might reconsider.

Behind her, the ticket window had already been shuttered. A sign taped to the glass said, in Chinese and in English, that all sailings were suspended until further notice, and someone had written the typhoon number at the bottom in black marker — No. 9, Meihua, upgraded to a severe typhoon at 1500, expected to make landfall on the southeastern coast after midnight.

She had missed it by eleven minutes. She had been standing in the shallows off Zhong'ao with a snorkel mask, watching a green sea turtle graze on the seagrass. It had moved so slowly, so unbothered, that she had forgotten to be a person with a schedule. When she finally stood up and looked at the sky, the light had gone the color of old brass, and the entire island had gone quiet in that particular way, and she had understood, with a small clean shock, that she had done it on purpose.

That was the part she did not want to look at yet, so she looked instead at the man sitting on the concrete barrier at the edge of the harbor.

He had been there when she arrived. A duffel bag sat between his feet, and he was eating from a paper bag of mahuajuan, the fried twisted dough the whole island sold, tearing off pieces and putting them in his mouth without much interest. He was in his fifties, greying at the temples, in a windbreaker that had been good ten years ago. He had the look of a man who had been waiting a long time and had made peace with it.

"It's not coming back," he said, without looking at her.

"I know."

"The nine-forty tomorrow, maybe. If the port's clear." He brushed crumbs off his knee. "The girl at the ticket window said ten. But she also said she didn't know."

The wind came around the breakwater and lifted the hem of her skirt. Out past the harbor mouth, the sea had started to lose its shape, the horizon going soft and bruised. The fishing boats were all in, doubled up on their moorings, and the whole fleet leaned together and creaked like a herd in bad weather.

"You're staying?" she asked.

"I'm staying."

"Where?"

He chewed. "Haven't got that far."

She had a room booked on the north end of the island for two nights, a place with a view of the reef, and she had booked it alone, and she had told her husband it was a conference in Hualien. She had thought about that lie for three weeks before she said it, the way you check a rope before you put weight on it. So when she heard herself say, "There's a guesthouse," she felt the rope slip a little in her hands, and she did not correct it.

The man looked at her for the first time. His face was broad and tired and not unkind. He had a way of holding his mouth that suggested he had spent a lot of his life listening to other people talk.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know," she said. "It's going to rain."

---

They walked up through the village together, past the shuttered shops, past the racks of electric scooters all plugged in and waiting for tourists who had left on the two o'clock boat. The mahuajuan shop had already boarded its front, but somebody's radio was playing behind the panel, a Taiwanese opera, the wailing turned low. A skinny yellow dog watched them from under a parked truck and did not bother to growl.

At the 7-Eleven near the fishing harbor, the shelves looked like the end of a siege. The clerk was up a stepladder, taping an X of masking tape across the glass, and she did not stop to look at them.

"Everything's closed," she said to the window. "If you need a place, A-ju at the end of the lane takes people. She's old. She won't turn you out. Take water."

They took water. They took instant noodles, six packs, two of the chicken and one of the beef and one that was just called Hot Pot Flavor. He paid for his own and she paid for hers and they did not discuss it.

The guesthouse was a two-story concrete box at the end of a lane, painted blue once, with a tin roof and a courtyard of cracked tiles where four big blue plastic barrels stood full of rain. The woman who answered the door was tiny and bent and wore a plastic raincoat still dripping, as though she had been standing in the yard waiting for something to happen.

A-ju took the money without looking at it, and showed them a room each, and stood in the corridor with her hands on her hips and delivered a set of instructions in a mix of Mandarin and Taiwanese that Si-yu understood about sixty percent of. The gist was: the house was old, the roof made noise but held, the power would likely go, the water in the barrels was for flushing, and she was going to her son's place on the other side of the island because she was not going to sit under a tin roof at her age and listen to that. She would be back when it was over.

"Do not open the shutter in the front room," she said, pointing a finger at each of them in turn. "If the window breaks, come to my son's. Three lanes over. Ask anybody."

And then she was gone up the lane under a yellow umbrella, small and quick, and they were alone in a house that was not theirs.

---

They ate at the small table in the front room, the two of them, with the shutters closed and the radio on for company. The storm had not arrived yet. It announced itself the way a crowd does, with a growing pressure of sound from far away, so that the quiet inside the house felt artificial, like a held breath.

His name was Chen Jia-hao. He was from Tainan. He had, he said, a small plumbing and electrical shop, the kind of place that fixed your water heater and also sold you a lightbulb, and it had been in the same spot on the same street for thirty-one years. He had a wife. He had a daughter, twenty-five, in Taichung, doing something with marketing. He had a son, twenty-two, in Hsinchu, at a semiconductor company. He told her all of this the way a man recites a driving license.

She told him her name, and that she was a pharmacist at a hospital in Taipei. She told him she had a husband named Wei-ting who designed websites, and a daughter named Yun, three years old, who was going through a phase of refusing to wear socks.

She did not know why she had told him her daughter's name. She had not said the name out loud in three days.

"Three years old," Jia-hao said. "Good age. Bad age. My daughter at three, she climbed the refrigerator."

"They don't fall, though. That's the thing. They never fall."

"Not the first time," he said.

The radio said the outer bands were coming ashore at Fangliao. The radio said people in low-lying areas should prepare. The radio said Meihua was moving northwest at eighteen kilometers per hour.

The first real gust hit the house at a quarter past nine, and the tin roof let out a long groan, a sound like a ship adjusting its bones, and Si-yu found that she had put both hands flat on the table.

"It's fine," Jia-hao said. "Tin talks. Concrete listens."

"That's not a real saying."

"No," he said. "But it's true."

---

She woke at two in the morning because the world had ended.

That was what it felt like, anyway. The wind was not a sound anymore; it had become a pressure inside the room, pushing at the walls, and under it was a lower noise, a kind of grinding, as though something enormous were being dragged along the road outside. The rain on the tin roof was so loud it had stopped being rain. And then the power went, with a click that was almost apologetic, and the dark came in so completely that she could not tell whether her eyes were open.

She lay there for a while with her heart going, and then she got up and felt her way into the corridor, one hand on the wall, and found that Jia-hao was already there, standing at the top of the stairs with a flashlight pointed at the ceiling.

"Shutter," he said.

"What?"

"Front room shutter. I heard it go."

They went down together. The front room was full of a sound she could not identify at first, and then she understood it was the wind getting through, and when Jia-hao put the flashlight on the window she saw that the wooden shutter had been torn half off its hinge and was slamming against the frame, and with every slam a sheet of water came into the room and spread across the tile.

He waded in and got his shoulder against it. She found the bolt on the floor, both halves, and got the bottom one through the bracket while he leaned his whole weight on the panel, and then she had to climb on a plastic stool, which skidded once, and get the top one, and her hands were shaking so badly that she dropped the pin twice before it went in, and when it finally did go in they both stood there in the dark, breathing, with the water around their ankles.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. That's the easy part."

"What's the hard part?"

"Finding out if anything else wants to come in."

They went through the house together with the flashlight. It took an hour. They found the kitchen window leaking and stuffed it with a towel; they found nothing else. At some point he filled the kettle and made tea on the gas burner, because the gas still worked even if the power didn't, and they sat on the floor of the corridor, the safest place in the house, with their backs against opposite walls and the tea going cold in their hands, and listened to the typhoon try to take the roof off.

"It's not going to," he said, when she looked up.

"How do you know?"

"Because I did the roof of a house like this once." He drank his tea. "Concrete, tin, no insulation. If A-ju's roof has lasted through forty typhoons, it'll last through this one. It's her window I'd worry about, and we already did the window."

She looked at him. "You fix roofs."

"I fix everything. That's what a water-and-electric is. You fix what the house needs." He shrugged. "I have a shop, but the shop is mostly me."

"How long?"

"Thirty-one years."

"Do you like it?"

It was, she realized afterwards, an unfair question to ask a stranger at three in the morning in the middle of a typhoon, and she would not have asked it of anyone else. But he took it seriously. He turned his cup around in his hands.

"I like the part where I go to a house and something is broken and when I leave it is not broken," he said. "That's the whole thing. That's the whole job. You spend a day in a flooded bathroom with your arm up a pipe and then the tap runs and the woman brings you a cup of tea and it is not broken anymore." He looked up. "Why, do you not like yours?"

"I like it," she said. "I hand people their medicine. They say thank you. Some of them say it for years."

"So?"

"So nothing." She pulled her knees up. "It's fine. It's a good job."

"Mm," he said, in a way that made her want to hit him slightly.

---

The eye came over them at eleven the next morning.

She knew what it was because she had read about it, but knowing about a thing is not the same as standing in it. One moment the wind was at the walls and then the sound simply stopped, like a radio being switched off, and the silence that followed was so complete that she could hear a fly somewhere in the room.

They went outside.

It was the strangest thing she had ever seen. The sky was a soft high grey-blue, absolutely still. Water stood in the lane in flat mirrors. A hundred meters away the trees had been stripped bare and the world had the shorn look of a landscape after an argument. And then she looked up, and saw that the sky directly overhead was clear, and there were stars faintly in it, in the middle of the morning, and around the edge of the clear circle there were two great walls of cloud rotating away from each other in opposite directions, so that she was standing at the bottom of a well.

"Fifteen minutes," Jia-hao said. "Maybe twenty. We should go."

"Go where?"

He looked at her. "You want to see the reef before it comes back."

They walked down the lane and out past the harbor, where the fishing boats were jumbled together and one had snapped its lines and lain over on its side like a dead animal, and along the shore road to the little beach at Zhong'ao. The water was glass. Absolutely glass. The reef showed through it, all the pale green and brown shapes of it, and out past the coral the sand dropped away into blue, and there, in the shallows where she had been standing only yesterday, a green sea turtle was swimming, big and unhurried, tilting up with its head above the surface to breathe.

She sat down on the sand in her wet shoes and watched it, and she began to cry.

She did not cry the way she had imagined she would, the time she had rehearsed it in the car outside the hospital, with her hands on the wheel. She cried the way a person cries when they are tired and nobody is watching, without any drama, without any sound. The turtle did not care. It paddled a few strokes and put its head down and went on eating.

Jia-hao sat a respectful distance away and looked at the sea, and let her finish.

"I have a daughter," she said, when she could talk. "Yun. Three years old."

"I know."

"I told my husband I was going to a conference in Hualien. There's no conference. I took three days off and I came here alone and I didn't tell anybody, and I've been sitting on a beach with a mask on my face for two days, and the reason is—" She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "The reason is I wanted to find out whether I would miss her."

The sea did the flat, tender thing the sea does in the eye of a storm. Behind them the wind was already beginning to come back; you could feel it in the sand, a thin, cold finger dragged over the back of the neck.

"When she was six months old," Si-yu said, "I stood over her crib one night. She was asleep and it was three in the morning and I had not slept more than ninety minutes at a time in four months, and I looked down at her, and a thought went through my head. A clear thought. It was like a sentence in a book. It was not a feeling. I did not want to do it. But it was there, in the room, with us." She stopped. "And I took a step back from the crib. And another one. And I went and sat in the bathroom on the floor for two hours with the light off."

The turtle surfaced and blew, a small wet sound.

"I went to a doctor," she said. "Later. Months later. And the doctor was very kind and very matter-of-fact, and he said this is common, this happens, this is a symptom, it is not who you are. And he gave me something and I took it and I am fine now. I am fine. I play with her, I read to her, I buy her the wrong socks on purpose because she likes to argue about it. I love her. I love her more than—" She shook her head. "That's not the part I can't say. The part I can't say is that for about five months, I did not feel it. I did not feel anything when I looked at her. And one afternoon I was walking her in the stroller by the river and I thought, I could just keep walking. Nobody would know for an hour. And I did not feel afraid when I thought it. I felt relieved." She looked at him. "I have never told anyone that. Not the doctor. Not my mother. Not Wei-ting. Nobody. You are the first person I have ever said it to, and I don't know your last name until yesterday and I will never see you again."

Jia-hao was quiet for a while. The clouds on the western rim had begun to boil and move, and the light was changing, the grey going yellow at the edges.

"Okay," he said.

"That's it? Okay?"

"What do you want me to say?" He looked at her, and there was nothing in his face but attention, and something else, something she could not name then. "You were sick. You got help. You got better. You didn't do anything."

"I thought about it."

"Everybody thinks about things. I have thought about driving my car off the bridge at Guiren maybe two hundred times in my life. Not because I want to die." He picked up a shell and put it down. "Because the thinking is free. The thinking is where you put the things you don't have anywhere else to put."

"That's not the same."

"No," he agreed. "It's not." He was quiet for a moment. "But I wasn't going to hand you my worst one just to make you feel better about yours. You'll get it. It's coming."

The light changed again, all at once, and the sea went from glass to steel in the space of a breath. Somewhere out past the reef, a wall of whiteness began to move toward them across the water, and the trees on the headland bent like grass, and the sound came back, that enormous sound, not approaching but arriving, the way it had left.

They ran all the way back.

---

The second half was worse than the first.

The back wall of a typhoon is where the damage usually is, and this one was no different. They spent the afternoon in the corridor with their backs to the concrete, listening to things come apart. Something big went off a roof three houses down and landed in the lane with a crash that shook the floor; the tin over the stairwell lifted and slapped down again four times in a row, and each time Si-yu felt her stomach lift with it. Water came in under the front door and they rolled towels against it and it came anyway. They bailed the front room twice with a plastic bucket A-ju used for mopping and poured it out the kitchen window into the rain, which was absurd and they both knew it was absurd, and they did it anyway, because the alternative was sitting still.

At six the power came back on for eleven minutes and then went off again for good.

By dark the wind had begun to slacken, in the way that a person who has been shouting for hours begins to lose their voice. It was still terrible, but it was a tired terrible, and they both knew what that meant. They boiled water on the gas and made the Hot Pot Flavor noodles and ate them sitting on mattresses in the upstairs corridor, which was the driest place in the house, with four candles from A-ju's kitchen altar lined up along the wall.

They had been drinking. There was a bottle of Kinmen kaoliang in the kitchen cupboard, half full, that had probably been there since A-ju's husband was alive, and they had taken turns with it out of the only clean glass in the house.

"So," she said. "You said it was coming."

"I said that?"

"You said, you'll get it, it's coming."

He leaned his head back against the wall and closed his eyes.

"I have a son," he said. "Chen Bo-yan. Twenty-two. He works at a company in Hsinchu, he makes the machines that make the chips, or something, I don't understand it, I ask and he explains and I understand maybe a third. He's a good boy. Better than me." He opened his eyes. "When he was two years old he had a fever, a bad one, he had a seizure in the night and we took him to the hospital, and they did tests. And one of the tests was blood type."

The candle on the far left guttered and steadied.

"I am O," he said. "My wife is O. Two O's make an O. That's not a thing you can argue with. That's just how it is." He looked at the ceiling. "My son is AB."

The rain had settled into a steady drum, and somewhere below them water was dripping into a bucket with a small, patient sound.

"The doctor brought me the paper himself," Jia-hao said. "He was a young man. He was very nervous. He handed it to me and he said, Mr. Chen, there is something here you should see. And I looked at it and I understood what he was telling me, and I understood that he thought he was telling me something I did not know." He smiled, a small flat smile. "I told him, thank you. And I folded the paper and put it in my pocket and I went back into the room and my wife was holding the boy and she was asleep in the chair, and the boy was on her chest, and I stood there in the doorway for about ten minutes. And then I sat down and I never said anything."

"You never said anything."

"Not that night. Not the next year. Not for twenty years."

"You never asked her."

"What would I ask her? Where would I begin? We were twenty-eight years old." He turned the glass in his hands. "People think the hard part is finding out. The hard part is the twenty years."

The candle guttered again. He waited for it to come back.

"I love that boy," he said. "You understand what I'm saying. I am telling you the true thing, which is that I love him, and he is my son, and I would put my hand in a fire for him, and there is not one second of my life where I have wanted him gone. And at the same time there is a door in me that I have not opened in twenty years, and behind it is a question, and I have not opened it because if I do, everything on the other side is true, and everything on this side is true, and I don't know how to hold both at once, so I have just stood in front of the door and dusted it and gone on with the day."

"Does he look like you?"

"He looks like my wife," he said. "Everybody says so. For twenty years people have said, oh, he's his mother's boy, and I have said yes, yes he is, and I have meant it." He drank. "Three years ago I found out my wife's first boyfriend — the one before me — is a man named A-Kai who now runs a breakfast shop in Yongkang. I looked him up. On my phone. At two in the morning. I did not go there. I want you to understand that. But I looked him up. And I looked at the photographs on his page. And that is the most ashamed I have ever been."

"Did you tell your wife?"

"I did not tell my wife. I have not told anyone." He set the glass down. "That is my whole life, right there in a sentence. I have not told anyone."

She was quiet. Outside, a shutter banged twice and stopped.

"My daughter knows," he said. "Not about the blood. She doesn't know anything. But two years ago she said to me, on the phone, she said, Dad, you know Bo-yan isn't like us, right? He's not like you and me. She said it like it was a joke. She was talking about the way he is, he's careful, he's a different kind of careful than we are. He saves his money, he reads the instructions, he doesn't shout. He is a completely different kind of man than I am." He looked at the candle. "And I sat in my car in the parking lot after that phone call for a long time."

"Do you think she knows?"

"I think she knows something. I think they all know something. I think the whole family has been walking around the room for twenty years and nobody has said the word." He shrugged. "That's why nobody says it."

"Why are you here?"

"Because it's the farthest I can get in a day," he said. "I looked at the map. Taipei is three hours. Kaohsiung is one. This is the far one. There's a ferry. If the ferry stops, it stops." He laughed, one short breath. "I didn't plan the typhoon."

"He's getting married."

"In a month. Wedding banquet at a restaurant in Hsinchu, three hundred people. His mother is doing the seating. And two weeks ago he asked me — he called me on the phone and he asked me — he said, Dad, I want you to be the one to speak. At the banquet. Before we eat, the father of the groom speaks, and he asked me." Jia-hao looked at his hands. "And I said, of course. And I have not slept properly since."

"About what?"

"Because I don't know if I'll say the right thing," he said. "Because I don't know which thing I'm going to say. Because I've been standing in front of a door for twenty years and now a waiter is going to hand me a microphone."

The rain kept on. The candles had burned down to stubs, all four of them at different heights, and the room smelled like paraffin and wet concrete and the sweet scorched smell of the noodles.

"Thank you for telling me," she said.

He waved a hand. "You told me yours."

"You said it was coming."

"I said it was coming because you told me yours and it was rude to leave you standing there on your own." He reached for the bottle and poured a finger into the glass and offered it to her and she took it. "Also because it wanted to come. It has wanted to come for a long time. You're just the first person I've met who wasn't already inside the room."

She drank.

"There's a thing I want to tell you," she said, "about the way I'll think about this."

"Don't."

"No. Listen." She put the glass down. "Tomorrow the ferry comes and we'll go back and you'll go to your shop and I'll go to the hospital and I will not tell anyone, and you will not tell anyone, and that's not the sad part. That's the good part. Right now you are the only person in the world who knows about me. That's not a debt. That's—" She looked for the word. "That's a place I can go. There's a room in my head now with you in it. And it doesn't have anything in it except what I told you, so it's clean. I've never had a room like that."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Okay," he said, and this time the word was a different word, and he picked up the empty noodle cups and stacked them one inside the other, one inside the other, one inside the other, all four, and put them down.

The morning after a typhoon is almost insulting.

The sky was a hard, rinsed blue. Every leaf on every tree was still dripping and the light coming through the water was so bright it hurt to look at, and the whole island was out in the street with brooms and shovels, dragging branches off the road, hauling the fallen boats upright. Nobody was angry. There was a strange holiday feeling to it. The mahuajuan shop had reopened and was giving away the damaged stock, and the girl from the 7-Eleven was out front in rubber boots, hosing the mud off the step.

A-ju came back at nine with a bag of wax apples from her son's tree and stood in the front room for a long time looking at the ceiling, and then said, in her mix of languages, that the tin would have to come off, all of it, and that she would have to find someone, and after the season, they were all busy, everyone was always busy.

The ten o'clock ferry left the harbor at 10:20, loaded with the people who had spent the last two days in the village hall and the temple and on the floors of their relatives' houses. It was not the big one; it was the smaller one they used when the sea was still being difficult, and it rolled heavily at the pier, and the porter took the bags and slung them into the hold.

There was a line at the ticket window. Jia-hao stood in it with her and then stepped out of it.

"I'll stay," he said. "One more day. I told her I'd look at the roof."

"That's her son's job."

"Her son runs a tour boat. He doesn't know a purlin from a pencil." He shrugged. "I'll look at it. If it's two days' work, I'll tell her, and she'll say no, and I'll do it anyway and she'll feed me fish for a week."

The line moved.

Si-yu took her ticket and walked up the ramp with her bag over her shoulder, and stood on the deck at the stern rail with the wind already pulling her hair across her face. The ferry's engine went up, that heavy diesel clatter, and the water between the boat and the pier began to widen and turn white.

He was standing on the pier in his windbreaker, hands in his pockets, in the middle of a harbor full of people with brooms and rubber boots and wet plywood, and he raised one hand, without any particular ceremony, the way you wave at somebody you might see again, or might not.

She raised hers.

She did not lower it while the pier receded and the harbor narrowed to a line and the island went from a shape to a color to a smudge, and the boat turned north into the long grey roll of the strait, and the sun on the water made it impossible to see anything at all.
