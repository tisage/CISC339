The first thing Eniola noticed when she entered her father’s house after the burial was that someone had moved the clock.

It had hung above the arch between the sitting room and dining room for thirty years, its brass pendulum swinging behind cloudy glass. Her father had wound it every Sunday morning before church, though it lost seven minutes a week and chimed at unpredictable hours. Now it stood on the sideboard beneath a framed photograph of their mother, leaving a pale square on the wall.

“Who put that there?” Eniola asked.

Her sister looked up from the dining table, where papers lay in small, exact piles.

“Good afternoon to you too.”

“I’m asking about the clock.”

“Bode’s boys knocked it while bringing in chairs after the visitors left. I moved it so it would not fall.”

“It has never fallen.”

“Everything falls eventually.”

At forty-six, Kemi still had the face their mother used to call deceptively soft: round cheeks, full mouth, wide eyes that made strangers assume kindness before she opened it. She wore a wrapper of dark blue adire and a white blouse with sleeves rolled above her wrists. A black biro rested behind one ear.

Eniola put her handbag on the nearest chair. “Where is Auntie Sade?”

“In the kitchen with the caterer’s pots. She says the women from the church have stolen three ladles.”

“Have they?”

“Does it matter?”

Eniola looked around the room. The plastic-covered sofas had been stripped of their covers for the mourners, revealing faded green upholstery. The tall cabinet still held their father’s glass animals, little antelopes and elephants he had collected during years of travelling for the Ministry of Works. The air smelled of jollof rice, camphor, sweat, and the lilies someone had placed beside his photograph.

In the picture, Chief Afolabi Akinwale wore a brown cap and a look of patient dissatisfaction. He had been seventy-eight when he died, sitting in his armchair after breakfast, one hand still wrapped around the newspaper. The doctor said it had been his heart. Auntie Sade said it was his pride, which had enlarged itself until the rest of him could no longer contain it.

Kemi tapped the papers.

“Mr. Olatunji is coming tomorrow at ten.”

“Tomorrow?”

“You told me you could only stay until Wednesday.”

“I said I had a hearing on Thursday.”

“So tomorrow is convenient.”

Eniola pulled out a chair but did not sit. “For what?”

“The will.”

“Baba had a will?”

Kemi’s eyes flickered, just once. “Apparently.”

“Apparently.”

“He told no one. The lawyer called me last week.”

“Why you?”

“He had my number.”

“He had mine too.”

“Yes,” Kemi said. “He did.”

There were many things Eniola could have said then. She could have asked why Kemi had not called sooner, why she had not mentioned the lawyer during the funeral arrangements, why their father’s last weeks had been described to her in clipped messages—Baba is resting. Baba has gone to the clinic. Baba says not to worry—as if worry were a tap Kemi could turn off from Ibadan.

Instead, she walked to the sideboard and lifted the clock.

It was heavier than she remembered. On the back, a strip of masking tape bore their father’s handwriting: DO NOT TILT.

“You should put this back,” she said.

Kemi smiled without warmth. “You have been in the house five minutes.”

“I know where things belong.”

“No,” Kemi said. “You remember where things belonged when you left.”

The kitchen door opened before Eniola could answer. Auntie Sade emerged holding a wooden spoon like a weapon. She was their father’s younger sister, a narrow woman in a starched black iro and buba, with silver hair tucked beneath a gele.

“Eniola! Ah, finally. Come and greet your aunt properly. You have grown thin in Lagos.”

“I have been thin since university, Auntie.”

“Then university has answered for its crimes.”

She pulled Eniola into a hug. Her body smelled of shea butter and smoke. Over Auntie Sade’s shoulder, Eniola saw Kemi gathering the papers into a folder.

“Tomorrow,” Kemi said.

Eniola nodded.

That night, she slept in her old room beneath the ceiling fan that clicked once on every rotation. The curtains were the same yellow flowered fabric their mother had sewn before her death. Kemi had not changed them. Neither had Baba.

On the bookshelf stood the old trophies from secondary school: Eniola’s debate cup with a cracked handle, Kemi’s inter-house athletics medal, a plastic plaque for “Most Promising Student” that Baba had displayed more proudly than either girl had found reasonable. Eniola lay awake looking at them.

When they were children, their father had pitted them against each other so casually that neither of them had noticed at first.

See how Kemi runs, he would say, as though Eniola’s feet had personally failed him.

See how Eniola speaks English, he would say to Kemi, as though language were a trick and not something learned from books.

When report cards came, he placed them side by side on the dining table. When guests visited, he summoned one daughter to recite a poem and the other to demonstrate a handstand. If Eniola won a prize, Kemi would be told that speed mattered less than discipline. If Kemi came first in a race, Eniola would hear that clever girls should not let their bodies become lazy.

Their mother, Morenike, had tried to soften this. She bought both of them mango sweets after results day, whatever the results were. She told them their father had been raised by a man who believed affection spoiled children.

“He loves you,” she would say.

“Then why does he behave like he hates us?” Kemi asked once.

Their mother had laughed sadly. “Because some people only know one language and are ashamed to learn another.”

After their mother died, when Eniola was sixteen and Kemi thirteen, the competitions grew sharper. It was Kemi who stayed home when their father fell ill with malaria. It was Eniola who earned the scholarship to university in Lagos. It was Kemi who learned to cook the soups their father liked. It was Eniola who left after graduation with two suitcases and a job at a legal chambers in Victoria Island.

Their father had not stopped her. At the motor park, he had pressed a brown envelope into her hand for the fare and said, “Do not disgrace yourself.”

For years, that was nearly all they said to each other.

At ten the following morning, Mr. Olatunji arrived in a cream safari suit despite the heat. He had been their father’s solicitor for twenty-two years and seemed to have inherited some of Baba’s mannerisms: the careful clearing of the throat, the air of having already heard every foolish thing anyone might say.

Auntie Sade sat at the head of the dining table. Kemi sat to her right. Eniola sat opposite, with the clock returned to its place on the wall.

Mr. Olatunji opened a leather folder.

“Your father executed this will three years ago,” he said. “It is properly witnessed and registered.”

“Three years,” Eniola repeated.

“Yes.”

Kemi’s fingers tightened around her teacup.

The lawyer began with money. There were two fixed deposits, a current account, shares in a telecommunications company, and a small pension balance. Auntie Sade received a monthly stipend for the rest of her life. The church received a donation for the repair of its roof, which made Auntie Sade snort.

“He complained about that roof every rainy season,” she said. “Now he has died and suddenly he is generous.”

Mr. Olatunji continued. The money, after the stipulated gifts and debts, was to be divided equally between the two daughters.

Then came the house.

The house on Oremeji Street, built by Chief Afolabi Akinwale in 1987, with its two-bedroom boys’ quarters, its overgrown compound, its unreliable generator, its mango tree, its guava tree, and its cracked front veranda, was left solely to Kemi Akinwale.

Eniola heard the words but did not understand them at first.

Mr. Olatunji went on, perhaps expecting a reaction. “There is also a note in your father’s handwriting, attached as an expression of wishes. It is not legally binding, but he requested that it be read.”

Kemi said, “Read it.”

The lawyer unfolded a single sheet.

“My daughters,” he read, “I have considered their different circumstances. Kemi has given more of her life to this house and shall have the house. Eniola has her profession and her independence. I trust they will each understand what is fair.”

The ceiling fan clicked. Once. Twice.

Eniola stared at the lawyer’s mouth. She thought, absurdly, that he had a small piece of biscuit caught in his moustache.

Kemi did not look at her.

“Different circumstances,” Eniola said.

Auntie Sade said quickly, “Your father had his reasons.”

“Did he?”

“Of course he did.”

Eniola leaned back in her chair. “Kemi is married. Her husband has a house in Akobo.”

“Had,” Kemi said quietly.

“What?”

“My husband had a house in Akobo.”

Eniola looked at her.

Kemi met her eyes at last. “Tunde moved out six months ago.”

Nobody spoke.

“He has moved out before,” Auntie Sade said, as if clarifying an ordinary matter. “Men behave foolishly. It does not mean anything.”

“It means he moved out,” Kemi said.

Eniola felt a brief, hot shame. She had not known. She had spoken to Kemi perhaps ten times in the past year, each conversation about Baba’s medication, the gate repair, whether the old cook had been paid. Kemi had never mentioned Tunde.

But shame shifted quickly into something else.

“So Baba knew,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And that makes this fair?”

Kemi’s face changed. “Do you think I asked him?”

“I think you knew about the will.”

“No.”

“You said apparently.”

“I knew there was a will. I did not know what was in it.”

Mr. Olatunji adjusted his folder. “Mrs. Akinwale—”

“No,” Eniola said. “Let her answer.”

Kemi’s voice grew hard. “You want the truth? Fine. Baba called me to his room three years ago. He said he had settled his affairs. He said I should not worry about anything. I assumed he meant the money, or this house, because I was the one here, changing his dressings and arguing with plumbers and taking him to the hospital when his driver disappeared for three days.”

“I sent money.”

“Yes. You sent money.”

“I had a job.”

“And I did not?”

“I never said you did not.”

“No. You just left all your sentences unfinished, so everyone else could hear what you meant.”

Auntie Sade said, “Kemi.”

But Eniola was already standing.

“Congratulations,” she said. “You have the house.”

She walked out before anyone could stop her.

In the compound, the afternoon sun had bleached the red earth nearly white. The mango tree rattled with birds. On the far wall, the cement had cracked in a zigzag line from the top to the ground.

Eniola stood beneath the tree and took out her phone. There were three missed calls from her chambers, two messages from a client, and one from her husband, Kunle: How did it go?

She typed, Fine. Baba was generous.

Then she deleted it.

She had married Kunle eleven years earlier, against her father’s wishes. Not because Kunle was bad—he was gentle, irritatingly patient, and a sound engineer who spent half his life among cables and musicians—but because he was not what Baba had imagined. He had no title, no family compound, no appetite for public displays of prosperity. Their flat in Yaba was small, bright, and permanently crowded with books, instruments, and the half-finished school projects of their daughter, Simisola.

Baba had attended the wedding in a pale grey agbada and said almost nothing. Afterward, he told Eniola that marriage was a roof and she had chosen one made of leaves.

When Kunle’s music work had thinned during the pandemic, it was Eniola’s salary that carried the household. She did not resent it. But the fixed deposits would not buy a house. The family house, in an area of Ibadan now swallowed by new banks, pharmacies, and apartment blocks, could have changed many things.

It could have paid Simisola’s school fees through secondary school. It could have allowed them to leave their landlord’s leaking building. It could have been sold, divided, made useful.

Instead it had gone to Kemi, who had always been better at being needed.

The back door opened. Kemi stepped out.

“Don’t follow me,” Eniola said.

“I am not following you. I am checking the generator.”

“You have not even started it.”

Kemi folded her arms. “You are angry because Baba hurt you. I understand that.”

“You understand?”

“Yes.”

“You don’t understand anything.”

“Then explain it.”

Eniola laughed once. “You want me to explain why it is insulting to be told that having a profession means you deserve less?”

“I was not given a prize, Eniola.”

“You were given a house.”

“I was given a house with a roof that leaks, a tenant in the boys’ quarters who has not paid rent since February, and a generator older than my daughter.”

“You can sell it.”

“And go where?”

“To Akobo.”

Kemi flinched so slightly that Eniola almost missed it.

“Sorry,” Eniola said, though she was not.

Kemi looked away. “That is the problem with you. You apologize like you are setting a cup down. Carefully. Quietly. You still want everyone to see you are the civilized one.”

“And what do you want?”

“I wanted you to come when Baba was sick.”

The words struck with enough force that Eniola had no answer.

“I came,” she said after a moment.

“You came for weekends. You came with oranges and foreign biscuits. You sat beside him and made him laugh, and then you went back to Lagos before he started shouting at night.”

“He shouted at night?”

“Sometimes he thought Mummy was in the room. Sometimes he thought he was still at work. Once he called me Eniola and told me I had ruined my life.”

Eniola’s throat tightened.

Kemi continued, “Do you know what he said when he was awake? He asked about you. Every day. Has Eniola called? How is her daughter? Is Kunle still wasting time with music? He could talk about you for an hour and then look at me as if I had interrupted.”

“That is not my fault.”

“I know.”

“But you say it as if it is.”

“I say it because you always got to be the one he missed.”

Eniola stared at her sister. For a moment she saw the thirteen-year-old girl with plaited hair and scabbed knees, standing in the schoolyard after losing a race because she had stopped to retie one shoe. Their father had said, in front of everyone, “Second place is where people go when they lack hunger.”

That evening, Kemi had cut the laces from all her shoes.

“You could have left,” Eniola said.

Kemi laughed, though there was no humor in it. “Where?”

“To Lagos. To anywhere.”

“With what money? With what job? You left because you could.”

“I worked for it.”

“Yes. You did.” Kemi’s eyes shone now, but she did not cry. “You always worked very hard to make leaving look like courage, not escape.”

She turned and went inside.

For the rest of the day, they spoke only when necessary. Auntie Sade sent Eniola to buy bread from the bakery near the roundabout. Kemi inspected a cupboard full of old documents. At dinner, they ate amala and ewedu in separate silences while Auntie Sade complained about the church women’s missing ladles.

Afterward, Eniola went upstairs to her father’s room.

It had been closed since the burial. The key hung on a nail beside the door, as it always had. She let herself in.

The room was orderly in the way of spaces belonging to people who have recently died: too orderly, emptied of the small evidence of use. His slippers stood under the bed. His reading glasses rested on a folded newspaper from two weeks before his death. On the bedside table sat a bottle of blood-pressure tablets, half full.

Eniola opened drawers without knowing what she sought.

Socks. Handkerchiefs. Receipts. A rosary that had belonged to their mother, though Baba had not been Catholic. An envelope containing photographs: their parents at a beach in Badagry; Kemi as a toddler, naked and furious in a basin; Eniola at graduation, wearing a borrowed gown and looking stunned.

At the bottom of the wardrobe was a metal deed box. It was unlocked.

Inside were property papers, bank statements, a survey map, and a blue file marked OREMEJI DEVELOPMENT.

Eniola took it to the desk.

The first pages were correspondence with a company called Crown Meridian Properties. They had made an offer to buy the house and adjoining plots. The amount was large enough that Eniola read it twice.

There was a draft agreement. A valuation report. A letter from their father declining the offer two years earlier.

Then, beneath those, was a newer document.

A loan agreement.

Chief Afolabi Akinwale had borrowed money from Crown Meridian Properties eighteen months before his death. The house had been listed as security.

Eniola felt the blood drain from her face.

The loan amount was not enormous compared with the value of the property, but the interest rate was vicious. Several repayment notices had been sent. The latest was dated three weeks earlier. Default proceedings would begin within thirty days.

At the back of the file was a handwritten note in Baba’s thin, unsteady script.

Kemi,

Do not let them frighten you. Olatunji knows what to do. The land is worth more than they say. Sell only if you must, and do not trust anyone who tells you family has no value.

Baba.

Eniola read it three times.

There was no mention of why he had borrowed the money.

She looked through the bank statements. Transfers had gone out in irregular amounts: hospital bills, pharmacy payments, cash withdrawals. One payment to Tunde Adeyemi. Another to Kemi Akinwale. Then, six months ago, a transfer for nearly all the remaining loan money to a private school in Ibadan.

The reference read: A. ADEYEMI FEES.

Kemi’s daughter. Ayo.

Eniola sat down.

Ayo was fourteen, bright and solemn, with Kemi’s face and none of Kemi’s caution. She had been sent away to boarding school in Jos two years before, after Kemi announced that it would “give her options.” Eniola had thought the fees came from Tunde’s business.

There was a sound at the door.

Kemi stood there, holding a tray with two cups of tea.

“You found it,” she said.

“You knew.”

“I knew about the loan.”

“Did Baba borrow money for Ayo’s school?”

Kemi set the tray down. “Partly.”

“And Tunde?”

“He borrowed from Baba for a contract. The contract failed. Then he borrowed more to cover the first loan. That is how men like him solve problems. By making them bigger.”

“Why did Baba give him money?”

“Because I asked.”

Eniola looked at her sister.

“Tunde had already begun selling things,” Kemi said. “My jewelry. The second car. He said he was saving us from embarrassment. I thought if Baba helped him, he would settle himself.”

“And did he?”

“No.”

“Then Baba mortgaged the house.”

“He wanted to pay off the school before Tunde touched that too.”

Eniola pushed the file away as if it had become dirty.

“So this is what he left you. A house you may lose.”

Kemi gave a tired smile. “Perhaps he thought it suited me. Something to look after.”

“Why did you not tell me?”

“What would you have done?”

“I am a lawyer.”

“You would have told me I should not have let it happen.”

“I would have helped.”

“You would have helped after you finished being right.”

Eniola stood. “That is unfair.”

“Is it?”

“Yes.”

Kemi’s expression hardened. “When Mummy was dying, you remember what you did?”

The room seemed to narrow.

“I was sixteen.”

“You were sixteen and you went to school. Every day. You said you had exams.”

“I had exams.”

“You came home one afternoon and Mummy had been taken to the hospital. You were angry because nobody had told you. You shouted at me in the corridor. You said, ‘Why didn’t you call me?’”

“I remember.”

“I had been twelve years old. Baba told me to stay with her because he was afraid she would be alone. I had not slept properly for three nights. And you shouted because nobody had called you.”

Eniola remembered differently. Or perhaps she had trained herself to. She remembered arriving at the hospital and seeing Kemi sitting on the floor outside the ward, her school uniform stained with something dark. She remembered fear rising so quickly it became rage. She remembered saying words she had never repeated.

You always want to be the important one.

Kemi had looked at her then with such naked hatred that Eniola had turned away.

Their mother died before dawn.

“I was a child,” Eniola said now.

“So was I.”

The fan ticked overhead. Somewhere downstairs, Auntie Sade shouted at someone to close the gate.

Kemi picked up the tea tray again.

“I am not asking you for the house,” she said. “Take the money Baba left you. Go back to your life. You have always been very good at that.”

She left the room.

Eniola did not sleep.

At dawn, she dressed quietly and walked to the junction to buy akara wrapped in old newspaper. The city was waking around her: buses coughing smoke into the road, schoolchildren in uniforms moving in knots, women arranging oranges in pyramids beneath umbrellas. A young man pushed a wheelbarrow full of loaves from the bakery. The air held the soft, grey coolness that came before the sun gathered itself.

At the gate, she found Auntie Sade watering plants with a dented plastic bucket.

“You are up early,” her aunt said.

“I couldn’t sleep.”

“Good. Sleep is for people with uncomplicated consciences.”

Eniola held out the akara. “I brought breakfast.”

Auntie Sade accepted the parcel. “Bribery is also for people with uncomplicated consciences.”

They stood in silence while water darkened the soil around the hibiscus.

“Auntie,” Eniola said, “did you know Baba mortgaged the house?”

Auntie Sade stopped pouring.

“I knew he borrowed money,” she said. “I did not know the full terms.”

“Why did nobody tell me?”

“Your father said not to.”

“And you listened?”

“I am his sister, not his jailer.”

“He was sick. He was making decisions that affected all of us.”

“He was a stubborn man long before he became sick.”

Eniola looked at the wet leaves. “He left Kemi a problem.”

“He left both of you problems. He was equal in that, at least.”

“Did he ever say why he gave her the house?”

Auntie Sade emptied the bucket beside the last plant.

“He said Kemi needed a place no one could send her away from.”

Eniola felt something sharp turn beneath her ribs.

“And me?”

“He said you would find your own way anywhere.”

“That sounds like praise.”

“It was, in his mind.”

“In his mind.”

Auntie Sade glanced at her. “You wanted him to say you were his favorite. He wanted you to need nothing from him. That is how foolish people wound each other.”

Eniola said nothing.

After breakfast, Mr. Olatunji returned with documents for the transfer of accounts. Eniola asked him to stay.

Kemi was in the dining room with Ayo on video call. Eniola could hear the girl’s voice from the phone, bright and worried. “Mummy, are you sure? I can come home for the term.”

“No,” Kemi said. “Face your books. Your grandfather would haunt us both if you failed chemistry.”

“He already haunts us.”

“Then he can mark your papers.”

When the call ended, Eniola placed the loan file on the table.

Kemi’s face closed.

“What now?”

“Mr. Olatunji has explained the options,” Eniola said.

“I know the options.”

“I want to hear them.”

The lawyer sat with his hands folded. “Crown Meridian is likely to press for repayment. The house could be sold in a private sale, which would bring a better price than a forced sale. Alternatively, the loan could be refinanced, though that would require steady income and a guarantor.”

Kemi said, “I cannot refinance it.”

“No,” Eniola said. “But I can.”

Kemi looked at her slowly.

“I am not offering to take the house,” Eniola said. “I am offering to guarantee a short-term loan. We sell the boys’ quarters plot separately.”

“The boys’ quarters plot?” Auntie Sade said. “Where will Bode live?”

“Bode has been living there without rent for twelve years,” Eniola said. “He can find another place.”

“His mother worked for this family.”

“And we have paid him more than enough for it.”

Mr. Olatunji cleared his throat. “The survey map does show that the rear section can be subdivided. There has been interest from a pharmacy chain.”

Kemi was still watching Eniola with suspicion. “Why?”

“Because the house is worth saving, if you want to save it.”

“Do you?”

Eniola looked around the room. At the cabinet of glass animals. At their mother’s photograph. At the clock, which had stopped sometime in the night.

“No,” she said. “Not for myself.”

Kemi’s mouth tightened. “I cannot pay you back.”

“I know.”

“Then what are you saying?”

“I am saying we make an agreement. You keep the house. I guarantee the loan. When the rear plot is sold, the debt is cleared. The remaining money goes into a trust for Ayo’s school fees and Simisola’s. Equal amounts.”

Auntie Sade inhaled sharply.

Kemi said, “And what do you get?”

“A legal interest in the trust, so nobody does anything foolish with it.”

“That is not what I meant.”

Eniola touched the stopped clock with one finger.

“I get to stop pretending Baba’s decisions are the same thing as justice.”

For a long time, no one spoke.

Then Kemi gave a short laugh that almost broke in the middle.

“You always have to make it sound like a court judgment.”

“It is a bad habit.”

“Yes,” Kemi said. “It is.”

But she sat down.

The negotiations with Crown Meridian took six weeks.

Eniola returned to Lagos for court hearings and came back to Ibadan every Friday evening. Kemi met with estate agents and surveyors. Auntie Sade objected to every proposal until the pharmacy chain’s representative brought documents showing that they would build a clinic beside the new shop, at which point she declared it was clearly God’s plan.

Bode moved out after three days of shouting and one afternoon in which Eniola discovered that he had been selling diesel from the generator tank. He left with a suitcase, a fan, and two of Baba’s old shirts. Kemi let him keep the shirts.

Tunde appeared once, unannounced, in a red SUV with tinted windows. He stood outside the gate calling Kemi’s name.

Eniola was in the front room reviewing contracts. She heard Kemi’s voice before she saw them.

“You cannot come here.”

“I came to talk to my wife.”

“You came because you heard there may be money.”

“That is an insult.”

“It is not enough of one.”

Tunde spotted Eniola through the window and lifted a hand in greeting, as though they were relatives at a naming ceremony.

“Eniola,” he called. “Please, tell your sister to behave sensibly.”

Eniola stepped onto the veranda.

“Leave,” she said.

He smiled. “You lawyers enjoy giving instructions.”

“I said leave.”

“This is family business.”

“No,” Kemi said from behind him. “It is not.”

Tunde’s smile vanished. For a second, he looked almost young, almost frightened. Then he got into his car and drove away, scattering gravel against the gate.

That night, Kemi sat on the veranda with a bottle of malt and said, “I should have left him years ago.”

Eniola sat beside her. “Yes.”

Kemi glanced at her.

Eniola lifted her own bottle. “You wanted honesty.”

They both laughed then, loudly enough that Auntie Sade shouted from inside the house to ask whether grief had finally finished their brains.

The rear plot sold for less than Eniola had hoped and more than Kemi had feared. The loan was paid. Crown Meridian sent a courteous letter expressing regret that the family had chosen not to proceed with the full-property sale.

On the day the final documents were signed, rain came down in sheets over Ibadan. The roads flooded quickly, turning gutters into rushing brown streams. Mr. Olatunji arrived late with the papers tucked inside a plastic bag beneath his shirt.

Kemi signed first.

Her hand trembled only once.

Eniola signed after her, as trustee of the education fund. The arrangement was not elegant. It did not erase the will or alter the deed. The house remained Kemi’s. The fixed deposits and shares had been divided. The trust held enough, with careful management, for two girls whose futures still seemed distant and expensive.

When it was done, Mr. Olatunji stood.

“Your father would have been pleased,” he said.

“No,” Kemi replied.

The lawyer paused.

“He would have complained about the interest rate,” Eniola said.

Mr. Olatunji’s mouth twitched. “That also is true.”

After he left, Kemi brought the old clock down from the wall.

“I had it repaired,” she said.

Eniola looked at it. The brass had been polished. The cloudy glass was clear again.

“You repaired it?”

“The clockmaker near Mapo. He said it only needed cleaning.”

“It stopped.”

“Everything stops if nobody winds it.”

Kemi carried it to the sideboard, hesitated, then looked at Eniola.

“Should we put it back?”

Eniola watched the pale square on the wall where it had hung for most of her life.

“No,” she said.

Kemi nodded.

Together they carried it into the dining room and set it on the long table between their mother’s photograph and a bowl of artificial fruit. Kemi found the key hanging from a chain beneath the case. She handed it to Eniola.

“You do it,” she said.

Eniola took the key.

For a moment she could not remember the motion. Then she inserted it into the small brass hole and turned.

The mechanism caught with a dry, delicate sound. The pendulum began to swing.

One way. Then the other.

Outside, rain tapped at the roof. In the compound, water gathered around the roots of the mango tree, darkening the earth where, years ago, two girls had raced each other barefoot from the gate to the veranda until their father called them inside.
