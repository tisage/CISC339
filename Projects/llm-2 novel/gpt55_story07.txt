The first time Isadora Cavalcanti de Melo heard about the blue barrels, it was raining so hard in Fortaleza that the newsroom windows turned white.

Rain in the capital had a way of making everyone pretend the sertão was close, that the cracked inland towns and their low thorny bushes were only a misunderstanding of geography. In the glass towers along Avenida Santos Dumont, people complained of traffic and wet shoes. In the newsroom of O Farol, the ceiling leaked into a plastic wastebasket beside the politics desk, making a hollow, patient sound.

Isadora had her sandals off under her chair and a spreadsheet open on her screen. The spreadsheet concerned campaign donations from construction firms to three state deputies, the sort of story that required more coffee than sleep. Her editor, Mateus, leaned over the partition with his phone in one hand.

“Dora,” he said. “Do you still have people in Santa Quitéria?”

“My aunt lives there,” she said, not looking up. “Why? Did somebody’s cow get elected mayor?”

He smiled without smiling. “A teacher sent me photos. Children with stomach infections in Morrinhos dos Ventos. She says it started after the cistern program delivered new filtration barrels.”

“Municipal?”

“State partnership with an NGO. Casa das Águas.”

Isadora’s hands stopped above the keyboard.

Mateus noticed. “You know them?”

“Everybody knows them.”

That wasn’t true, not exactly. But in Ceará, in the years when drought came like a second government, everyone in the interior knew the name of Dra. Célia Araripe. Doctor, activist, daughter of a judge from Crato, woman with silver hair cut blunt at the chin and a voice that never rose because it never needed to. She had spent thirty years arguing for cisterns instead of water trucks, community wells instead of favors from mayors, health posts where women did not have to beg for prenatal vitamins from a councilman’s secretary. Her organization, Casa das Águas, had begun in two rooms behind a church and now appeared on banners beside ministers and foreign donors.

When Isadora was fourteen, her younger brother, André, had developed dengue hemorrhagic fever in Juazeiro do Norte during a week when the local hospital had no platelets and no one answered the phone in Fortaleza. It was Célia Araripe who arrived in a dusty white Hilux with two nurses, shouted at a director until doors opened, and sat with Isadora’s mother through the worst night. Later, when André recovered, Célia sent him a book about beetles because someone told her he liked insects. On the first page, she wrote, Para André, que vai sobreviver a muita coisa bonita ainda.

Isadora had become a journalist in part because of women like that, women who stepped into rooms where other people waited politely outside.

“What kind of infections?” she asked.

“Diarrhea. Vomiting. Two hospitalizations. One child in Sobral, serious.”

“The barrels could be contaminated during transport.”

“Could be.” Mateus held out his phone.

The photo showed a row of blue plastic barrels under the white sun, each with a black tap near the bottom and a sticker: ÁGUA LIMPA, VIDA DIGNA. Below it were the logos of the state government, Casa das Águas, and HidraNorte Soluções Hídricas.

“Never heard of HidraNorte,” Isadora said.

“Neither have I. Go tomorrow?”

She looked at the rain sliding down the glass.

“Today,” she said.

The bus to Santa Quitéria left at six in the evening, damp with passengers and plastic bags. By the time it reached the interior, the rain had become memory. Isadora sat by the window watching Fortaleza dissolve into gas stations, then roadside chapels, then dark.

She had been born in Crato, where the hills were green enough in certain months for people to believe in mercy. Her father taught history at a public school and corrected the grammar of radio announcers aloud. Her mother sold fabrics from a stall near the market and knew everyone’s ailments, debts, daughters, scandals. Isadora grew up listening to adult conversations from behind bolts of cloth: who had gone to São Paulo and returned with money, whose son had joined a church with guitars, which mayor had paved the street to his mistress’s house.

Journalism, to her, had never felt like entering the public world. It felt like refusing to leave the private one.

In Santa Quitéria, her cousin Tiago borrowed a neighbor’s motorcycle and drove her seventy kilometers farther, toward Morrinhos dos Ventos. The road narrowed, then surrendered. Dust rose around them in a red veil. The land opened flat and pale, with mandacaru cactus lifting its arms like someone too tired to ask again.

Morrinhos was a cluster of houses around a square where the church paint had peeled from blue to bone. Goats slept in the shade of a campaign billboard left over from the election, the candidate’s smile punctured by sun.

The teacher who had written to Mateus was named Marlene, a square-shouldered woman in her fifties with a rosary bracelet and a voice made hoarse by chalk dust. She met Isadora outside the school, where children in uniforms chased one another in a yard with no tree.

“I didn’t want to make trouble,” Marlene said, before Isadora asked anything. “I called the health secretary first. Three times. They said it was virus season.”

“Is it?”

“It’s always virus season when they don’t want paperwork.”

Inside her classroom, Marlene pulled a folder from a metal cabinet. She had written names, dates, symptoms, households, whether each family had received a barrel. Her handwriting was careful, looping. Seventeen children ill in three weeks. Eight adults. All but two used water filtered through the new blue barrels.

“Who delivered them?”

“Men from HidraNorte. Two trucks. The mayor gave a speech. Dra. Célia was not here, but she sent a video.”

She said Célia’s name with the same hesitation Isadora felt, as if invoking a saint in a police report.

“Do you have one of the barrels?”

Marlene’s mouth tightened. “My sister does.”

They walked under the sun to a house at the edge of town. The sister, a thin woman named Rosângela, stood in the doorway with a baby on her hip and invited them in. The barrel sat in the kitchen, elevated on bricks, its blue plastic bright against the soot-dark wall. Isadora crouched beside it. The sticker was peeling at one corner.

“Do you mind if I take photos?”

“Take the whole thing,” Rosângela said. “Since it came, my boys spend more time in the bathroom than in school.”

The inside smelled faintly of chemicals, not strong, but wrong. Isadora unscrewed the lid and photographed the filter disk. It looked cheap, a gray pad fitted loosely where ceramic should have been.

“Were there instructions?”

“A boy in a vest said wash it once and use. He was in a hurry.”

Outside, a pickup truck rolled slowly past the house. The driver looked at them, looked away, then accelerated.

Marlene saw Isadora watching. “That’s Raimundinho. Works for the mayor when there is work. For others when there isn’t.”

“Has he been here before?”

“Everyone has been here before. Morrinhos is small.”

By late afternoon, Isadora had interviewed six families, the nurse at the health post, and one boy still weak enough that he fell asleep while she spoke to his grandmother. She photographed medical prescriptions, receipts for bottled water bought by people who could not afford bottled water, and a municipal memo warning residents to “avoid alarmist rumors regarding public health.”

The nurse, Janaína, waited until they were alone to say, “I sent stool samples to Sobral. No answer. I also sent water samples.”

“To the state lab?”

She shook her head. “To my cousin at the university. Quietly.”

“And?”

“He called yesterday. E. coli in three samples. Also high residue from industrial solvent in one, but he wants to test again.”

“Solvent?”

“He said maybe from recycled plastic. I don’t know.” Janaína folded and unfolded a prescription pad. “I voted for this mayor. My husband says to stop talking.”

“Why are you talking?”

The nurse looked toward the waiting room, where an old man coughed into a towel.

“Because my daughter drinks water too.”

On the ride back to Santa Quitéria, Isadora held her notebook closed against the wind. Her phone buzzed when signal returned: a message from an unknown number.

Careful with people who want to use you. Casa das Águas saves lives. Ask who benefits from attacking them.

She stared at it until Tiago slowed at a curve.

“Everything all right?” he called.

“Everything is always all right until it isn’t.”

She slept at her aunt’s house in a room with a fan that clicked every seven seconds. In the morning, there were three missed calls from Mateus and one voice message from Dra. Célia Araripe.

“Isadora, minha filha, I heard you were in Morrinhos. I wish you had called me. I’m in Fortaleza tomorrow. Come by the office. We’ll show you whatever you need. There is a lot of misinformation, and I would hate for your work to be used by the wrong people.”

Her voice was exactly as Isadora remembered it: calm, warm, certain.

Casa das Águas occupied a restored warehouse near Praia de Iracema, all white walls, potted plants, and photographs of rural women smiling beside cisterns. Isadora arrived the next afternoon with dust still in her shoes. A receptionist offered coffee in a cup stamped with the NGO’s logo.

Célia Araripe came down the stairs in linen trousers and leather sandals, her silver hair shining under the skylight. She embraced Isadora with both arms.

“Look at you,” she said, holding her at arm’s length. “Your mother sends me photos from time to time. André is an engineer now?”

“Entomologist,” Isadora said. “Close enough if you are a beetle.”

Célia laughed. “He always had a strange elegance, that boy.”

For a moment, Isadora was fourteen again, sitting on a hospital floor with her knees against her chest, watching this woman bring order into terror.

They went to a conference room. On the table, someone had arranged binders, procurement forms, printed emails, and a pitcher of water with mint leaves. Célia sat across from Isadora, not at the head.

“I’ll be direct,” she said. “The Morrinhos situation is concerning, and we are investigating. But there are municipal elections next year. Some groups would love to damage the cistern program. They prefer water trucks because trucks are votes on wheels.”

“The families I spoke with aren’t political groups.”

“No. They are frightened people. Frightened people can be guided.”

“By whom?”

Célia sighed. “Mayor Paulo Sérgio has enemies. The state secretary has enemies. I have enemies. You will have enemies too, if you continue to do your job.”

“I already have a few. They’re not very imaginative.”

The older woman smiled, but it faded quickly.

“HidraNorte supplied the barrels?” Isadora asked.

“Yes.”

“Why that company? They seem new.”

“The state procurement office approved them. Casa das Águas is the technical partner, not the purchasing authority.”

“Your logo is on the barrels.”

“Our reputation helps people accept the program. That’s why we are careful.”

“Were the filters ceramic?”

“They were supposed to be.” Célia slid a binder forward. “Specifications. Independent lab certificate. Delivery reports.”

The documents appeared in order. Too much in order, perhaps. Isadora had learned to distrust binders prepared before questions were asked.

“Did your team inspect the deliveries?”

“In sample lots.”

“In Morrinhos?”

Célia paused. Only a second.

“I would have to confirm.”

Isadora wrote that down.

Célia leaned back. “You think I’m hiding something.”

“I think children are sick.”

“Children are always sick in places where the water comes from a hole in the ground and politicians remember the town only for cameras.”

“Then the barrels mattered.”

“Yes. They mattered.” Célia’s voice sharpened, then softened. “Dora, do you know how many wells we have built? How many girls stay in school because they don’t spend mornings carrying water? One supplier may have failed. A municipal worker may have stored equipment improperly. We will find out. But if the story becomes ‘Casa das Águas poisons children,’ donors leave, programs freeze, and the people who suffer are the same ones you interviewed.”

The mint leaves floated in the pitcher like drowned insects.

“I’m not looking for a slogan,” Isadora said.

“No. You are looking for truth.” Célia’s eyes rested on her face with something like pity. “Just remember that truth is not a stone you find whole in the road. Someone always cut it from somewhere.”

Before Isadora left, Célia touched her arm.

“How is your mother’s arthritis?”

“Better in dry weather.”

“Tell her to call me. There’s a doctor in Crato I trust.”

Outside, the sea was a hard strip of blue beyond the buildings. Isadora sat in her car for ten minutes without turning the key.

The first article ran cautiously: “Families Report Illness After Delivery of Water Filtration Barrels in Rural Ceará.” It quoted residents, the nurse, the municipality, HidraNorte’s brief statement, and Casa das Águas promising investigation. It did not mention solvent residue because the second test was not complete.

Within hours, the mayor of Morrinhos dos Ventos posted a video calling the story “terrorism against the poor.” A state deputy shared it with three laughing emojis. Then a mother from a different village sent Isadora photos of a blue barrel with the same sticker. Another message came from a community radio host. Then another from a health agent in Canindé.

By the end of the week, O Farol had mapped thirty-six complaints in nine municipalities.

The lab reports arrived on a Friday evening. Five water samples from three towns showed fecal contamination. Two contained traces of toluene and xylene consistent with improperly treated recycled plastic. The ceramic filters specified in the contract had been replaced with compressed fiber pads, cheaper and less effective.

“Now it’s not one supplier failing,” Mateus said.

They were alone in the newsroom, the weekend staff reduced to a sports intern and a photographer editing images of a footballer’s wedding.

“No,” Isadora said. “Now it’s a system.”

“A system with signatures.”

He dropped photocopies on her desk. They had come from a source in the state procurement office: invoices, delivery certifications, payment releases. Several bore the digital approval of Casa das Águas’ technical director, a man named Cláudio Sampaio. Others included a note: “Alteração de material autorizada em caráter emergencial.” Material change authorized on an emergency basis.

The authorizing signature was Célia Araripe’s.

Isadora read the line three times.

“There may be context,” she said.

Mateus watched her over his glasses. “There is always context. Get it.”

That night, she called her mother.

“Dra. Célia?” Dona Lurdes said after Isadora explained only enough to make the silence heavy. “Are you sure?”

“No.”

“Then be sure before you put her name in anything.”

“I know.”

“She saved your brother.”

“I know, mãe.”

“And if she did something wrong?”

Isadora lay on her sofa, looking at the ceiling fan moving shadows around the room.

Her mother exhaled. “Then be more sure.”

On Monday morning, an envelope waited under the windshield wiper of Isadora’s car. Inside was a flash drive wrapped in a receipt from a bakery in Messejana. No note.

Mateus wanted IT to open it on an isolated laptop. The drive contained audio files, photos of documents, and a spreadsheet named HN_pagamentos_final. The metadata had been scrubbed poorly enough to suggest fear rather than sophistication.

The spreadsheet listed transfers from HidraNorte to a consulting firm called Sertão Vivo Estratégia Social. The firm’s owner, according to the corporate registry, was Célia’s nephew, Lucas Araripe, a smiling man of thirty-two whose Instagram showed triathlon medals, beach clubs, and one photo with his arm around his aunt at a Casa das Águas gala.

The transfers began two weeks after HidraNorte won the barrel contract and continued after each state payment. Total: R$ 1.8 million.

“Consulting?” Mateus said.

“Maybe legitimate.”

He gave her a look.

“I said maybe.”

She found Lucas’s office in a coworking space with exposed brick and motivational phrases painted on the wall in English. He was not there, but a receptionist said he came “rarely, for meetings.” His company had no website beyond a landing page. Its listed phone number rang at a pet grooming salon in Maracanaú.

The audio files were worse.

At first, they were only muffled meetings: male voices, coffee cups, the hum of air conditioning. Then Célia’s voice emerged, unmistakable.

“We cannot delay distribution another six months. The communities are waiting.”

A man Isadora later identified as Cláudio said, “The alternative filters are not certified for this use.”

“They are temporary.”

“The contract says—”

“I know what the contract says.”

Another male voice, younger, perhaps Lucas: “The supplier can meet the deadline with the pads. The ceramic units are stuck in customs. If we miss the rainy-season schedule, storage costs explode, and the state cancels phase two.”

Cláudio again: “And if people get sick?”

A long silence.

Célia said, “Then we replace them as soon as the ceramic arrives. Quietly. With instructions to boil water.”

“You want that in writing?”

“I want children to have something rather than nothing.”

Listening, Isadora felt heat rise to her face, as if she had been caught doing something shameful.

There were three more recordings. In one, Lucas joked about “saving the old lady’s mission from her own ethics.” In another, HidraNorte’s director discussed payments to Sertão Vivo for “political smoothing.” The final file was a call between Célia and an unknown woman.

“You promised no one would be harmed,” Célia said.

“No one has been harmed. There are stomach viruses everywhere.”

“Don’t lie to me.”

“Then don’t ask questions you cannot afford.”

Isadora removed the headphones and sat very still. Through the newsroom glass, she could see Mateus arguing with someone on the phone, gesturing with a pen. Life continued obscenely: keyboards, coffee, the sports intern laughing at a meme.

She called Célia’s office. The assistant said Dra. Célia was traveling. Isadora called her cell. No answer. She sent a message: I have documents and recordings indicating you authorized substitute filters and that HidraNorte paid your nephew’s firm. I need to speak with you before publication.

The reply came two hours later.

Come to my house tonight. Alone, please.

Célia lived in an old apartment in Meireles, not the mansion Isadora half-expected from enemies’ whispers, but a wide, book-filled place with worn wooden floors and windows open to the sea air. On the walls were framed woodcuts, a photo of Célia as a young doctor in the 1980s wearing jeans and a stethoscope, another of her with rural women holding shovels.

She opened the door herself. She looked smaller without the architecture of office and staff around her.

“Coffee?” she asked.

“No, thank you.”

“Good. It’s terrible at this hour anyway.”

They sat in the living room. Somewhere below, a car alarm chirped and stopped.

Isadora placed copies of the documents on the table. Not the originals. Never the originals.

Célia did not touch them.

“How did you get recordings?” she asked.

“That’s not the question you want to start with.”

For the first time since Isadora had known her, Célia looked angry.

“Do not teach me what questions to ask in my own house.”

The anger passed as quickly as it came. She removed her glasses and pressed thumb and forefinger to her eyes.

“Lucas is an idiot,” she said.

“Is he a criminal?”

“He saw an opportunity. HidraNorte needed someone who understood mayors, secretaries, community associations. He created a consultancy.”

“With your knowledge?”

“I knew he had a contract. I did not know the amounts.”

“That’s hard to believe.”

“Yes,” Célia said. “Many true things are.”

“You authorized uncertified filters.”

Célia looked at the documents as if they had crawled there.

“I authorized temporary distribution of alternative filters under emergency conditions.”

“People used them as permanent filters because no one replaced them.”

“They were supposed to be replaced within forty-five days.”

“They weren’t.”

“No.”

“Did you know?”

Célia’s face changed. Not dramatically; she was too disciplined for that. But something in her mouth collapsed inward.

“In Morrinhos? No. In other places, I suspected delays.”

“Delays.”

“Call it what you want.”

“I’m asking what you call it.”

Célia stood and walked to the window. Beyond her, the black ocean folded and unfolded, indifferent.

“Do you know what happened before those barrels?” she asked.

“Don’t.”

“No, listen to me. In Lagoa do Mato, women were taking water from an abandoned quarry where goats drowned. In Barro Vermelho, a boy lost two fingers lowering a bucket into a well lined with scrap metal. We had funding approved, contracts signed, communities trained. Then customs held the ceramic filters, HidraNorte threatened to walk away, and the state said if we didn’t deliver before the fiscal deadline, the money returned to Brasília and the program died.”

“So you accepted a lie.”

“I accepted an interim measure.”

“You put your logo on it.”

“I put my life on it!” Célia turned from the window. Her voice did not rise much, but the room seemed to tighten around it. “Every mayor who smiles at a ribbon-cutting and steals from the next shipment, every secretary who delays a permit until his cousin gets hired, every donor who wants photographs of grateful women but not salaries for engineers—I carry them. I fight with mud in my mouth every day. And yes, I signed. Because if I refused, the whole program collapsed and ten thousand families waited another year.”

“Some children couldn’t wait three weeks.”

Célia flinched.

There it was: not victory, not satisfaction, only the ugly contact of fact with flesh.

Isadora’s phone vibrated in her bag. She ignored it.

“Why pay Lucas?” she asked.

Célia sank back into the chair. “I didn’t pay Lucas.”

“HidraNorte did.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because Lucas knows people. Because my surname opens doors and he sells the echo. Because I was vain enough to think my family could orbit this work without contaminating it.”

“Did he give you money?”

“No.”

“Did Casa das Águas receive any kickback?”

“No.”

“Did you report the conflict when you learned?”

A long silence.

“No.”

“Why?”

Célia laughed once, without humor. “Because I thought I could fix it quietly.”

“By replacing the filters quietly.”

“Yes.”

“By hiding illnesses quietly.”

“No.” She leaned forward. “No. I did not know people were ill until your story.”

“The recordings—”

“After your story. I called HidraNorte. That woman, their operations director, told me it was panic, politics, viruses. I wanted to believe her for twenty-four hours because I was tired.” She looked at Isadora then, directly. “There. Put that in your article if you need a confession. I was tired, and children drank bad water.”

Isadora thought of André in the hospital bed, his lips cracked, his small hand hot around hers. She thought of Célia sitting with her mother, refusing to leave. She thought of Rosângela’s boys doubled over in a house where the barrel stood like a promise.

“I don’t need a confession,” she said. “I need answers.”

“You have them.”

“Not all.”

Célia nodded slowly. “No. Not all.”

She rose and went to a bookshelf. From behind a row of health policy reports, she removed a folder.

“I was going to give this to the state prosecutor next week,” she said.

“Next week.”

“I know.”

Inside were internal emails, inspection reports, and a handwritten timeline. They showed that Casa das Águas technicians in two municipalities had flagged the substitute filters. Their reports were forwarded to Cláudio, then to Célia. Célia wrote: “Proceed with distribution where no safe source exists. Mark for urgent replacement. No public communication until replacement plan confirmed.”

There were also emails from HidraNorte delaying replacement, blaming ports, transport strikes, supplier bankruptcy. Then a message from Lucas to Célia: Tia, pressing them too hard now will blow up everything. Let me handle.

At the bottom of the folder was a resignation letter signed by Célia, dated but not submitted.

“Why give me this?” Isadora asked.

“Because you’ll get it anyway. Or you won’t, and the story will be simpler than it should be.”

“Are you trying to protect yourself?”

“Yes,” Célia said. “And the work. And perhaps, at this late hour, the people I failed. We are rarely pure in our motives. You’ll learn that, if you haven’t already.”

Isadora stood to leave. At the door, Célia said her name.

When Isadora turned, the older woman seemed suddenly aged beyond the evening.

“Your brother,” Célia said. “I remember him asking if mosquitoes had souls. Your mother laughed for the first time in days.”

Isadora swallowed. “He studies beetles now. Mosquitoes disappointed him.”

Célia smiled, and tears stood in her eyes but did not fall.

“Good,” she said. “Disappointment can be useful.”

The investigation published in three parts.

The first showed that HidraNorte had supplied uncertified filtration barrels to at least eleven municipalities, using cheaper substitute pads and recycled plastic containers containing chemical residues. The second traced payments from HidraNorte to Lucas Araripe’s consulting firm and detailed the conflict of interest. The third examined Casa das Águas’ internal decisions, including Célia’s authorization to distribute the substitute filters without public notice, her failure to disclose her nephew’s financial ties, and delayed action after field warnings.

Isadora argued with Mateus over every verb.

“‘Allowed’ is too soft,” he said.

“‘Ordered’ isn’t accurate.”

“‘Approved.’”

“Fine.”

Lawyers read the drafts. Sources panicked, recanted, returned. The unknown person who had sent the flash drive disappeared into silence. Cláudio, the technical director, agreed to speak on record only after HidraNorte claimed he had approved all substitutions independently.

“I warned them,” he told Isadora in a bakery at seven in the morning, hands shaking around a paper cup of coffee. “But I also signed delivery reports later. I told myself the same story everyone tells. That the next step will fix the last one.”

The day before publication, Isadora called Célia for final comment.

“I’m stepping down,” Célia said.

“Before the article?”

“After. If I do it before, people will say it is proof of everything and read nothing.”

“They may say that anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Do you want to give a statement?”

A pause.

“Write that I accept responsibility for decisions made under my authority, that I deny personal enrichment, and that Casa das Águas must cooperate fully with investigators.”

“That’s very official.”

“I have spent all day with lawyers.”

“And unofficially?”

Célia breathed through the phone. “Unofficially, Dora, I am sorry.”

Isadora closed her eyes.

“I can’t use that.”

“I know.”

When the stories went live, the reaction came like summer rain over asphalt: violent, steaming, brief in some places, pooling dangerously in others. The governor announced an inquiry. The health department suspended the barrel program. HidraNorte’s director denied wrongdoing from behind mirrored sunglasses outside a police station. Lucas Araripe posted that he was the victim of “digital lynching” and then deleted his social media.

In Morrinhos dos Ventos, residents gathered in the school to watch a television report on Marlene’s old set. Rosângela called Isadora afterward.

“They came today,” she said. “Men from the state. Took the barrel. Gave us bottled water for a week.”

“Only a week?”

“That’s what I said.” A child shouted in the background. Rosângela covered the phone and shouted back, then returned. “The boys are better.”

“Good.”

“The mayor says you hate poor people.”

“He’s said worse.”

Marlene came on the line. “He also says the barrels were sabotaged by opposition.”

“Of course.”

“But people are talking now.” The teacher lowered her voice. “Not only about barrels. About the water trucks. About the clinic medicine. Once a mouth opens, it remembers other words.”

Threats arrived. Most were dull, obscene messages from profiles with flags and Bible verses. One man sent a photo of O Farol’s entrance with the caption: Journalists also drink water. Mateus reported it. Nothing happened, then something did: a police car parked outside for two afternoons and left.

Isadora’s mother did not call until the second day.

“I read everything,” Dona Lurdes said.

“And?”

“You wrote like someone washing a wound.”

“I don’t know if that’s good.”

“It hurts. It is necessary. It can still infect.”

Only her mother could make comfort sound like a diagnosis.

“André called me,” Dona Lurdes added. “He said he is proud.”

“He could call me.”

“He said you don’t answer.”

“I’ve been busy.”

“You answer strangers.”

“That’s my job.”

“Family are the strangest people.” Her mother paused. “Dra. Célia sent me a message.”

Isadora sat down on the edge of her bed. “What did she say?”

“That she was sorry. That she hoped André was well.”

“And what did you say?”

“I said he was.”

“Only that?”

“What else was there?”

Célia resigned three days after publication. In her statement, she wore no makeup and stood before the Casa das Águas logo. She did not attack the press. She did not mention sabotage or enemies. She said she had made decisions she believed would prevent harm, and harm had followed. She said the organization’s work mattered beyond any one person, including herself.

Some praised her dignity. Others called it theater. The prosecutor opened investigations into HidraNorte, Lucas, municipal officials, and Casa das Águas leadership. Donors froze payments. Two rural water projects halted. On the radio, men who had spent careers trading water for votes declared themselves defenders of transparency.

A week later, Isadora returned to Morrinhos dos Ventos.

She told herself it was for follow-up reporting, which was true. Replacement filters had not arrived. Bottled water distribution was uneven. The state had promised tankers, and the first tanker came with a photographer, the second without water, the third not at all.

But she also went because the story had become too large on screens, too full of statements and procedures. She wanted to stand again where the dust stuck to her ankles and facts had faces.

Marlene met her with a straw hat.

“You look worse,” the teacher said.

“Thank you.”

“It means you worked.”

They walked to Rosângela’s house. The blue barrel was gone, leaving a clean circle on the floor where dust had not settled. In its place stood three plastic jugs, two already empty. Rosângela’s boys were outside trying to trap a lizard under a bucket.

“Have they been back?” Isadora asked.

“Only reporters,” Rosângela said, and gave her a look. “Some bring snacks. One asked me to cry again.”

“Again?”

“He said he missed the shot.”

Marlene muttered a word Isadora chose not to write down.

In the afternoon, Isadora sat in the school while Marlene’s students drew maps of their homes for a geography lesson. A girl with braids colored the well blue, the road red, the sun orange with long fierce rays. In the corner, she drew the missing barrel, then crossed it out.

“What will you put there instead?” Isadora asked.

The girl shrugged. “A tree.”

“Does one grow there?”

“No.”

She picked up a green pencil and began anyway.

On the bus back to Fortaleza, Isadora finally called André. He answered on the fifth ring, breathless, with wind behind him.

“Are you in a field?” she asked.

“Chapada Diamantina. Beetles wait for no sister.”

“You said you were proud.”

“I am. Also worried. Mostly proud. Don’t tell mãe; she prefers worry.”

“She knows.”

“She knows everything after it’s useless.”

They were quiet for a while, comfortable in the old way of siblings who had once shared the back seat on long trips through hot country.

“Do you remember Célia?” Isadora asked.

“Of course.”

“Do you hate her?”

“For almost killing me retroactively?”

“Don’t be stupid.”

“I’m trying not to.” Wind blew across the line. “I remember her bringing that beetle book. I remember mãe crying on her shoulder. I remember Pai pretending not to cry. Those things happened.”

“And now this happened.”

“Yes.”

“How do you put them together?”

“I don’t know. I study insects. We pin things separately in boxes.”

Despite herself, Isadora laughed.

André said, softer, “Maybe people don’t fit in one box. That doesn’t mean you don’t label the specimen.”

“You’ve been waiting your whole life to make journalism sound like beetle collecting.”

“It was inevitable.”

After they hung up, Isadora watched the sertão pass in the window: thorn trees, goats, satellite dishes, a boy flying a kite made from a plastic bag. Near dusk, clouds gathered without promising anything.

Months later, the first indictment named HidraNorte’s director, two state procurement officials, Lucas Araripe, and the mayor of Morrinhos dos Ventos, who had arranged storage of the barrels in a warehouse previously used for pesticides. Célia was charged not with corruption but with administrative misconduct and reckless endangerment. Her lawyers said they would contest the charges. Casa das Águas survived, though smaller; engineers replaced board members; donors returned cautiously, with auditors attached like barnacles.

Isadora received an invitation to speak at a journalism seminar in São Paulo about “Accountability in Civil Society Partnerships.” She declined. Mateus told her she was allergic to panels because panels did not bleed.

One evening in December, when Fortaleza was hot enough after sunset that the walls seemed to breathe, an envelope arrived at O Farol. It contained no return address. Inside was a single page torn from a notebook.

Dora,

I am told not to write to you, so I will keep this brief.

There is an inventory of ceramic filters in a warehouse outside Sobral. They were purchased with emergency funds after your articles and have not been distributed because three departments are arguing over transport responsibility. The communities listed include Morrinhos dos Ventos.

Do what you do.

C.

Below that was an address, a contact name, and a schedule of warehouse hours.

Isadora read it twice. Then she walked to Mateus’s desk and dropped the page in front of him.

He looked at the handwriting. “Is this who I think it is?”

“Yes.”

“Can we use it?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want to?”

She looked through the newsroom window. It had begun to rain: not the blinding white rain of the coast in March, but a thin uncertain drizzle, silver under the streetlights, gone almost before it touched the ground.

“I want the filters delivered,” she said.

Mateus picked up his phone. “Then let’s be annoying.”

The next morning, O Farol published a short piece: “Replacement Water Filters Sit Undelivered in Sobral Warehouse Amid Bureaucratic Dispute.” It named no source. By noon, the health secretary announced immediate transport. By evening, trucks were on the road, filmed this time not by campaign photographers but by residents with cheap phones.

Marlene sent a video two days later. In it, men unloaded boxes beside the school while children shouted advice no one needed. Rosângela stood with her arms crossed, watching a technician install a ceramic filter into a new container. The girl with braids waved at the camera and pointed to something off-screen.

Then Marlene turned the phone.

Beside the kitchen wall of Rosângela’s house, where the old barrel had stood, someone had planted a small sapling inside a ring of stones. Its leaves were few and dusty. A goat nosed toward it, and one of the boys chased the animal away with great seriousness.

Marlene’s voice came through, amused and tired.

“Tell Fortaleza,” she said, “that if they send water, we will handle the tree.”

Isadora played the video once, then again. She did not forward it to Célia. She did not delete the letter.

Instead, she folded it into the back of her notebook, behind pages filled with names, dates, symptoms, signatures, and questions that had led where they led. Outside, the rain stopped. The streets shone briefly, reflecting the city lights in broken gold, before the heat lifted the water away.
