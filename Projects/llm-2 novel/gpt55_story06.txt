The house had learned to breathe without her mother.

When Kang Hye-jin slid open the warped blue gate, the old place exhaled the smell of dried kelp, mildew, ash, and the medicinal sweetness of pears left too long in a box. The wind came off the harbor and moved through the persimmon tree, rattling the bare twigs against the second-floor window. A gull screamed somewhere beyond the alley. From the direction of the fish market came the hollow thump of plastic tubs being stacked for morning auction.

“Still standing,” her uncle said behind her, as if the house had been expected to collapse out of spite.

Kang Jong-su carried one cardboard box under his arm—the box of documents from the funeral home, the death certificate tucked inside a folder with gold letters. He was her mother’s younger brother, though he looked older than his sixty-one years, with a fisherman’s sun-browned skin and a lower lip stained by tobacco. His black funeral armband had been folded away the moment the last mourner left.

Hye-jin had not yet removed hers.

“It’s damp,” Uncle Jong-su said. “You should sleep at my place.”

“I’m fine.”

“There may be rats.”

“I grew up here.”

“That was before the ceiling started sagging.”

He pushed past her into the courtyard, stepping around the cracked onggi jars lined against the wall. Most were empty now. In Hye-jin’s childhood, they had held soy sauce, fermented bean paste, red pepper paste, brined anchovies, all of them labeled in her mother’s square handwriting. Kang Sun-ok had believed anything edible could be made stronger by time, salt, and darkness.

Now the jars held rainwater and leaves.

Uncle Jong-su unlocked the front door with her mother’s key, although the house now belonged to Hye-jin. The door stuck at first, then scraped open. Dust shifted in the entryway.

Her mother’s shoes were still lined up on the cement floor: black rubber slippers, flowered market shoes, one pair of fake leather sandals with the straps rubbed white. Hye-jin stared at them too long.

Uncle Jong-su cleared his throat.

“About the house,” he said.

“We buried her this morning.”

“I know that. But people talk fast. The pension man asked me again after the service. He can buy this and the Kim house together. Tear down both, build something nice for Seoul people.”

“Something nice.”

“Clean. Profitable.” He set the box on the low dining table. “You live in Seoul. You won’t come back here to patch the roof every rainy season.”

Hye-jin took off her shoes and stepped into the main room. The ondol floor was cold. Her mother had kept the heat low even when she was ill, if she had been ill for as long as everyone now claimed. Pancreatic cancer, the doctor said. Quiet until it wasn’t. By the time Sun-ok went to the clinic, there had been nothing to do but make her comfortable, though Hye-jin did not know who had made her comfortable. Not her daughter, who had been in a hotel conference room near Gangnam Station, showing a cosmetics company three concepts for rebranding their snail-mucin cream.

Her mother had called twice that week. Hye-jin had sent both calls to voicemail and then forgotten to listen.

“I’ll sort things first,” Hye-jin said.

“Don’t sort too much. Throw away. Old people keep trash.”

She glanced at him.

He raised both hands. “Not trash, then. Memories. But memories have no resale value.”

Her mother’s portrait from the funeral leaned against the wall near the television, waiting for the forty-nine-day rites Hye-jin had not decided how to observe. In the photograph, Sun-ok wore a dark green blouse and a skeptical expression, as if the photographer had tried to make her smile and failed. Her hair was permed tight against her head. Her mouth was thin and familiar.

Hye-jin picked up the portrait and set it on the cabinet, higher than her uncle’s knees.

“I’ll call you tomorrow,” she said.

Uncle Jong-su looked as if he might argue, but then the house gave a long wooden groan in the wind, and he shrugged.

“Lock the gate. And don’t open if anyone strange comes by. Your mother had all kinds of people around near the end.”

“What people?”

“People.” He slipped his feet back into his shoes. “Women from the factories, workers from the abalone farms. Foreigners. I told her not to get involved in other people’s messes.”

Hye-jin waited.

He scratched his cheek. “If someone comes asking, send them to the police. You hear me?”

The word police hung in the cold entryway between them.

After he left, she bolted the gate and stood in the courtyard while his footsteps retreated down the alley. A scooter whined past. Somewhere a woman laughed sharply, then coughed. The village, which had seemed impossibly small when Hye-jin fled it at nineteen, had become smaller still, shrinking around the harbor like a fist.

She carried her suitcase upstairs to her old room.

Her mother had not changed it, which annoyed her more than if she had emptied it. The same yellowing wallpaper with tiny blue flowers. The same desk with a burn mark from when Hye-jin had secretly lit incense there at sixteen, trying to disguise cigarette smoke. The same narrow bed, neatly made with a thick winter blanket. On the shelf, her high school textbooks stood in a row, their spines faded: Korean Literature, Mathematics II, English Listening Practice. A sticker of H.O.T. peeled from the wardrobe mirror in a curl.

Only one thing was different. Someone had been sleeping there.

The blanket was tucked too smoothly to prove it, but the pillow held a shallow hollow, and on the floor beside the bed was a hair tie, black elastic with two tiny pink beads. Hye-jin picked it up. It was child-sized.

She stood very still.

Downstairs, the house creaked again.

“Hello?” she called.

No answer.

She told herself it belonged to a neighbor’s child. During the funeral days, people had come and gone carrying food, envelopes of condolence money, plastic bags of fruit. Someone’s daughter might have slept upstairs while the adults played cards and drank soju in low voices. Someone had forgotten the hair tie. That was all.

She put it on the desk and opened her suitcase.

That night she ate instant ramyeon from a pot because she could not bear to search the refrigerator. The gas flame fluttered blue beneath the dented aluminum. She sat at the kitchen table, where her mother had once sliced radish into perfect cubes for kimchi, and listened to the harbor buoys knocking in the distance.

Her phone buzzed with messages from Seoul.

Are you okay?

Call if you need anything.

Don’t rush back.

The last was from her team leader, which meant rush back.

Hye-jin set the phone face down.

The kitchen shelves were crowded with chipped bowls, jars of sesame oil, plastic containers cloudy with age. On the wall hung her mother’s calendar. The date of her death was not marked. Instead, three days before it, Sun-ok had written in red marker: pay oil man. Two days after: soak beans. On the final week: HJ? followed by a question mark.

Hye-jin touched the square with her fingertip.

Above her, from the ceiling, came a soft thump.

She looked up.

A rat, she thought. Uncle was right.

Then came another sound: a held breath released too quickly.

Hye-jin stood so fast her chair scraped the floor.

“Who’s there?”

Silence.

Her mother’s house was built oddly, expanded in pieces as money allowed. The back room opened to a pantry; the pantry had a ladder to the attic crawlspace, where Sun-ok used to dry peppers and hang seaweed from rope lines. As a child, Hye-jin had been afraid of that attic. It smelled of dust and the sea, and the roof beams looked like ribs.

She took the largest kitchen knife from the wooden block.

“Come out,” she said, hating the tremor in her voice. “I’m calling the police.”

Something shifted above the pantry.

Then a child began to cry.

The sound was small, muffled, immediately hushed.

Hye-jin lowered the knife a little.

In the pantry, sacks of rice leaned against the wall. Bundles of dried anchovies hung from hooks, silver eyes glinting in the dim light. The attic ladder was folded up against the ceiling. Hye-jin reached for the cord and pulled. Dust fell into her hair as the ladder unfolded with a wooden clatter.

“I know you’re there,” she said.

For several seconds nothing happened.

Then a woman’s face appeared in the square darkness above.

She was young, perhaps thirty, with wide cheekbones and hair cut bluntly at her jaw. Her skin had the grayish pallor of someone who had not been outside in days. One hand gripped the edge of the opening. The other held a little girl pressed against her side.

“Please,” the woman whispered in Korean. “No police.”

Hye-jin stared at her.

The child’s eyes were round and wet. She wore a padded pink jacket indoors, zipped to her chin. The black hair tie from upstairs held one of her pigtails; the other pigtail had come loose.

“Who are you?” Hye-jin asked.

The woman swallowed. “Lan. Nguyen Thi Lan.”

The name came out practiced but frightened.

Hye-jin kept the knife visible. “Why are you in my mother’s house?”

“Sun-ok eomeoni say stay.”

“My mother is dead.”

Lan flinched, not from surprise. From hearing it said again.

“I know,” she whispered.

The little girl began to cry harder.

Lan murmured to her in Vietnamese, pressing her lips to the child’s hair.

Hye-jin stood at the foot of the ladder, barefoot on the cold pantry floor, holding the knife as if she knew what to do with it. Her first clear thought was absurd: Uncle Jong-su would say I told you.

Her second was: How long have they been here?

“Come down,” she said. “Slowly.”

Lan hesitated.

“I’m not going to hurt you. Just come down.”

The attic ladder creaked beneath their weight. Lan descended first, moving stiffly, then lifted the child down. The little girl hid behind her mother’s leg. She looked about five or six. Her cheeks were chapped, and she clutched a stuffed rabbit so worn its face had disappeared.

Under the pantry bulb, Lan appeared thinner than Hye-jin had first thought. Her sweater hung off her shoulders. Her socks did not match.

“How long?” Hye-jin asked.

Lan looked at the child, then back at Hye-jin. “Here? Two months. Before, other place.”

“Two months?”

“Sun-ok eomeoni help.”

“Don’t call her that.”

Lan fell silent.

The child sniffed.

Hye-jin put the knife on a shelf, blade facing away. “Does anyone else know you’re here?”

Lan shook her head too quickly.

“My uncle?”

“No. Your mother say not tell him.”

Of course she did, Hye-jin thought.

“What are you running from?”

Lan looked toward the gate as if someone might be listening from the alley. “Boss. Bae sajang. Abalone farm. He keep passport. Say debt. Say if I go, immigration catch me. Mai sick. I ask money. He hit.” She touched her ribs, then dropped her hand.

The child—Mai—leaned into her.

Hye-jin felt the beginning of a headache at the base of her skull. She had known, in the abstract way city people know things, that migrant workers lived in coastal towns and farms. She had seen them at bus terminals, in convenience stores, in the background of news reports about accidents. They had not been in her mother’s attic.

“Where is your passport now?” she asked.

Lan’s eyes flicked to the kitchen.

“My mother has it?”

Lan nodded.

Hye-jin laughed once, without humor. “Why would she—”

Lan pulled something from her sweater pocket: a folded piece of paper, soft from being handled. She offered it with both hands.

Hye-jin unfolded it.

Her mother’s handwriting filled half the page, black pen, firm strokes.

Hye-jin-ah,

If I’m not here and Lan shows you this, don’t shout. She understands more Korean than she speaks, and the child understands everything. There is rice in the blue bin and medicine in the second drawer. Passport is not where anyone stupid would look. Call the number written inside the lid of the bean paste jar, the small one with the crack.

Don’t call your uncle. Don’t call Bae. Don’t call the police unless Maria tells you.

You were always good at finding things.

The last line struck Hye-jin harder than the rest.

She read it again. Not: I’m sorry. Not: I should have told you I was dying. Not even: I love you.

You were always good at finding things.

Anger moved through her so cleanly that for a moment it felt like relief.

“Fine,” she said.

Lan watched her face.

“You and Mai can sleep downstairs tonight,” Hye-jin said. “Not in the attic. It’s freezing up there.”

Lan’s shoulders sagged, but she did not smile.

“I need to think,” Hye-jin added. “Tomorrow we call this Maria.”

At the name, Lan nodded. “Maria seonsaengnim. Mokpo.”

Hye-jin found extra blankets in the wardrobe, though they smelled of camphor. She warmed rice and doenjang soup from containers in the refrigerator, food left by neighbors for mourners. Mai ate with desperate concentration, both hands around the spoon. Lan tried to slow her with murmurs.

Watching them, Hye-jin remembered her mother pushing bowls toward her without comment whenever she came home from school. Eat. As if hunger were the only ache a person could admit to having.

She slept badly.

Dreams broke apart before morning: her mother in the attic, her mother at the harbor, her mother standing behind the funeral portrait tapping the glass with one fingernail. When Hye-jin woke, gray light filled her old room. For a few seconds she believed she was sixteen again and late for school.

Then she heard Mai laughing downstairs.

It was a quick burst, surprised out of her. Hye-jin sat up.

In the kitchen, Lan was washing rice at the sink while Mai sat at the table drawing on the back of an old utility bill. The child had used a red marker to make a house with an enormous roof. Beside it she drew three stick figures. One had curly hair like Sun-ok’s perm.

Lan turned quickly. “Sorry. She found pen.”

“It’s fine.”

Hye-jin opened the refrigerator at last.

Inside were the usual things: kimchi in plastic tubs, half a radish wrapped in newspaper, soybean sprouts wilting in a bag, anchovy broth in a pitcher. The freezer held mackerel, dumplings, small packets of rice cakes. Ordinary. Her mother had hidden people in the house but kept her refrigerator ordinary.

“Did my mother say where exactly your passport was?”

Lan shook her head. “She say, if something happen, Hye-jin find.”

Hye-jin closed the refrigerator harder than necessary.

She searched the obvious places first. Desk drawers. The cabinet beneath the television. The tin box where Sun-ok kept bankbooks and family seals. There were receipts tied with rubber bands, old insurance documents, temple donation slips, photographs in envelopes.

In one photograph, Hye-jin stood at seven years old on the beach, furious in a red swimsuit, because her mother had insisted on cutting her bangs too short. Sun-ok stood behind her, squinting against the sun, one hand on Hye-jin’s shoulder. There were no photographs from after Hye-jin left for university, except one newspaper clipping about her advertising firm’s award, folded carefully along the edges.

She set it aside without knowing why.

By midmorning, Uncle Jong-su arrived with a real estate agent.

Hye-jin saw them through the front window: her uncle in his padded vest, the agent in polished shoes unsuitable for fish-market alleys. Behind them came a third man Hye-jin did not know. He was stocky, with a broad face and hair slicked flat. He wore a black windbreaker zipped to his throat.

Lan, who had been wiping the table, went still.

“Bae,” she breathed.

Hye-jin’s body reacted before her mind did. She pushed Lan toward the pantry.

“Take Mai. Quiet.”

Lan grabbed the child and vanished behind the pantry door. Hye-jin stepped into the entryway just as her uncle knocked.

“You don’t need to knock,” she said, opening the door only wide enough to fill the space. “You have keys.”

Uncle Jong-su frowned. “Why is the gate locked?”

“Because you told me to lock it.”

The real estate agent smiled too brightly. “Ms. Kang? I’m Oh Min-cheol. Condolences. Your uncle said we could take quick measurements.”

“You can’t.”

The agent’s smile weakened.

Uncle Jong-su sighed. “Hye-jin-ah, don’t be difficult.”

“My mother died four days ago.”

“And the market won’t wait forty-nine days out of respect.”

The stocky man looked past her into the house. “Sun-ok ajumma isn’t here, then?”

Hye-jin met his eyes. “No. She’s dead.”

“I heard. A shame.” He did not sound ashamed. “She had something of mine.”

“What?”

“A worker. Vietnamese woman. Ran off with company property.”

Behind the pantry door, there was no sound at all.

“I don’t know anything about that,” Hye-jin said.

The man smiled with one side of his mouth. “Maybe she didn’t tell you. Old women get sentimental. They don’t understand contracts.”

“What company property did the worker take?”

His eyes cooled. “Passport. Advance money. Clothes.”

“Clothes belong to your company?”

Uncle Jong-su made a warning noise. “Hye-jin.”

Bae leaned closer. His breath smelled of canned coffee. “If she comes here, call me. If immigration gets involved, this house gets involved too. You understand?”

Hye-jin had spent years in meeting rooms with men who mistook softness for permission. She gave him the expression she used when clients asked whether she could make the design “more feminine but less emotional.”

“No,” she said. “Explain it.”

Bae stared at her.

The real estate agent coughed.

Uncle Jong-su grabbed Bae’s sleeve. “Let’s go. She’s tired.”

“We’ll come back tomorrow,” the agent said, still trying to smile. “Just to look.”

“No,” Hye-jin said, and shut the door.

Her hands were shaking.

From the pantry came Mai’s thin whisper. Lan shushed her.

Hye-jin waited until the men’s voices faded down the alley before opening the pantry door. Lan had one hand clamped over her own mouth, as if stopping herself from apologizing.

“You can’t stay here,” Hye-jin said.

Lan nodded.

“I mean it. He knows to look here.”

“I know.”

“Where is this number?”

Lan pointed toward the courtyard. “Bean paste jar.”

There were six smaller jars by the wall. Hye-jin crouched among them, the cold seeping through her jeans. Two were empty, one held rainwater, one held a nest of dead leaves. The fifth contained actual doenjang, dark and pungent, covered by a circle of plastic wrap beneath the lid. The sixth had a crack down the side repaired with wire.

Inside its lid, written in black marker, was a phone number.

Hye-jin dialed from her own phone. A woman answered on the fourth ring.

“Yeoboseyo?”

“My name is Kang Hye-jin. I’m Kang Sun-ok’s daughter.”

There was a pause.

Then the woman said softly, “Ah.”

That single sound held more knowledge of Sun-ok’s recent life than Hye-jin possessed.

“Lan and her daughter are here,” Hye-jin said. “A man named Bae came looking.”

“Are they safe right now?”

“Yes.”

“Do you have her passport?”

“Not yet. My mother hid it.”

A small exhale, almost a laugh. “She would.”

“You’re Maria?”

“Sister Maria, yes. In Mokpo. Your mother helped several women reach us. Lan was supposed to come last month, but the child had a fever, and then your mother…” She stopped. “I’m sorry for your loss.”

The phrase passed over Hye-jin like rain over plastic.

“I need to get them to you,” Hye-jin said.

“Yes. But not by bus if Bae is watching the terminal. Can you drive?”

“My mother’s truck is here.”

“Bring them after dark. Call me when you leave. And Ms. Kang?”

“Yes?”

“Your mother trusted you.”

Hye-jin looked at the cracked bean paste jar, at the black numbers inside the lid. “She shouldn’t have.”

Sister Maria did not answer immediately. “Maybe she knew that too.”

After the call, Hye-jin spent the afternoon taking the house apart.

She searched with a fury that made Lan keep Mai in the main room, drawing house after house on funeral envelopes. Hye-jin emptied drawers, shook out blankets, lifted floor mats. She found 340,000 won in cash inside a flour tin; three gold rings wrapped in tissue; her father’s old army watch; batteries; a dead mobile phone; and a stack of letters addressed in Vietnamese, Korean, and shaky English. None had stamps.

In her mother’s bedroom, beneath the winter clothes, she found a plastic storage box sealed with tape.

Inside were documents arranged in clear folders. Copies of passports. Hospital receipts. Wage slips. Photographs.

Women stood in the courtyard beside Sun-ok’s onggi jars, smiling shyly, holding children, wearing borrowed jackets. A young man with a bandaged hand sat at the kitchen table eating noodles. Two women posed with Sun-ok at the bus terminal, one making a peace sign, Sun-ok looking annoyed by the camera but not moving away.

Names were written on the backs.

Asha. Cambodia. Left for Ansan, 2021.

Mina. Philippines. Baby born healthy, 3.2 kg.

Tran Quoc Huy. Hand healed. Sent home with wages.

Lan + Mai. Wait for Maria.

Hye-jin spread the photographs across the floor.

All those years, when she had imagined her mother alone in the house, becoming smaller and more bitter, Sun-ok had been opening the gate to strangers. Cooking rice. Hiding passports. Writing numbers inside jar lids. Calling someone’s child healthy.

Hye-jin pressed the heel of her hand to her eye until sparks appeared.

Lan stood in the doorway. “Sun-ok eomeoni say you work with pictures.”

“Advertising.”

“Advertising,” Lan repeated carefully. “She show me newspaper.”

Hye-jin looked up.

Lan pointed to the clipping on the floor. “She say, my daughter make ugly bottle beautiful so people pay too much.”

Despite herself, Hye-jin laughed.

It came out cracked. Lan smiled a little, uncertain.

“She said that?”

“Yes. She proud.”

“She never told me.”

Lan’s smile faded.

Hye-jin gathered the photographs into a stack. “She didn’t tell me anything.”

Lan sat on the edge of the floor, leaving space between them. “My mother also not tell. When I leave Vietnam, she angry. Say, go then. Later my aunt tell me she borrow money for bus to airport, just to see from far. I didn’t know.”

Hye-jin did not want comfort from this woman. She wanted to be alone with her anger, to hold it up and find it solid. But the house was full of other people’s proof.

Mai came in carrying the stuffed rabbit by one ear. She looked at a photograph of herself in the courtyard and touched it with one finger.

“Halmeoni,” she said.

Grandmother.

Hye-jin looked away.

The passport was in the kimchi refrigerator.

Not inside any container, not under the lid, but taped to the underside of the machine itself. Hye-jin found it only after remembering her mother once hiding money from her father there, years before he drank himself into debt and then into the sea—at least, that was how the village told it. Sun-ok had waited until he passed out, then taped the cash beneath the humming white box because, she said, men never looked below what fed them.

Hye-jin lay flat on the kitchen floor with a flashlight between her teeth and peeled away the tape. A plastic sleeve dropped into her hand. Inside were two passports, a folded birth certificate, and a bank envelope containing unfamiliar won notes and U.S. dollars.

Lan knelt and began to cry without sound.

Mai tried to wipe her mother’s cheeks with the rabbit.

They left after nine.

The village lanes were dark except for blue television light flickering behind curtains. Hye-jin loaded one small bag into the back of her mother’s pickup truck, along with a box of food Lan tried to refuse. The truck smelled of gasoline, dried squid, and her mother’s peppermint candies. The steering wheel cover was cracked.

As Hye-jin opened the passenger door, the gate across the alley creaked.

Mrs. Kim from next door stood in her slippers, cardigan clutched at her chest. She had brought soup to the funeral and told everyone Sun-ok had looked peaceful, though Hye-jin knew the dead never looked peaceful, only absent.

Mrs. Kim looked at Lan. At Mai. At the bag.

For one second, nobody moved.

Then the old woman said, “Road by the school has police tonight. Drunk-driving check.”

Hye-jin’s throat tightened.

Mrs. Kim pointed with her chin. “Take the sea wall road. Slower, but empty.”

“Thank you,” Hye-jin said.

Mrs. Kim sniffed. “Your mother still owes me two jars of anchovy sauce.”

“I’ll look for them.”

“They’re under the stairs.” She stepped back into her house and closed the door.

Lan sat in the back seat with Mai asleep across her lap. Hye-jin drove without turning on the radio. The road curved along the black water. Fishing boats rocked in the harbor, their work lights strung like low stars. The island receded behind them, then returned in the mirror, a dark hump against the sky.

At the checkpoint near the bridge—because of course there was a checkpoint, sea wall road or not—a young policeman waved his glowing baton. Hye-jin lowered the window. Cold air rushed in.

“Good evening. License.”

She handed it over.

He shone his flashlight inside the cab. Lan bowed her head over Mai’s sleeping face. Hye-jin felt her pulse in her gums.

“Where are you headed?” the policeman asked.

“Mokpo,” Hye-jin said. “My daughter has a fever.”

The lie came easily. Too easily.

The flashlight moved to Mai’s flushed cheeks, her open mouth. Perhaps she did look feverish. Perhaps all sleeping children looked like evidence.

“At this hour?”

“My mother died,” Hye-jin said.

The policeman looked back at her.

She did not have to make her voice shake.

“Funeral today. I forgot her medicine at my aunt’s. We’re going there now.”

He returned the license quickly. “Drive carefully.”

“Yes.”

As she pulled away, Lan let out a breath that became a sob. Hye-jin kept both hands on the wheel.

In Mokpo, Sister Maria met them behind a small brick building with a blue cross over the door. She was not what Hye-jin expected. No habit. Just a padded coat over gray sweatpants, short hair, glasses fogging in the cold. She hugged Lan first, then crouched to look at Mai.

“You’ve gotten tall,” she said in Korean.

Mai hid her face.

Sister Maria turned to Hye-jin. “Come in for tea.”

“I should get back.”

“It’s late.”

“I have to clean the house.”

The older woman studied her. “Of course.”

Lan stood with the bag at her feet, passport clutched under her coat. For a moment she seemed unable to decide whether touching Hye-jin would be allowed. Then she bowed deeply.

“Thank you,” she said.

Hye-jin shook her head. “It was my mother.”

Lan straightened. “Your mother wait for you.”

That sentence was worse than an accusation.

Hye-jin crouched in front of Mai. “Keep your rabbit safe.”

Mai looked at her solemnly. Then she held out the red marker she had used all day to draw houses.

“For you,” she said.

Hye-jin took it.

The drive back was longer because she stopped once at a rest area and cried in the truck with both hands over her face. No one knocked on the window. Trucks hissed by on the wet road. From the glove compartment, when she reached for tissues, she found a roll of peppermint candies and an old cassette tape labeled Hye-jin—middle school speech contest. Her mother must have kept it there for years, rattling among toll receipts.

Dawn was paling the harbor when she returned.

Uncle Jong-su stood at the gate.

He wore the same padded vest, hair flattened on one side from sleep. Beside him was Bae’s dark car, empty.

Hye-jin parked the truck and got out.

Her uncle looked at the back seat, then at her. “Where did you take them?”

“Mokpo.”

He swore under his breath. “Are you insane?”

“Probably.”

“If Bae makes trouble—”

“He kept their passports.”

“You think people care? He knows people. He’ll say your mother stole from him. He’ll say you helped.”

“I did help.”

Uncle Jong-su stared at her as if seeing an unfamiliar relative at a wedding. “Your mother caused enough trouble alive. Don’t inherit that too.”

The morning cold moved between them.

Hye-jin said, “Did you know?”

He looked away.

“How much?”

“I knew she let women sleep here sometimes. I told her to stop. She was sick, Hye-jin-ah. She had no money. She wouldn’t listen.”

“She had cancer.”

“I told her to call you.”

Hye-jin flinched.

His face softened briefly, which was worse. “She said you were busy.”

The gulls began their morning racket over the harbor.

Uncle Jong-su rubbed his forehead. “Sell the house. Pay what needs paying. Go back to Seoul. That is the clean way.”

Hye-jin opened the gate. “I’m not ready.”

He gave a short, bitter laugh. “Ready? No one is ready.”

“No,” she said. “I mean the house isn’t clean yet.”

She went inside and shut the gate between them.

For three days, she cleaned.

Not the way her uncle meant. She did throw things away: expired medicine, cracked plastic basins, newspapers swollen with damp. But she also made piles. Things to send. Things to ask about. Things to keep until someone called.

Mrs. Kim came by for the anchovy sauce and stayed to help wash blankets. She did not ask where Lan had gone. She only said, while wringing water from a towel, “Your mother made terrible coffee,” and laughed so hard she had to sit down.

On the second day, Hye-jin found the burner phone in a tin of barley tea bags. Its battery was dead. When she charged it, messages arrived in bursts, some months old, some recent.

Eomeoni, arrived safe.

Can I give your number to my cousin?

Thank you for medicine.

Are you there?

Sun-ok auntie?

The last few had come after her death.

Hye-jin sat at the kitchen table with the phone in front of her and her mother’s portrait propped on the cabinet. The house was quiet except for the refrigerator’s hum and the wind pressing at the windows. She wanted to ask the photograph what she was supposed to do now. The photograph offered nothing. Sun-ok’s eyes remained skeptical.

So Hye-jin opened a notebook and began copying names.

That evening she cooked rice, though only for herself. She made seaweed soup from the dried miyeok hanging in the pantry and added too much garlic, the way her mother disliked. She ate at the low table with the red marker beside her bowl.

Afterward, she took one of Mai’s drawings from the stack left behind on utility bills. The house with the enormous roof. Three figures: a woman, a child, and an old woman with curly hair.

Hye-jin taped it to the refrigerator.

Her phone buzzed. A message from her team leader: We need your return date.

She looked around the kitchen—the chipped bowls, the drying rack, the jar lids, the calendar still open to the month her mother had died.

Then she picked up the burner phone.

For a while she only listened to its faint electronic hiss. Finally, she recorded a new voicemail greeting, speaking slowly, her Seoul accent sounding strange in the old coastal kitchen.

“This is Kang Sun-ok’s phone,” she said. “This is her daughter, Kang Hye-jin. If you knew my mother, leave a message. If you need the room, don’t come without calling first.”

She stopped there.

Outside, the tide was going out, dragging pebbles down the shore with a sound like many small things being sorted in the dark.
