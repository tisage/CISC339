The plant breathed at night.

That was when you could hear it best, when the town had put out its lights and the mill had stopped and the last of the canoes had come in off the river, and there was nothing left in the world but the water going through the wheel and the wheel going round and the armature turning inside its iron collar, and the whole whitewashed building humming like something alive. Kwabena Aboagye stood in the doorway of the turbine hall at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday night and listened, and the plant told him what he needed to know.

The river came down from the north, from the hills and the ruins of the big dam at Kpong, and at Adakope it narrowed between two banks of black stone and fell four meters through the trench that the old engineers had blasted out of the rock in the time of wires. There had been a weir once, a proper one, with gates and a fish ladder and a house for the operator with glass in the windows. Now the weir was a rubble spine across the water, patched with sandbags and palm trunks and old car doors, and the house had lost its roof and grown a flame tree out of the middle of what used to be the kitchen. But the trench still ran, and the wheel still turned, and the generator still made its sixty kilowatts when the river was high and its thirty-five when the river was low, and that was enough. That was the whole world.

Kwabena walked the length of the turbine hall. His knees hurt, as they did every night, but he had learned long ago to walk the pain out; the first twenty steps were the worst and then the body gave up complaining. He put his hand on the bearing housing, the big one on the river side, and felt the warmth come through the iron into his palm. Warm was good. Warm meant the oil was doing its work. Hot was bad, and cold was worse, because cold meant the oil had stopped moving and the metal was grinding itself to death and you had maybe an hour, maybe less, before the shaft seized and the wheel stopped and the lights went out in the town and the cold boxes at the clinic began to sweat and the pump died and the people drank from the river and got the sickness that came with the river.

Warm was good. The bearing housing was warm.

He took the long-spouted oil can from its hook and gave the forward greaser three drops, then the aft greaser three drops, counting them the way his father had taught him, the way Awurade Yaw had taught his father, six drops and no more, because a greaser that was overfilled blew out its seals and a greaser that was underfilled ran dry, and there was no seal kit in the world anymore, no New Holland, no Bearing and Seal Supply Limited, Accra Industrial. There was what was in the store room and what could be made from other things and what could be taken off the dead machines of the dead world, and no more.

He wrote the temperature in the ledger, and the time, and the river gauge, and the load. Sixty-one kilowatts, the gauge glass trembling just a little above the mark. The town was drawing sixty-one kilowatts on a Tuesday night, which meant the clinic's cold chain was cycling, and the mill's second motor was on for the evening shift, and the pump at the standpipe was filling the tank on the hill, and every third house had its one bulb burning. Naa Dedei's rules, agreed at council, carved into a board that hung in the market shed: one bulb per house, four hours, from seven to eleven. The clinic, the pump, the mill, and the radio at the police post were exempt, and the radio had been dead for two years, but nobody had told the rules yet.

Kwabena stood at the switchboard and looked at the gauges and let the sound of the machine come into him. That was when he heard it.

A thin note. High, thin, almost lost under the bass of the wheel and the hum of the generator. Like a mosquito in another room, or a wire drawn tight. He stood still and held his breath. The note was there. It was there when the load was high. It was not there when the load dropped, when the mill's second motor shut off and the gauge fell to fifty, and it came back when the pump started again.

He went down the iron stairs to the turbine floor and put his ear against the bearing housing, and the note was in the iron, and the iron was warmer than it should have been. He put his hand on it and held it there for a long count, and then he took his hand away and looked at his palm, and there was a grey shine on his skin. Metal. Fine metal, the ghost of metal, ground so fine you could not see the grains until they lay in the oil on your hand.

He stood there a long moment in the dark of the turbine hall, with the wheel turning beside him and the note singing in the iron, and he did the arithmetic that he had been putting off for a year and a half, since the day he had opened the spare bearing crate and found it half full of mouse nests and rust and a length of chain that someone had put there instead of a bearing, God knew when, God knew why.

There was no spare. The river was falling. The note was new.

He climbed the stairs and wrote in the ledger: *Aft bearing singing. Metal in oil. Running hot under load.*

Then he closed the book and hung the oil can on its hook and went out into the night to tell the town, or the part of the town that mattered, that the heart of the world had begun to wear out.

---

Ama Aboagye had known her father was coming before she heard his footsteps on the path, because the lamp on her clinic desk dimmed and steadied and dimmed again, and that was the way the light behaved when the load on the plant changed, when someone threw a switch down at the station. She was writing up the day's notes by that lamp — the fisherman with the infected cut, the child with the fever, the old woman with the cough that would not go — and she did not look up when the door opened. She said, “You have changed the load.”

“The load changed itself,” Kwabena said. He came in and stood by the door with his cap in his hands. Her father never sat down in the clinic, not once in all the years, as if he believed that sitting in a place where people died would make him a patient there.

“You look like a man who has been carrying something heavy.”

“I have a bearing that is singing,” he said. “Aft main bearing. The one without a spare.”

Ama put down the pen. She was thirty-one years old and she had her mother's face, round and calm and impossible to alarm, and she had her father's hands, which was a thing she knew and did not often think about. She looked at the lamp on the desk and then at the lamp in the ward beyond the open door, where three beds held three patients and one of them was Yaa Nyarko, who was eight months gone with her second child and had been told to rest until the baby turned, and who had not rested once. “How long.”

“If I nurse it, maybe three weeks. If the river falls the way it is falling, less.”

“And if you can't nurse it?”

“Then the wheel stops,” her father said. “And everything stops.”

She got up and went to the door of the ward and looked at Yaa, who was asleep, or pretending to sleep, her hands folded on the mound of her belly. The baby was lying transverse; Ama had felt it with her hands that morning, the hard curve of the head where the head should not be, and she had thought about the old hospital in Ada with its theatre and its lights, and the way the road to Ada had been under water since the third year of the quiet, when the sea had come up over the spit and taken the causeway and not given it back.

“There is a baby coming,” Ama said. “A hard one. She will need light, and suction, and the cold boxes for the plasma, and maybe the pump if I have to do a section, and I cannot do a section in the dark.”

“I know,” said Kwabena.

“So find a bearing.”

“There is no bearing on this earth,” he said, “except the one that is singing in my machine. Unless there is one up the river.”

“Up the river.”

“At Kpong. The old pump station, the one above the ferry. There were four units there. They were bigger than ours, but a bearing is a bearing. The metal can be re-poured. The shell can be re-machined. My father did it in the bad year, before you were born. He cut a bearing out of a pump that had stood in water for twenty years and he poured it again in the workshop and we ran on it for nine years.”

Ama looked at her father. He was sixty-three years old, and his knees were bad, and the journey up the river was two days there and two days back if the water was kind, and it was not a kind river anymore. There were sandbars that shifted in the night and men at the old salt works who took what they wanted and asked nothing, and there was the ferry crossing where the bridge had fallen and the wreck sat in the channel like a sleeping animal, and there were dogs in the ruins of Kpong, wild dogs, and people said other things too.

“You are not going alone,” she said.

“I am going with Efua.”

“Efua is nineteen.”

“Efua is the only person in this town besides me who knows the difference between a bearing and a bushing,” Kwabena said, “and if I don't come back, someone has to know it after me.” He said it flatly, the way he said everything, and Ama heard the other sentence underneath it, the one he did not say: *and if I don't come back she will be the one who keeps the light on for you and your baby, and she will be alone, and she had better have learned everything I can teach her before I go.*

“Sit down, Papa.”

“I don't sit in the clinic.”

“Then stand,” she said, and went to the shelf and took down the tin of biscuits that she kept for patients who had come a long way, and gave him two, and poured him a cup of the millet water that stood in the clay pot by the window. He ate standing up, the way he did everything, and when he had finished he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and said, “The council meets in the morning. Naa Dedei will want to hear it from you as well as me. She trusts you more than she trusts me.”

“She trusts me because I don't want her chair.”

“Then come to the council and be trusted,” said Kwabena, and went out into the dark, and Ama stood in the doorway and watched the lamp on the path go down the hill toward the station, and the light in the clinic dimmed and steadied, dimmed and steadied, as her father walked away.

---

The council met under the mango tree in the market shed, because the shed had a roof and the roof had a radio aerial on it that no longer connected to anything, and because Naa Dedei said that any meeting held indoors was a meeting held in the dark, and they had enough dark.

There were nine of them: Naa Dedei, who was the chair and had been a trader in cloth and salt and now traded in everything; Kojo Mensah the fisherman, whose wife Yaa lay in the clinic; the imam from the river mosque and the catechist from the mission, who between them covered most of the town's praying; old Maame Efua Asantewaa, who was Efua's grandmother and the oldest person in Adakope and remembered the time of wires the way other people remembered their children's names; the two farmers from the inland road; the head of the fish smokers' guild; and Kwabena, who was not elected to anything and sat at every meeting all the same, because the town had learned long ago that no decision could be made without knowing what the plant could give.

Efua sat behind her grandmother's chair on an upturned crate, with her notebook on her knees. Kwabena had told her to come. She was a small, quick young woman with a square jaw and hands that were never still, and she had come to the plant at fourteen with a note from her grandmother that said *she takes machines apart, keep her*, and she had never left. She had learned the plant the way a child learns a language — by living in it, by touching it, by being scolded. She knew the switchboard better than Kwabena did; she knew which breaker was held together with fishing line and which breaker only *looked* held together with fishing line, and she had drawn every circuit in the notebook, in pencil, with the wires colored by the stub of a red pencil that she sharpened every Sunday.

“Kwabena has something to tell us,” Naa Dedei said. “Then we will eat, and then we will decide.”

Kwabena told them. He told them about the note and the metal and the heat, and he did not soften it, because he was not a man who softened things. When he was done, the shed was quiet except for the wind in the mango leaves, and Maame Asantewaa said, “My husband poured a bearing once. In the bad year. With his own hands.”

“Yes, Maame,” said Kwabena.

“It took him nine days and he burned his arm to the bone.”

“It took him three days,” said Kwabena gently, because the old woman's memory had begun to fold in on itself, “and he burned his arm, but not to the bone, and the bearing ran for nine years.”

“Nine days,” said Maame Asantewaa, with absolute conviction, and nobody argued with her, because she was ninety-four and had earned the right to remember things wrong.

Kojo the fisherman spoke. He had a voice like the bottom of a boat. “You want to go up the river.”

“I want to go up the river.”

“To Kpong.”

“To the pump station. There's a store there, or there was. Four units. The bearings were white metal on bronze shells. Even if the machines are gone, the shells may be in the ground, in the pit, under the water line. And there is the scrap yard at the ferry, where the trucks were.”

“The ferry is where the men are,” said the fish smoker, whose name was Ataa and who had lost a nephew at the ferry in the fourth year of the quiet. “They take everything. They took the ferry chain. They took the pontoon. They took my sister's son.”

“They take what they can sell,” said Kwabena. “I am not selling anything. I am going to dig in the mud, and I am coming back with a piece of bronze, and if they stop me I will give them salt and fish hooks and the news, and if they will not be given, I will turn around and come home. I am not a young man. Nobody wants what I have except the town.”

“The town needs you here,” said the imam.

“The town needs a bearing,” said Kwabena. “The town will have me either way. But it will not have light without a bearing, and the clinic will not have cold without light, and Yaa Nyarko's baby will not have a safe road without cold and light, and I have been keeping this town alive for thirty years and I am telling you that the thing I cannot make myself is a bearing, and the thing I can fetch is a bearing, and I am going to fetch it.”

Naa Dedei let the silence sit for a moment, the way she always did, so that everyone could feel the weight of the thing they were deciding. Then she said, “How much diesel.”

“Eight cans,” said Kwabena. “Four for the outboard up and back, four for the Lister, because I will have to shut the river wheel to pour, and the Lister is the only thing that will light the workshop while I do it.”

“We have eleven cans.”

“Then we will have three when I am done, and we will have a bearing, and we will have light.”

“And if you are not back in ten days?”

Kwabena was quiet. Then he said, “Then shut the weir and drain the trench and take the wheel apart and use the bronze for something else, and tell Efua to keep the notebook, and put the baby on the next canoe to Ada if the sea is flat.” He said it the way he said everything, as if he were reading a figure off a gauge, and behind him Efua stopped writing, and her pencil sat on the page and made a dot that grew as she held it there.

Naa Dedei looked at the girl, and then at the old man, and then at Ama, who stood at the edge of the shed with her arms folded and her face like her mother's, giving nothing away. “Take the girl,” she said. “Take four cans for the outboard and four for the Lister and the last three cans stay here, and if the clinic needs the Lister while you're gone, we'll burn the last three cans and the clinic will have its light. That is the bargain. Go with God, or with whatever is listening.”

“Thank you, Naa.”

“Don't thank me. Bring back a bearing.”

---

They left at first light in the old aluminum boat that had been a river taxi before the quiet, with the outboard that Kwabena had rebuilt from three dead outboards and that started, that morning, on the fourth pull, which Efua took as an omen and Kwabena took as evidence that the carburetor was still set rich. The river was wide and brown and slow, and the banks went by with their ruins: a church with no roof and a flame tree growing out of the pulpit; a filling station whose canopy had fallen across its own pumps like a man asleep on his tools; a school where the desks still stood in rows in the roofless classroom, waiting for a class that had stopped coming in the third year.

They went up the river for two days, sleeping the first night on a sandbar with a fire of driftwood and a watch kept by turns, because Efua's grandmother had told her stories and Kwabena had seen things in his time that he did not talk about. On the second day, in the afternoon, the river narrowed and the hills came down to the water, and the current turned against them, and they went the last five kilometers with the outboard coughing and the fuel running down, past the drowned terraces of the old irrigation scheme, where the rice had gone wild and the paddy birds stood in the shallow water like sentries who had forgotten their orders, until at last they came around the last bend and saw the ferry.

The bridge had fallen in the fourth year. It lay in the water in three pieces, the two ends still anchored to their abutments and the middle a rusted cage of girders standing up out of the channel at an angle, with the wreck of a truck still caught in it, its cab pointing down into the brown water as if it were drinking. The pontoon that had once crossed beside the bridge was gone, all but a concrete ramp on the far bank and a winch house on the near one, and in the winch house and around it there were people. Six of them. Three men, two women, and a child. They had a fire going, and a line of fish drying on a rack, and a stack of things that had been salvaged and sorted and stacked: steel plate, copper wire, aluminum sheet, a tower of tires.

Kwabena cut the engine and let the boat drift in, and the eldest of the men came down to the water's edge with a cutlass hanging from his belt and stood with his arms crossed and waited.

They talked, as men talk at a river crossing, about the water and the fish and the weather. The man's name was Kossi, and he was Ewe, from the other side of the river before the bridge fell, and he had been keeping the crossing for six years, and he kept it honestly by his own account, which meant that he took a toll and did not take lives, and Kwabena, who had learned long ago not to argue with other men's accounts of themselves, said that this was a good way to live. Then he told Kossi what he wanted.

“The pump station,” he said. “The old irrigation station above the ferry. Is there anything left?”

Kossi looked at him for a long moment. Then he said, “There is iron in the mud and dogs in the yard, and everything worth carrying was carried. But you know that, old man. Why are you here?”

“I want the bearing shells,” said Kwabena. “The white metal is gone, or the white metal can be poured. I need the bronze shells.”

Kossi laughed. He had a big laugh and a gap in his front teeth, and the laugh went across the water and came back off the wreck of the bridge. “You came two days up the river for shells.”

“I came two days up the river for a heart,” said Kwabena. “Everything else in my town I can make or mend or go without. The heart I cannot make. So I came for a shell to pour a heart into.”

Kossi stopped laughing. He looked at the old man and then at the girl, who sat in the bow with her notebook on her knees and her pencil in her hand and a look on her face like someone who was memorizing everything. He said, “The station is half a kilometer up that bank. The yard is underwater at the far end and the pump house is full of mud. There are dogs. There are also two casings in the pump pit that nobody carried because nobody could lift them, and the bearing shells in those casings are as big as your head. If you can lift them, you can have them. For the toll.”

“What toll.”

Kossi looked at the boat. “The outboard,” he said. “Or the salt.”

“I have no salt.”

“Then the outboard.”

“The outboard is the way home,” said Kwabena. “If you take it, you may as well take my hands.”

They stood there, the two of them, with the brown water going by and the child on the bank watching with big eyes, and Efua watching from the boat with her pencil motionless. Then Kossi said, “There is a third thing.”

“Name it.”

“Your town has light,” said Kossi. “I have heard it. They say the town at Adakope has a wheel in the water and that the light comes on at seven and goes off at eleven, every night, and the clinic has cold, and the mill turns, and I have heard this for five years and I have never once seen it. When I was a boy, my mother took me to Ada, and there was electric, and I remember the bulb over the table, and I have thought about that bulb for forty years.” He looked at the old man. “When your wheel turns again, and the light comes on, I want my daughter to see it. That is the toll. You give me a paper with your mark, and when the light returns, my daughter comes down the river and stands under your light. That is all.”

Kwabena looked at the child on the bank, a girl of perhaps nine with her hair in two hard knots and a scab on her knee, and he said, “She will have to come before the council in Adakope and they will give her milk and a place for the night, and she will stand under the light, and if they say no, she will stand under it anyway, because I am the one who runs the wheel and I will not put it out while a child is looking at it.”

“Then it is agreed,” said Kossi, and spat in his palm, and Kwabena spat in his, and they shook, which was the custom of the ferry and not the custom of Adakope, but Kwabena had been a young man on many rivers and knew what was needed.

---

The pump house was as Kossi had said. Half of it was underwater, and the half that was not was full of river mud the color of old chocolate, and there were dogs, three of them, thin and pale as ghosts, that stood on the far bank and watched with their heads low and did not come near the fire that Efua built at the edge of the mud, because a fire is the first thing a wild dog learns to fear and the last thing it forgets.

They dug for six hours, the two of them, with a shovel and a crowbar and their hands, in the pit of the old pump house, and the mud took everything they gave it and asked for more. Twice they hit something hard and twice it was only the flange of a pipe. The third time, near the end of the afternoon, when the light was going gold and their backs had stopped speaking to them out of exhaustion, Kwabena's crowbar rang on something that rang back, and he went down on his knees in the mud and pulled, and Efua pulled with him, and they hauled a bronze shell up out of the water and laid it on the bank like a body, and it was pitted and green with verdigris and it weighed, by the feel of it, fifteen kilos, and on the curved inner face there was still, under the corrosion, the thin silver-grey smear of the white metal that had once let a shaft turn in it for thirty years.

“It's a bearing,” said Efua. Her voice had gone strange, half a laugh and half something else. She was covered in mud to her elbows and her hair had come out of its cloth and she did not care, she was standing in the sun on the bank of the river holding the edge of the bronze shell and her eyes were shining like the eyes of a person who has been told that the thing she wanted could not be had. “Kwabena. It's a bearing.”

“It's half a bearing,” he said. “Come. There was a second casing in the pit. Where there is one shell there is usually its mate, and I am not coming back here in the dry season.”

They dug again, and the light went from gold to red, and the dogs on the far bank began to move in a slow circle, and Efua stood guard with the shovel while Kwabena went down into the pit one last time, and it was nearly dark when he came up with the second shell in his arms, and sat down in the mud, and did not get up for a long time.

That night they camped on a sandbar below the ferry, and built the fire high, and ate the fish that Kossi's wife gave them — she had insisted, in the way of a woman who has decided that the transaction needed to be balanced — and Efua took out her notebook and drew the two shells in the firelight, with their dimensions in hand-spans and thumb-widths, and the thickness of the metal at the crown and at the parting line, and the way the oil grooves ran, and the way the babbit had failed on the lower half where the load had sat. Kwabena watched her draw and corrected her once, and she rubbed out the line with her thumb and drew it again, and it was right.

“Why do you write everything,” he said.

“Because you will die someday,” said Efua, without looking up from the page.

Kwabena was quiet. The fire cracked. On the far bank, the dogs had stopped circling and the ferry was dark.

“That's the right answer,” he said.

---

They were home in three days, because the river ran with them and the outboard held, and they came around the bend and saw the town on the hill and the whitewashed building at the water's edge and the smoke from the smoker's fires, and Efua stood up in the bow of the boat and did not wave, because she was not a child and because her hands were full of bronze, but the whole town knew they were coming before they landed, and there were people on the bank, and Naa Dedei was there with her arms folded, and Ama was there, and Maame Asantewaa in her chair carried down to the water like a queen.

Yaa Nyarko was still in the clinic, still pregnant, still transverse. Ama looked at her father and did not ask if he had found it, because she could see the bronze wrapped in sacking in the bottom of the boat, and because her father's face had answered the question two days before she had even formed it. She said, “She has begun to leak. I think it will be soon.”

Kwabena went to the plant, because that was where the work was, and the work was the only answer he had. He stood in the turbine hall with the sacking on the floor and looked at the aft bearing housing, and it was warm, warm, warm, and when he put his ear to it the note was there, louder than it had been, and he wrote in the ledger: *Home with two shells. Pour tomorrow.*

---

They poured at dawn, in the workshop behind the plant, with the door open to the river and the Lister running to power the blower, because the river wheel had to be stopped for the pour and a stopped wheel meant the town was running on the diesel and the batteries, and there was not enough diesel or enough battery to run the blower and the furnace and the lights of the town at the same time. The town knew. Naa Dedei had gone to every house herself and told them: *the wheel is stopped today, the light will be off after nine, fill your pots first.*

The furnace was an old oil drum lined with firebrick, and the crucible was a piece of ceramic pipe capped at one end with fireclay that Kwabena had made himself, years ago, in the bad year. Efua fed the fire with charcoal and with the last of the eucalyptus that had been cut the dry season before, and the bronze went in, and the white metal went in, in pieces of old bearing metal and old solder and old fishing weights and a pewter cup that Maame Asantewaa had donated to the work with her own hands and a blessing in the old language.

Kwabena heated the shell in the fire until it was too hot to spit on, and he tinned the bearing face with a tin of old solder melted on a wire, and then the metal was ready and he took the crucible out of the fire with the tongs and poured.

The pour is the thing that cannot be taught, only learned. The metal must be hot enough to run and cool enough not to burn, and the shell must be hot enough to take the bond and cool enough not to sweat the tin, and the stream must go in one place and one place only, so that the babbit goes where you send it and does not fold over on itself and trap an air pocket, because an air pocket in a poured bearing is a hole waiting for the shaft, and the hole will find the shaft and the shaft will find the hole and then you are where you were, only worse.

Kwabena poured, and Efua stood beside him with the second crucible in the fire, ready in case the pour failed and had to be redone before the shell cooled, which is the oldest reason there is for having an apprentice. The metal came out of the crucible in a golden stream and it went where he sent it and it filled the shell and rose, and he tapped the shell with the tongs, tap tap tap, and a bubble came up and broke and did not return, and when the pour was done he set the shell in the sand box to cool by the fire, and sat down on the floor of the workshop, and his hands were shaking, and Efua saw that his hands were shaking and said nothing, and gave him water.

They let it cool until evening. Then Kwabena took it out and looked at it and set it on the bench beside the other one and said, “In the morning we scrape. Fetch the bluing.”

---

They scraped for two days, and this is the work that made the old men old: the bearing face was poured high on purpose, so that it could be cut down to fit the shaft, and the only way to cut it down is with a scraper and your hands and the blue. You paint the shaft with bluing, you put the bearing on it, you turn it a quarter turn, you take it off, and where the blue has touched the white metal you scrape away the metal with a scraper until only the high spots remain blue, and then you do it again, and again, and again, until the blue is spread across the whole face in a thousand little marks like a map made of freckles, seventy percent contact or better, because a bearing that touches everywhere wears in and a bearing that touches only on its high spots wears out.

Kwabena scraped, and Efua scraped, and her hands blistered and healed and blistered again, and on the second night she fell asleep over the bench and woke with the scraper in her hand and the bluing on her cheek and her grandmother's voice in her ear saying *this is why women should be teachers, not engineers,* which was a joke between them, because Maame Asantewaa had been the one who had sent her to the plant in the first place.

On the afternoon of the second day, they put the new bearing in the housing and bolted it up and put the shaft in and turned it by hand, and it turned, and it turned sweetly, and there was no note, and Kwabena stood with his hand on the housing and his eyes closed and felt the smoothness of the turning through the iron, and did not smile, because he was not a man who smiled at the bench, but Efua looked at him and saw the thing that was not a smile and understood it.

“We will run it tomorrow at half load,” he said. “Slowly. And watch the temperature. And if the temperature climbs, we stop.”

That night, Ama came to the plant.

Kwabena was in the turbine hall, walking the length of it with the oil can in his hand though the wheel was still stopped and there was nothing to oil. He heard her come in, and he did not turn around, because he knew her step.

“She has begun,” Ama said. “Yaa. It will be tonight.”

He turned. She stood in the doorway with a lamp in her hand and a cloth over her arm, and her face was calm the way it always was, but her hands were not, and he saw her hands.

“What do you need.”

“Light,” she said. “All night. The lamp in the ward, and the lamp over the bed, and the cold box on, because if she bleeds I will need the plasma and the plasma has to be cold. And if the baby is transverse still, I will have to do the version, and after the version I will have to wait and listen, and if the cord comes first I will have to do the section, and if I do the section I will need everything you have, every watt, and I will need it for an hour, and then for another hour after.”

Kwabena looked at the stopped wheel. Then he looked at the switchboard, and at the oil cans on the wall, and at the new bearing in its housing with its bluing-freckled face, and he did the arithmetic that he had been doing all his life, and he said, “Then we will run the wheel for you. Half load at first, and as much as you need after that. I will nurse the bearing with my own hands.”

“It is not yet run in.”

“Papa.”

“I have prayed every night for thirty years to a machine,” said Kwabena. “And she has anointed my hands with oil and she has taken the sweat of my brow and she has let me walk in her house, and tonight I will ask her for one more night, and she will give it or she will not.” He put the oil can down. “Send the boy to open the sluice. I will be at the switchboard.”

---

The light came on at seven, as it always did, and the town saw it come on, and there was a small cheer in the market shed where people had gathered because they did not want to be alone in their houses, and the cheer died almost at once because everyone in the town knew what was happening in the clinic, and the cheer was for something else, for the ordinary thing, for the light.

The wheel turned. The gauge read forty kilowatts, then fifty, then sixty-one, because the mill's second motor was not running tonight, but the clinic's lamps were all burning, and the cold box was on, and the pump was filling the tank on the hill, and the ward lamp was bright, and Ama's hands moved under it.

Kwabena stood at the switchboard and watched the temperature gauge on the bearing. It climbed.
