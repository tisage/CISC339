Did AI Just Prove a 167-Year-Old Math Problem?

On November 20, 2026, an Anthropic mathematician working with Claude announced a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. I want to walk you through what actually happened and why I think it's worth five minutes of class time.

A problem that's resisted every mathematician since 1859 may have just been cracked — not by a human holding a pencil, but by a human and an AI together, writing a proof so long no person could ever read all of it themselves.

Here's what I know so far

A mathematician at Anthropic, Levent Alpöge, posted a note saying that working with Claude, he'd found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — technically the broader Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, which covers more ground than the original 1859 conjecture. I want to be upfront with you about the caveat I'd put on anything this new: it's not a short, elegant argument on paper. It's a formal proof, written in a language called Lean, about 17 million lines long, spread across more than 175,000 files. No single human has read the whole thing, and nobody ever will — that's not how this kind of proof gets checked.

The day after, the communities that maintain Lean's proof library checked the code and certified it compiles with no logical gaps. An MIT mathematician re-verified it independently with a different tool and said the odds of a hidden error are "approximately zero." What hasn't happened yet is a plain-English explanation of why the proof works — the logic checks out, but nobody has translated the idea into something a human can follow start to finish. I'll tell you honestly: that's the part that makes me want to wait before I call this "settled."

What the Riemann Hypothesis actually says

Primes look scattered and unpredictable the farther out you count them. In the 1800s, Riemann found a function — the zeta function — whose behavior secretly encodes exactly how primes are distributed. His conjecture is about one very specific property of that function: that all of its important "zero points" line up perfectly on a single line, instead of scattering randomly.

If that's true, the apparent randomness of primes isn't random at all — it's governed by a hidden, precise order. That's the entire hypothesis, in one sentence I give every class: chaos on the surface, structure underneath.

What I mean by "formal proof"

Lean is a proof assistant: every statement has to be built up from basic logical axioms, and a separate program — the "Lean kernel" — mechanically checks that every step follows validly from the last. There's no room for a hand-wavy "and then it's obvious that...". I tell my students this is the opposite failure mode from asking an LLM to just write out a proof in English: a plausible-sounding but wrong argument can slip past a human reader. It can't slip past Lean's kernel.

What I actually want you to take from this

Here's my honest take, as someone who's been teaching this material for a while. The headline isn't really about prime numbers — most commentators covering this agree the real story is that an AI system can now assist in research-level mathematical discovery that I'd have told you, a year ago, was decades away.

Three things I want stuck in your head from this lecture: First, formal verification is turning into a real engineering discipline, not just a theory-class topic — the same idea behind Lean already underlies safety-critical software, and I expect AI-assisted formal verification to show up in security engineering well before it's common in pure math. Second, this is what AI-assisted research looks like — a human mathematician steering and checking, an AI generating formal artifacts at a scale no person could produce alone. That pattern is coming to security research before it's finished maturing in mathematics. Third, and this is the one I'll actually put on an exam: the reason anyone believes this proof is that independent humans and independent tools re-checked it from first principles — not because an AI said so. That's the same standard I expect you to apply to any security claim in this course. Don't trust. Verify.
